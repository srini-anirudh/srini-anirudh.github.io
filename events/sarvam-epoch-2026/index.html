First, the design

Sarvam's design team rocked. It was simply India's best AI conference. Period. The effort that went into the design and the planning was amazing, and you could feel it from the first shot of the hall.

The main stage: a pixel-style “epoch” wordmark in lights, and a ceiling washed in colourThe hall from the back, with the ceiling lights turned blue

A few things I noticed. The stage carried a big wordmark in a pixel typeface, lit from inside. The ceiling was washed in moving colour, green and yellow in one shot and blue in the next. The screens were rounded panels, not hard rectangles.

The visuals had a point of view, and it was Indian. The cover art says it before anything else: a painted chhatri dome, cut down the middle by pixel blocks, with pixel-art flowers in navy and green around it. The opening screens used blue block-print patterns. The technical-session slide (below) used orange and red textile-style blocks next to a black carved pillar. Pixels for the technology, craft for the country.

The opening animation

Before the keynote began, the three screens played an animation. A stack of pixel blocks twisted into a ribbon of blue, red, orange and green, then spread into a rolling wave of coloured block rows. The wordmark stayed put on the white panels the whole time. It was simple, and it set the mood: everything on this stage was made of the same small square.

The song and dance

Then came the performance. A live song, with dancers in red and saffron in front of the screens. What made it work was what the screens did behind them.

  1. Circuits. Orange traces run together into one bright point, and from it blue filaments fan out like a neural network.
  2. Embroidery. The screen turns into cross-stitch flowers, wired together with circuit-board lines. A sunburst pulses in the middle of the pattern.
  3. Weave. A woven border of yellow triangles on red lines. Later, an arched doorway in carpet colours, and then diagonal bands of colour like a loom at full speed, with the truss pillars lit on either side.

Circuit board into embroidery, and neural network into weave. The message was clear without a single word: this technology is being built here, out of this culture, and it does not need to hide either one.

This is how I would want a sovereign AI company to be. Serious about the technology, proud of where it comes from, and careful enough about both to design every pixel. Hats off to the team.

Mission

The opening keynote: build at the frontier

Pratyush Kumar, Co-founder, Sarvam

Build at the frontier, create value for all, on the decade’s clock, not quarters.

4 min read 11 slides

The opening keynote was by Pratyush Kumar, one of Sarvam's co-founders. He started with the mission, and the mission was a nice one.

Pratyush Kumar opens the first keynote

The first big slide said it in one line: build at the frontier, to create value for all, on the decade's clock, not quarters. Sarvam's own summary of the session calls these three commitments. Build at the frontier. Create value at population scale. Keep a decade-long horizon.

Build at the frontier, on the decade’s clock, not quartersWhy train our models? Accumulated judgement is the moat

Then came the question every Indian AI company gets: why train your own models at all? The slide answered it in four words. Accumulated judgement is the moat. I read that as: every model you train teaches the team what to keep, what to drop and what to measure, and that judgement is hard to copy.

The next slide showed how Sarvam sees the stack. Everything we build is built from the vantage of model research. On the right sit the layers: user, application, data, harness, models and infra. The point, as I took it, is that these are not separate businesses. They all sit on the model research and feed back into it.

Everything is built from the vantage of model research: user, application, data, harness, models, infraLevers of scale: 100T tokens, trillion-plus parameters, 10k accelerators, RL

The levers-of-scale slide named four things to push on. Data, at 100T tokens of training. Models, with trillion-plus parameters. Compute, at 10k accelerators. And RL, scaled with inference-time compute. The slide did not say which of these are today and which are the target. Sarvam's page describes the model journey as running from Llama 3 to a 100B+ model, so I read this as the direction of travel.

One slide turned the research story into a service. Making infra and know-how available to enterprises to hill-climb on their specific tasks. The picture is the loop from the RL environments talk at the PyTorch meetup: an agent acts, an environment responds, reward pipelines score the result, and that data trains the agent again. Sarvam wants to run this loop for companies. There is more on that loop in Talk 3 of the meetup page.

Infra and know-how for enterprises to hill-climb: agent, environment, reward pipelinesTextiles, steel, semiconductors: from craft to industry

One slide I liked for the framing. Accelerating India's research ambition. Textiles went from hand loom to mill. Steel went from forge to plant. Semiconductors went from lab to fab. I read it as a promise to do the same for AI research, and move it from a craft to an industry.

The last part of the keynote was the “token factory”. The goal is on the slide: produce the largest share of the tokens India consumes. Sarvam wants to serve AI to the world from India, with its own models, frontier open models and inference infrastructure in India, at global price-performance and global reliability.

The goal: the largest share of the tokens India consumesServing AI to the world, from India

The slide that tied back to my last event said that building a token factory is hard but worthwhile. It listed five hard parts: workload-aware routing, prefill and decode separation, KV cache reuse, memory hierarchy and kernel-level optimisation. These are the same topics as the inference and profiling talks at the PyTorch meetup. The map next to it shows who the factory serves, which is general users, knowledge workers and builders. In front of it sits a constantly evolving harness of skills, memory, tools and sub-agents, and the models for text, vision and speech.

The numbers behind the mission

Pratyush said Sarvam has a cluster of 10,000 NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs in India, and will use it to build a trillion-parameter foundation model from scratch. That matches the levers-of-scale slide: 10k accelerators, trillion-plus parameters, 100T tokens. He also said Sarvam has already pre-trained a 100B+ model from scratch on 4,000 Hopper GPUs, and is now moving to scale RL.

What stayed with me. The mission was not “build a model”. It was to own the whole path from research to tokens served, and to be patient about it.

Fireside

Building a frontier model in India

In conversation with Devendra Singh Chaplot

You do not need impossible resources. A handful of experts, many motivated people, and 10,000 chips.

2 min read 1 slide

This was one of the sessions I enjoyed most. Pratyush Kumar sat down with Devendra Singh Chaplot for a conversation on what it takes to build at the frontier, from India.

Devendra Singh Chaplot’s introduction: Thinking Machines and Mistral on the slide

His introduction slide shows a PhD from CMU and a B.Tech from IIT Bombay. It lists the founding team at Thinking Machines, where he led Tinker for enterprises, and the founding team at Mistral, where he led multimodal research. A third line is hidden behind the speakers and ends in “lead”. On the technical-session slide he is listed under Sarvam.

What does it take, in numbers?

Devendra said that building at the frontier does not need impossible resources. Take a model with 3 trillion total parameters, with about 100B active in a mixture-of-experts design, trained on 100 trillion tokens. That is about 6 × 1025 FLOPs of pre-training. On the 10,000 Blackwell chips that had just been announced, with good utilisation, you could pre-train it in about two months.

I checked the arithmetic, because it is a good one to know. The rule of thumb for training compute is 6 × active parameters × tokens. Here that is 6 × 1011 × 1014, which is 6 × 1025. Spread over two months (about 5.2 million seconds) and 10,000 chips, it comes to about 1.2 PFLOP/s sustained per chip. NVIDIA's published dense peaks for a B200 are roughly 2.25 PFLOPS in BF16 and 4.5 PFLOPS in FP8. So the claim needs about half of BF16 peak, or about a quarter of FP8 peak. That is demanding, but believable. Note what the sum leaves out: getting 100 trillion good tokens, and the RL stage after pre-training.

What about the people?

Having been on the founding teams of two frontier labs, he said there is a misconception that you need a huge team of very experienced people. You only need a handful of experienced people to guide the architecture. What you need in numbers is highly motivated, talented people, and India has plenty of them. The technology is not that old, so with the right guidance you can catch up quickly.

Ten years out: the foothills of AGI?

Pratyush asked what the world looks like in ten years. Devendra flipped the question. Do you want a world with three models, or with thousands? Thousands of models, he argued, breed innovation and specialisation. India has its own languages, education systems, legal systems and public policy, so it needs thousands of models customised to that context. That is why they have to be built here.

Why this fits. “Thousands of models” is the other half of the Custom Model Training launch. If the future is many small specialised models, then training them has to become a service.

Models

Sarvam 105B, tuned for conversations and for work

A 105B model that beats bigger models on Indian conversations, at a fraction of the price.

2 min read 2 slides

Sarvam 105B first came out as a checkpoint in February. At Epoch, Sarvam released updated variants for the two jobs where it sees the most traction in India: conversational agents and work agents.

Conversations

Public benchmarks miss too much for conversation. They do not cover ambiguous messages, noisy speech recognition, code-mixed speech, long policy documents, or an instruction that must still be followed many turns later. So Sarvam evaluated on a production benchmark built from hundreds of millions of minutes of Indian conversations.

Voice-call capability82.174.778.9
Instruction following63.437.452.4

Linguistic quality, win rate61.36.032.7

These numbers come from Sarvam's summary post. The widest gaps are in instruction following and linguistic quality, the two things that decide whether a voice agent survives a real call. The post puts the price at $0.80 per million blended tokens, against $4.50 for GPT 5.4 Mini and $9.00 for Gemini 3.5 Flash.

Example 1: retrieval over policy documents, in code-mixed Hindi and English

This slide shows what “linguistic quality” means in practice. A bank agent asks, in Hinglish, whether the customer wants a new personal loan. The customer replies in the same mix: interested, but I heard something about policy changes, what is that? Sarvam 105B retrieves both policy terms and answers in the same mixed language. The process is simpler, interest rates now start at 11 percent, and there are no foreclosure charges after six months. The slide lists how other models fail. One quotes the rate but drops the foreclosure waiver. One replies entirely in English to a customer who is code-switching. One reads the policy clause out word for word on the call.

Work agents

For work agents, Sarvam tuned a reasoning variant and tested it on HarnessBench. It has 106 sandboxed tasks across software engineering, data and finance, knowledge retrieval, office work, tool use, SRE and long-running autonomy.

Sarvam 105B against GLM 5.2 (753B) on the harness bench, and the price

The chart compares Sarvam 105B (black bars) with GLM 5.2, a 753B model (green bars). GLM 5.2 is ahead overall, 79.6 against 67.7. It also leads on completion, tool use, consistency, robustness and process. Both score 100 on security. The right half of the slide is about price. Sarvam 105B is 5.5x cheaper, at $0.80 against $4.40 per million output tokens.

The useful idea is routing. In Sarvam's own agent set-up, 36% of tasks go to Sarvam 105B and 64% go to GLM 5.2. That cuts serving cost by roughly 40%. A model does not have to win every task. It has to win enough of the cheap ones.

Both variants are available through the Sarvam API. One is tuned for low-latency conversations. The other is for complex tasks inside an agent harness.

Sarvam Vision 1.0: multilingual visual reasoning, handwriting, Indic documents, structured outputs

The live examples

Key-value extraction from a complex table into structured JSONKey-value extraction from a rental agreement into typed fields

The first demo took a dense expense table, with regions, budget and actual columns and subtotals, and turned it into clean JSON. Each region got its own budget and actual value, plus the variance. The second took a rental agreement on stamp paper and filled typed fields: the agreement location, the date, and the owner's and renter's details.

Handwriting was the part I enjoyed most. A Tamil handwritten letter was read in full and split into a header, a body and a footer. A handwritten form was turned into structured fields. Handwriting in Indian scripts is where general OCR models usually fall apart.

Vision Edge

Sarvam Vision Edge: a custom model for Odia land records, 50% more accurateFifteen land documents processed, with deed type, district and dates extracted

Vision Edge brings these capabilities into a customer's own environment, with models customised for one workflow. The example was a land-record digitisation project for the Government of Odisha. A custom model reached 50% higher accuracy than the best general-purpose model tested on the same task.

The demo slide shows a batch of fifteen scanned documents on the left and the extracted fields on the right. Each document gets a type, such as deed of mortgage, general power of attorney or will. The fields include the district (Balasore, Cuttack, Khurda), the village and the presentation date. These are old, stamped, photocopied pages. That is the real input, not a clean PDF.

The lesson. A general model is a good start. A model tuned on one workflow's ugly data beat it by half again.

Speech

Saaras V4 and Bulbul V4

The best English ASR is now an Indian one, and text-to-speech you can direct inside the text.

2 min read 3 slides

The speech section had one slide I will remember: the best English ASR is now an Indian one.

The best English ASR is now an Indian one

Saaras V4: speech to text

Saaras V4 supports 22 Indian languages. Sarvam says it records the lowest average word error rate of any system tested across seven standard English suites. These include LibriSpeech, GigaSpeech and Svarah, which is built from Indian-accented speech. That is Sarvam's own claim from its summary post.

Five modes, one model: the same Hindi-English sentence in five output styles

The slide that explains the product best is “five modes, one model”. It shows one sentence about T20 batting and Virat Kohli, spoken in Hindi mixed with English, in five forms:

  • Verbatim. Every word as spoken. Nothing is cleaned up.
  • Transcribe. Numerals are normalised and punctuation is restored. “Twenty twenty” becomes T20.
  • Code-mixed. Native script, with the English words kept in English.
  • Transliteration. The whole utterance in the Roman script.
  • Translation. A faithful English translation.

Saaras V4 also does multi-speaker ASR in a single pass. It does diarisation and transcription together, instead of running two systems one after the other. That matters for call recordings, meetings and interviews, where who said it matters as much as what was said. The speaker explained that this is one speaker-biased encoder doing both jobs, and showed that it copes with heavy overlap, back-channelling and noisy audio, such as Indian news-channel debates.

Bulbul V4: text to speech

Directability: the same line as baseline, guided and directed speech

Bulbul V4 is a research preview, and the feature that stood out is directability. The slide uses one line: “I cannot believe we actually won the lottery! This is life-changing… but I just wish my dad were here to see it.” It shows three levels of control.

  • Baseline. Plain text. The model decides how to say it.
  • Guided. You add a few tags: an overall mood, an emphasis on one word, and a laugh.
  • Directed. You mark up the whole line. It starts happy, stresses “believe”, laughs after “lottery”, turns sad for the last clause, and sighs before it.

So emotion, emphasis, pacing and cues such as laughter are controlled inside the text, while the audio is generated. The model has Navarasa, nine emotions, and you can steer it mid-sentence, for example from excited to sad on one particular word. It matters most for enterprise voice, where names, amounts and tone have to be delivered exactly. Sarvam's Content Studio also covers voice cloning, dubbing and live translation, and the session list includes a talk on voice cloning.

Training

Custom model training, and RL at scale

Bring your data. Sarvam runs the training in India and hands you the weights.

2 min read 3 slides

Not every announcement was a model. One was Custom Model Training: Sarvam trains a model on your data, on infrastructure in India, and hands you the weights.

Sarvam's summary lists the terms. The training data stays isolated and is never used to train Sarvam's own models. When training is done, the weights are delivered to the organisation and owned by it. The goal is smaller, task-specific models that cut latency and inference cost, instead of sending every request to one big general model.

Three problems with doing it yourself: access to GPUs, standing up pipelines, efficient use

The slide on why this is a service lists three problems, and they are the ones I recognise from cluster work.

  1. Access to GPUs. Capacity is committed in bulk, and you pay from day one whether you are training or idle.
  2. Standing up the pipelines. A healthy cluster is weeks of work: interconnect, storage, matching CUDA stacks and orchestration.
  3. Efficient utilisation. “It runs” is not “it runs well”. There are version conflicts, runs that “succeed” at zero steps, and throughput far below what the hardware supports.
Optimised RL training infra: async RL and LoRATotal mean reward, train against validation, over about 200 steps

The next slide went into RL training, which is where the systems work shows. In a simple loop, generation and training take turns, and each side idles while the other works. A team ends up with a fraction of its hardware's throughput. There is a second cost. Every step ships updated weights to the generators, and at 100B+ parameters that is hundreds of GB across nodes.

The slide gives two fixes. Async RL overlaps generation and training instead of alternating them, so nothing sits idle. LoRA trains fewer parameters, so the weight sync is much smaller. This is the same pipeline thinking as the RL environments talk at the PyTorch meetup, applied to a managed service.

The plot is a real run: total mean reward for the training set (smoothed, red) and the validation set (black). Reading the axes, the reward climbs from about 0.75 to above 0.9 in roughly 200 steps. Validation sits above training and flattens near the end. A flat validation curve is what you want to see before you stop.

Inference

Sarvam Inference: open models served from India

Faster and cheaper on the same open models, and an agent that found a 15x speed-up in one night.

3 min read 6 slides

Sarvam Inference is the serving layer. It runs Sarvam's own models and frontier open-weight models from data centres in India. Developers get the best open models. Enterprises get data residency and compliance with Indian rules by default.

The models on Sarvam Inference: Saaras V4, Bulbul V3, Vision, Translate, Dub, GLM 5.2, Gemma 4 31B, Sarvam 105B

The line-up slide shows eight models, each with its job. Saaras V4 for speech to text. Bulbul V3 for text to speech. Vision for document AI. Sarvam Translate for translation. Sarvam Dub for dubbing. GLM 5.2 for coding and tool calling. Gemma 4 31B for multi-modal chat. And Sarvam 105B for Indic conversation.

Gemma 4 31B: speed against price, 0.63 s time to first token, $0.40 in and $1.00 out

This chart is the clearest picture of the pitch. The vertical axis is output tokens per second for one stream, so faster is higher. The horizontal axis is the blended price per million tokens, using a 7:2:1 mix of input, cached and output tokens, so cheaper is further left. The green box is “faster and cheaper”. The slide says only Sarvam operates there. Providers such as Lightning AI, SambaNova, Together AI, DeepInfra and Google AI Studio sit outside it. On the right of the slide: 0.63 s time to first token and $0.40 input and $1.00 output per million tokens.

The summary post says the same story holds for other open models, using the P50 of real traffic on OpenRouter. It says GLM 5.2 is first on both speed and time to first token. It also claims Sarvam can take in a new open model and tune its serving within three days of launch. All of this runs on the first fleet of Blackwell GPUs in India, and the fleet is growing.

How the speed is made

The summary names the work: kernels, disaggregated prefill and decode, KV cache across workload types, and trained speculator models. Then the stage showed something I did not expect, which was the inference team using an agent to do the optimising.

Agentic model optimisation: three layers of work and an agent with four partsUp to 15x faster inference in one night on one H100

The first slide splits the work into three layers. Bespoke kernels: Triton, CUDA, fusion and custom MMA. Framework and scheduler surgery: topology-aware placement, chunked prefill and async decode. Systems tuning: dynamic batching and CUDA graphs. Under that sits the agent, with four parts. Profiling as an MCP tool. Persistent memory. A self-improvement loop. And Sarvam's own expertise, written down as skills.

The chart is the result. Over one night on one H100, from about 23:00 to 08:00, the agent searched for faster versions of a model's inference. Each branch in the picture is an attempt. The main line keeps the winners: a baseline, then vectorised kernels, then GPU-resident data, then CUDA streams with overlap. The end of the line is up to 15x faster. It says “up to”, so I would not read it as a promise for every model. But it is a concrete case of what the profiling talk at the meetup argued: if you can measure it, you can optimise it, and now an agent can do the loop.

Platform

Indus, Work Agents and Sarvam Code

One workspace with many agents. The metric that keeps coming back is cost per finished task.

4 min read 5 slides

Indus is Sarvam's platform for AI work. One workspace holds several kinds of agent: voice agents for conversations, work agents for employees, document agents for paperwork, a content studio for media, and coding agents for engineering.

Voice agents

Pricing per minute: market ₹12, a human agent ₹7.5, Sarvam ₹3.50

Sarvam's voice agents have spoken more than 325 million minutes. They are now generally available, with no waitlist, at ₹3.50 per minute. That includes authoring, simulation, phone numbers, evaluation and analytics. The slide compares it with the market price of ₹12 and the cost of a human agent at ₹7.5.

On stage the claims were even tighter than the post. A developer can deploy a voice agent in under an hour and get a local phone number in about 30 seconds. The voice platform is Samvaad. And 325 million minutes works out to about 618 years of conversation.

The workflow in the post is the part I found useful. A business team describes the goal of an agent in plain language. It simulates a thousand calls against it. It goes live on a phone number in under two hours. The whole stack can sit inside the company's own VPC when compliance demands it.

Work agents

Work agents are a fleet of AI coworkers. Each one drafts, researches, analyses and runs processes, and runs code in a sandbox to produce finished artifacts such as a report, a dashboard or an app. They are live now. They run in a custom, secure sandbox and connect to tools such as Slack and GitHub, for long multi-step tasks.

The first chart is a harness benchmark. Using the same tasks, Hermes scores 76.06% and Sarvam Work scores 79.76%. The second is about retrieval. It compares an agent using a legacy knowledge base, the same agent using Sarvam's knowledge base, and Sarvam Work using Sarvam's knowledge base:

Sarvam Code

Serious engineering, done with taste, at a fraction of the costTerminal Bench 2.1: dollars per solved task

Sarvam Code is in early beta. Sarvam says it is entering a crowded category with one view: code is being generated faster, but that speed is not turning into product outcomes. Duplicate code builds up, review becomes the bottleneck, and long tasks on frontier models get expensive. So Sarvam Code optimises cost per completed task. It routes work across 100B, 700B and 3T-scale models, keeps going on a hard task until it is done, and avoids re-reading the same codebase. The harness is a trio of agents, a planner, a worker and a verifier, so it can correct itself on a long task instead of failing half-way.

The hero slide shows the idea in a terminal. A plan for fixing a flaky retry has four verified steps: reproduce the flake, patch the backoff and jitter, add a regression test, and check that 214 tests pass. The bill at the end is $0.14.

The Terminal Bench chart ranks by dollars per solved task. Sarvam Code with GLM-5.2 solved 72 of 89 tasks at $2.0 each. The other rows cost more, from $3.4 up to $7.4 per solved task. Two of them solved a few more tasks than Sarvam Code did, 74 and 75 of 89. So the claim here is not the highest score. It is the lowest price for a solved task, and I think that is the right metric for coding agents.

The technical session

The technical session (15:20 on the agenda) was very nice. It was mostly about continual learning. Sarvam's programme describes it as a panel on continual learning, evals and enterprise improvement loops.

The technical session panel, on the stage under the Epoch block pattern

The slide names four of the five people on stage: Vivek Raghavan, co-founder of Sarvam; Manish Gupta, Senior Director at Google DeepMind; Devendra Singh Chaplot, Sarvam; and Aaditya Sood, Surge Venture Partner at Peak XV.

The conversation moved between three questions. How should a model and the layer around it divide the work? How do you keep improving a model after it ships? And why is that hard inside a company?

Model and harness: separate mechanism from policy

The panel agreed that the orchestration layer, the “harness”, will stay essential even as models get more capable.

Aaditya Sood put it as separating mechanism from policy. The model handles the non-deterministic intelligence. The harness holds the business-specific policy: risk appetite, approvals and authority. Devendra Chaplot added that a harness is where customisation happens. One model cannot fit what every user and application needs.

Scale does not remove the harness

A bigger model, 100B parameters and up, needs less hand-holding from the harness on basic tasks. Vivek Raghavan said the harness matters more for decomposing problems. A hard question, such as exploring a scientific hypothesis, can be split into sub-problems. Several agents then work on them in parallel, either competing or collaborating.

“Evals are everything”

Raghavan was blunt about this one: if you cannot evaluate a capability, you cannot build it. The panel then talked about how evals get harder as tasks get less verifiable and more subjective. The example was judging how well a model speaks the Haryanvi dialect. Cultural and linguistic accuracy still needs human judgement. It cannot be fully automated.

Real traces or synthetic environments?

For continual improvement, Chaplot said each source does a different job.

  • Real production traces show what users actually intend, and where the model falls short today.
  • Synthetic environments are what you need to push past the current limit and teach the model something entirely new.

Manish Gupta warned against leaning too hard on real-world “exhaust”. A user’s intervention is not always correct. His example: code that compiles can still fail to solve the business problem behind it. A trace tells you what happened, not whether it was right.

Continual learning inside an enterprise

Gupta said enterprises guard their proprietary data closely. Many hesitate to share it for continuous model training, because they fear leakage.

Sood described enterprise architecture as a layer cake. In it, the harness is the tightly coupled clutch to the model’s engine. Business rules stay fixed in the harness, while the model underneath keeps changing quickly.

The thread through all five points. Let the model keep learning. Keep the rules, the approvals and the evals outside it, where a company can own them.

What I am taking away

tok 01Own the whole pathModels, harness, data and infra were shown as one stack, built from model research. The mission was patient: a decade, not a quarter.
tok 02Price and speed are featuresTime to first token, tokens per second and rupees per minute were on the main slides, next to accuracy.
tok 03Test on your own trafficPublic benchmarks miss code-mixing, noisy audio and long policies. Sarvam built its own from real conversations.
tok 04Route, do not pick one model36% of tasks to a small model and 64% to a big one cut the cost by about 40%.
tok 05Count cost per finished taskTokens are the wrong unit for agents. Dollars per solved task, and rupees per call, are what a buyer feels.
tok 06Agents optimise systems tooAn agent with a profiler found a 15x speed-up in a night. The loop is: measure, try, keep what wins.
tok 07The frontier is a sum you can do3T parameters, 100B active, 100T tokens is about 6×1025 FLOPs. It is a compute-and-people problem, not magic.
tok 08Thousands of models, not threeIndia has its own languages, legal systems and public policy. Specialised models, trained as a service, fit that.
tok 09The hard parts repeatPrefill and decode, KV cache reuse, kernels and async RL were on Sarvam's slides, just as at the PyTorch meetup.
tok 10Policy lives outside the modelMechanism in the model, policy in the harness. Approvals, authority and risk rules stay fixed while the model changes.
tok 11Evals are everythingIf you cannot evaluate it, you cannot build it. Subjective tasks, such as a dialect, still need human judges.
tok 12Traces teach, environments stretchProduction traces show real intent and gaps. Synthetic environments teach new skills. Neither proves a result was right.

The theme, as at the last event: India is not only using models. It is building the factory under them.
