Mission

The opening keynote: build at the frontier

Pratyush Kumar, Co-founder, Sarvam

Build at the frontier, create value for all, on the decade’s clock, not quarters.

10 min read 11 slides

The opening keynote was by Pratyush Kumar, one of Sarvam's co-founders. He began with the name. An epoch, he said, is the moment in training when a model has seen the data once, and learning takes hold. This was the first ever Epoch, the first of a series where Sarvam’s ideas, products and partnerships come out into the open. Day one was for builders. The next day was for enterprise, government and policy leaders. He promised research, new models and a few products nobody expected, with booths and buildathons in the afternoon and the whole team in the room to talk. Then he got to the mission, and the mission was a nice one.

Pratyush Kumar opens the first keynote

He called AI both financially lucrative and the pinnacle of human engineering and insight. That pinnacle moves faster than in earlier technology waves, which is why being at the very edge matters so much. For Sarvam, he said, it means building the frontier, defining it, sometimes keeping pace with it and sometimes pushing it further.

The first big slide said it in one line: build at the frontier, to create value for all, on the decade's clock, not quarters. Sarvam's own summary of the session calls these three commitments. Build at the frontier. Create value at population scale. Keep a decade-long horizon.

Build at the frontier, on the decade’s clock, not quartersWhy train our models? Accumulated judgement is the moat

The second commitment, creating value, he separated from “token maxing”. He pointed to the studies asking where AI’s return on investment is. India has seen digital public infrastructure work at population scale, and he wants the same for AI. Value means solving real problems with real outcomes for enterprises, governments and citizens, and the word all is in the name Sarvam. On the third, he said we are in year four of the post-ChatGPT world, “the second over of a Test match”, so the horizon has to be decades, not quarters. The field is noisy, with a new thread or point of view every day, so the engineering has to stay at the frontier while the mission runs much longer. We are never going back to a world before LLMs, he said, and India’s opportunity in AI is now.

Then came the question every Indian AI company gets: why train your own models at all? Some voices in the country say a strong open model, like Kimi K3 from Moonshot, is all we need. Pratyush called that absolutely the wrong conclusion. Use the best models out there, closed and open, but also make your own bets. The slide gave the reason in four words. Accumulated judgement is the moat. Many call it taste. A team that trains models learns what good data is, how to work with GPUs at scale, and how to make a model useful in a product.

Non-trivial. The moat he named is not the application and not the user base. It is the judgement a team builds by training models over several years. The proof was Sarvam’s own path: contributing to Llama 3 and learning to add language skills; pre-training a tiny 2B model on the first thousand Hopper GPUs in India with Infosys; building Sarvam M, a thinking model on top of Mistral’s small model, with RL, which he called the first thinking Mistral model; then pre-training a 100B+ model from scratch on 4,000 Hopper GPUs. Each step took on one more capability, so the judgement was built in layers.

There was a second argument, about access. Today there are excellent open models, and Sarvam uses them all the time. But a country cannot make its progress depend on fair access to a technology that many see as a huge differentiator. He joked that each quarter has its theme: token maxing, then complaints about expensive closed models, and maybe next a quarter of closed Chinese models.

Non-trivial. His case for training at home is not that open models are bad. It is that the grievance changes every quarter, so the only stable hedge is the ability to build your own.

The next slide showed how Sarvam sees the stack. Everything we build is built from the vantage of model research. The layers are infra, models, harness, data, and users and applications. Many companies build at one or two of these. A model builder sees all of them differently. Infra looks different after training on thousands of GPUs than it does to an inference provider. Data is something you understand deeply. He added that users will move toward custom models for specific applications, and a model builder can customise models for them.

Non-trivial. A harness looks different when you have trained the model. He said Sarvam understands where models need load-bearing components in the harness. My reading is that a company that only wraps someone else’s model has to guess.

Everything is built from the vantage of model research: user, application, data, harness, models, infraLevers of scale: 100T tokens, trillion-plus parameters, 10k accelerators, RL

Before the levers, he previewed what would be released. On text, Sarvam would release its best conversational models and very effective agentic models, post-trained on its own sovereign models, with what he called the most competitive conversational AI in India on price and benchmarks. On vision, the document models would get new benchmark results and global competitiveness across languages. On speech recognition, the models would be pushed further against international competition. On speech synthesis, Sarvam would release very human-like, expressive audio, which he said is better heard than described. Everything would be available to build on right away.

The levers-of-scale slide named four things to push on, and Pratyush gave the numbers for each.

  • Data. From tens of trillions of tokens to almost hundreds of trillions. The numbers sound abstract, he said, but the scale is not: it takes an enormous amount of content and engineering effort.
  • Models. An investment in trillion-plus parameter models, built from scratch in India, for coding, cybersecurity, simulation and science.
  • Compute. Access today to the largest Blackwell cluster in India, run by Sarvam from bare metal, and scaling up to about 10,000 accelerators. Running compute reliably in India, he argued, is a condition for the first two levers.
  • RL. The algorithm that keeps paying off as models train for longer, including continual learning on specific tasks.

He did not give the size of today’s cluster on stage. The 10,000 figure came back in the fireside that followed.

One slide turned the research story into a service. Making infra and know-how available to enterprises to hill-climb on their specific tasks. The picture is the loop from the RL environments talk at the PyTorch meetup: an agent acts, an environment responds, reward pipelines score the result, and that data trains the agent again. Sarvam wants to run this loop for companies. There is more on that loop in Talk 3 of the meetup page.

Infra and know-how for enterprises to hill-climb: agent, environment, reward pipelinesTextiles, steel, semiconductors, and now tokens

One slide I liked for the framing. Accelerating India's research ambition. Technology waves, Pratyush said, are won by building the systems that manufacture what people need: textiles, steel, and in the late 1980s semiconductors. In this era the core component is the token. Today most of the tokens Indians use are produced outside the country, some by international vendors and some by Indian ones.

Non-trivial. The framing is manufacturing, not software. Sovereignty over how you manufacture intelligence is the point, and the measurable goal is the largest share of the tokens India consumes, produced in India.

Where the research is going

He also listed five research problems Sarvam is committing to.

  1. Dense models for the edge. Memory is getting expensive, and mixture-of-experts models are great in the cloud but hard to fit on a local machine.
  2. Live interactive systems. Low-latency, high-fidelity, multimodal experiences, built on what running the country’s largest voice platform has taught the team. It is not only a harness problem. It is the model and the systems engineering too.
  3. Cyber systems. Few companies in the room have not worried about cybersecurity in the last month, he said, and Sarvam is no exception. The team built tools because of it, with results shown later in the day.
  4. Systems for real operations. Work with the strategic sector, including digital twins that simulate large systems and then operate them.
  5. Foundation models for customer data. India has enormous digital public goods and finance data. He said the market keeps asking for models built on this data, and that it needs real innovation.

The last part of the keynote was the “token factory”. The goal is on the slide: produce the largest share of the tokens India consumes. Sarvam wants to serve AI to the world from India, with its own models, frontier open models and inference infrastructure in India, at global price-performance and global reliability. He announced an inference platform running in India, serving Sarvam’s models and a range of frontier open models. Indian developers and enterprises, he said, should not have to pay a tax to use models here, and he promised data security of the highest order.

The goal: the largest share of the tokens India consumesServing AI to the world, from India

The slide that tied back to my last event said that building a token factory is hard but worthwhile. In his words, the hard parts are taking thousands of GPUs and many workloads and routing them, splitting parts of the compute apart, handling the KV cache extremely well, and managing the memory hierarchy, from the on-chip memory on the GPU out to the disk and shared file system. Then there is kernel-level optimisation of the model on the GPU. Models, GPUs and methods all keep changing, so the frontier applies here as much as it does to models. These are the same topics as the inference and profiling talks at the PyTorch meetup. The map next to it shows who the factory serves, which is general users, knowledge workers and builders. In front of it sit the models for text, vision and speech, and on top of them a constantly evolving harness.

His framing of the harness stuck with me. Think of the model as the CPU and the harness as the operating system. A year from now, using a model directly may feel as odd as writing assembly. Over the last three months Sarvam has built harnesses that optimise skills, memory, tools, sub-agents and RLMs (recursive language models, as I read it), and hill-climb on the prompts. He said Sarvam has harness results that are globally competitive, across work agents, coding agents and cybersecurity agents.

Non-trivial. His words: “You might have the best token factory, you might have the best models. You need to ensure that for that combination of models, you have the right harness that gets the right performance.” The three pieces have to be tuned together.

Non-trivial. “No tax” is the product promise for inference. Being in India should carry no premium on price, security or reliability.

Weaving

He ended on the theme of the event. China did not move fast on AI because a couple of labs built it, he said. It was a whole-industry effort of universities, startups and organisations exchanging ideas. He quoted the Chinese premier: no single string can weave a cloth by itself. Then he added that Epoch’s theme is also weaving. It is the same idea as the screens in the design section: many small threads, one cloth. He asked everyone to build the frontier together.

What stayed with me. The mission was not “build a model”. It was to own the whole path from research to tokens served, and to be patient about it.

Moonshots

Aiming for moonshots: from strategic to cognitive independence

Dr. S. Somanath, former Chairman, ISRO

ISRO built the applications first and the launcher later. And a physics test that two AI tools failed.

7 min read

Dr. S. Somanath, the former chairman of ISRO, spoke on sovereign AI as the next moonshot. He said up front that he comes from a different domain, space technology, and that India has done something fantastic in space, so it should now do the same for AI. The talk had a clear structure: an analogy from space, a case for language and education, and then a long, technical section on where AI meets physics.

From strategic independence to cognitive independence

Space gave India strategic independence: the ability to design, develop, build and launch from its own soil. When people abroad ask how India reached the Moon so cheaply, he said, cost is not the real point. The point is that everything was Indian: the rocket, navigation, propulsion, software, mission planning and manufacturing. Nothing came from outside. The new race is for intelligence, and the sovereign AI stack will decide cognitive independence. His lines: access is not ownership, and adoption is not the real advantage. If someone else owns the intelligence layer, you do not own it.

The space-to-AI map

He has built rockets and the systems around them, and he mapped the parts one for one.

SpaceAI stack
Launch vehicleFoundation model
Launch pad (Sriharikota)GPU and compute architecture and infrastructure
SpacecraftAI applications built on Sarvam
Navigation technologyData and data governance
Payload (communication, imaging)Domain knowledge in the AI stack
Mission controlAI orchestration

Without your own launch vehicle you lack the freedom to choose what to build and launch, and a mission like Chandrayaan-3 would never get to orbit if you depended on another nation's rocket. Space is also unforgiving. If one layer fails, one signal, one correction, one piece of software, the result is a rocket that explodes. The same holds for the AI stack: foundation models, compute, applications, an indigenous content and data pipeline, and domain development. Every layer has to succeed.

The ISRO case study: applications first

When ISRO built the PSLV, he said, it never looked at what NASA was doing, because there was nothing to replicate. And ISRO did not build the rocket first. It built the applications first: satellite communication and village resource mapping, and the capacity to analyse data and generate intelligence. Then it built sensors and launched them on Russian rockets. Only after that capacity existed did it build its own launcher. He called this an interesting case study for AI.

Non-trivial. India's space programme did not start with the rocket. It started with the applications and the ability to turn data into intelligence, bought launches, and built the launcher once the demand and the skills existed. The AI analogy is that the order of building matters: capacity to use intelligence at scale came before capacity to produce it.

He added a point aimed at the room: “We don't want more engineers to become prompt engineers. We want them to become the foundation model scientists.”

Language, education and health

India speaks many languages, with dialects numbering 1,500 and more. Language has historically divided the country, though he said it is not a serious problem now. He noted that Sarvam handles more than 22 Indian languages, speech recognition, multilingual speech synthesis, code-mixed speech and voice preservation across languages. He described how he calculates in his own mother tongue in his head and then translates to another language, and said AI can reduce that pain.

  • Education. Good teachers are scarce. AI should not replace teachers. It should take over the routine, such as attendance, question setting, evaluation and report cards, so teachers become enablers and motivators. NEP 2020 gives fertile ground for personalised, competency-oriented, lifelong learning at national scale, since students learn at different paces. His moonshot: every child, regardless of geography, language, income or background, should have access to the world's best teacher, laboratory and library through AI.
  • Healthcare. In his village the doctor will look at you, not touch you, and write a prescription. AI can do more by listening and by drawing on wearables, previous records and disease-spread data. He listed imaging for cancer, tuberculosis and stroke, precision medicine from genetics, environment and lifestyle, drug discovery with less sample evaluation, and public health in rural India. His one-line summary: a doctor with AI will outperform a doctor without it.

Physics-informed AI: the technical heart of the talk

His argument was that the real world is not driven only by data. It is constrained by Newton's laws and by relativity. Embedding physics, equations and energy balances into data-driven models could do wonders, and he hopes AI with physics-informed systems will one day design a real rocket, though today it cannot. In his words, AI should be about understanding reality, not only predicting it.

He gave a test. He took a small physics problem, a sketch of a funnel emptying water, and the reverse case, and asked which empties faster. One AI tool answered wrongly. A second tool gave the same wrong answer. When he gave the correct answer, the tool agreed with him.

Non-trivial. The funnel test shows the failure mode of purely data-driven systems. They are fluent, they agree with you when corrected, and in high-end computation an error that nobody can detect is not tolerable. That is the case for putting physics in the loop.

Where he sees the pressure points, in order of the talk:

  • Simulation cost. Aerodynamics and computational fluid dynamics simulations run for hours on large computing networks. Discretising the governing equations, the Navier–Stokes equations, gives a very large matrix. For each rocket flight ISRO runs thousands of simulations, if not millions, and engineers go through the data personally. AI surrogate models could speed up decisions on small design changes. He named physics-informed neural networks.
  • Propulsion. Combustion instabilities, thermal runaway, transients and the energy release of chemicals in flow fields are all computationally hard.
  • Digital twins. He wants real digital twins that run from the model through operating systems and sensors to reality, with test feedback going back into one model. For the LVM-3 he has never had a full digital twin, only bits and pieces, so before each launch he runs mission, propulsion, guidance, control and wind simulations separately. He described a new engineering loop: design, build, test, relearn, redesign, rebuild.
  • Guidance, navigation and control. ISRO has started using AI with sensors and navigation algorithms for path planning and collision avoidance.
  • Space situational awareness. With a very large number of satellites in orbit, operators have to nudge a satellite when a collision is possible, and must predict at least a day ahead. This is done today with closed-form numerical techniques, which he called not the efficient way.
  • Manufacturing, materials and structures. Materials, design and quality checks are all candidates, as are biotechnology and chemistry.

The close

He ended with history. People said India could not build launch services, so it launched satellites on Russian and other rockets. Then it built the PSLV. He was on that team, and he still remembers walking in, in 1985, and ordering a drawing board for an A4 sketch. He saw it grow from that sketch to reality. His advice: never let the thought that something is not possible for us take hold. India's moonshot was never about reaching the Moon, but about proving that the limits imposed by the constraints of the mind can be overcome. AI is the next moonshot, and the goal is not another software industry but a sovereign stack. The measure of AI in education, he said, will be not the machine's intelligence but the confidence, creativity and capacity of young minds. He also pointed to the startup Skyroot and its Vikram-1 launch as a sign that space has become affordable.

Fireside

Building a frontier model in India

In conversation with Devendra Singh Chaplot

You do not need impossible resources. A handful of experts, many motivated people, and 10,000 chips.

4 min read 1 slide

This closed the models part of the keynote. Pratyush Kumar invited Devendra Singh Chaplot on stage and made two announcements. Sarvam is opening a San Francisco office, to hire people who can contribute to the mission. And Devendra is joining as an advisor. He had flown in from San Francisco the day before. Pratyush said Sarvam wants globally competitive, best-in-class models, and wants the people who build for companies across the world to also build for India. Then they talked about what it takes to build at the frontier, from India.

Devendra Singh Chaplot’s introduction: Thinking Machines and Mistral on the slide

His introduction slide shows a PhD from CMU and a B.Tech from IIT Bombay. It lists the founding team at Thinking Machines, where he led Tinker for enterprises, and the founding team at Mistral. A third line is hidden behind the speakers and ends in “lead”. In his own words, he was most recently the pre-training lead at xAI. Before that he was on the founding teams at Mistral, where he worked on Mistral 7B, 8x7B and Large, and at Thinking Machines Lab. On the technical-session slide he is listed under Sarvam.

What does it take, in numbers?

Devendra said that many people think building at the frontier takes huge resources, and some think it is impossible in India. That is not true, he said. Take a model about the size of Kimi K3, with 3 trillion total parameters, with about 100B active in a mixture-of-experts design, and even if you train it for as many as 100 trillion tokens. That is about 6 × 1025 FLOPs of pre-training. On the 10,000 Blackwell chips that had just been announced, with good utilisation, you could pre-train it in about two months.

I checked the arithmetic, because it is a good one to know. The rule of thumb for training compute is 6 × active parameters × tokens. Here that is 6 × 1011 × 1014, which is 6 × 1025. Spread over two months (about 5.2 million seconds) and 10,000 chips, it comes to about 1.2 PFLOP/s sustained per chip. NVIDIA's published dense peaks for a B200 are roughly 2.25 PFLOPS in BF16 and 4.5 PFLOPS in FP8. So the claim needs about half of BF16 peak, or about a quarter of FP8 peak. That is demanding, but believable. Note what the sum leaves out: getting 100 trillion good tokens, and the RL stage after pre-training. His conclusion was that compute is not the barrier. Pratyush’s own summary at the end was “10,000 Blackwells for six months”, a few experienced people and lots of motivated ones. Two months is the pre-training-only figure at good utilisation. My reading is that the gap leaves room for the work the estimate leaves out.

What about the people?

Having been on the founding teams of two frontier labs, he said there is a misconception that you need a huge team of very experienced people. You need a handful of experienced people, and mostly you need a lot of motivated and talented ones, and India has plenty of them. Even people with experience, himself included, have only a few years on this work, so experience is not the bottleneck. With the right guidance from a few experienced people, a lot of people can catch up quickly. Pratyush added that mission-oriented teams go at the problem with a lot of energy.

Ten years out: the foothills of AGI?

Pratyush asked whether we are in the foothills of AGI, climbing, or already past that, and then what the world looks like in ten years. Devendra flipped the question and asked what the audience wants that world to be. Do you want a world with only three models, or with thousands? Most people, he said, would choose thousands, and it is good for the world.

My link, not his. “Thousands of models” fits the Custom Model Training launch. If the future is many small specialised models, then training them has to become something you can do with an SDK.

Models

Sarvam 105B, tuned for conversations and for work

A 105B model that beats bigger models on Indian conversations, at a fraction of the price.

5 min read 2 slides

Sarvam 105B first came out as a checkpoint in February. At Epoch, Sarvam released updated variants for the two jobs where it sees the most traction in India: conversational agents and work agents.

Conversations

Public benchmarks miss too much for conversation. They do not cover ambiguous messages, noisy speech recognition, code-mixed speech, answers that must be drawn from retrieved documents, or an instruction that must still be followed many turns later. So Sarvam built its own benchmark from production data, to show where frontier models break when they run as voice agents.

One instruction-following test puts a fixed line in the system prompt. The model must say it word for word on the tenth turn, for example ending the call with “Arre sir, we are done for today”. A model that loses the instruction over ten turns fails. Sarvam compared 105B against GPT 5.4 Mini and Gemini 3.5 Flash on it.

These numbers come from Sarvam's summary post. The widest gaps are in instruction following and linguistic quality, the two things that decide whether a voice agent survives a real call. The post puts the price at $0.80 per million blended tokens, against $4.50 for GPT 5.4 Mini and $9.00 for Gemini 3.5 Flash. On stage the speaker said about five times cheaper than GPT 5.4 Mini and eleven times cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash, and described it as intelligence per dollar. His headline claim was careful: the best conversational model available hosted in India, at a far lower price.

Example 1: retrieval over policy documents, in code-mixed Hindi and English

This slide shows what “linguistic quality” means in practice. The speaker described an assistant asking about a policy, with a customer who wants to know what changed in the policy documents. On the slide it is an agent offering a new personal loan in Hinglish, and the customer replies in the same mix: interested, but I heard something about policy changes, what is that? Sarvam 105B retrieves about fifteen policy documents with a tool call, then answers briefly in the same mixed language. The process is simpler, interest rates now start at 11 percent, and there are no foreclosure charges after six months. The slide lists how other models fail. One quotes the rate but drops the foreclosure waiver. One replies entirely in English to a customer who is code-switching. One reads the policy clause out word for word on the call. The speaker said Gemini, in this case, went through all fifteen points it had retrieved instead of answering directly.

Non-trivial. A good voice answer is not a complete answer. The model has to select from what it retrieved, say it briefly, and stay in the caller’s language mix. The failure modes on the slide are about selecting, and about language.

The second example was instruction following. The system prompt says the agent must not assume anything, and may only call the tool that clears a balance when the user is very clear. In production logs, frontier models often ignored that rule and called the balance tool on an ambiguous request, and money was involved. NVIDIA × Sarvam: serving mixture-of-experts on Blackwell

Expert parallelism, one-sided all-to-all, NVFP4 and the four problems of serving a trillion-parameter model.

9 min read

Utkarsh from NVIDIA and Ashwin from Sarvam covered how to co-design, optimise and serve large mixture-of-experts (MoE) models on NVIDIA Blackwell. Utkarsh did hardware, kernels and numerics. Ashwin did serving. It was a short talk without Q&A, and the recording stops partway through Ashwin's section.

Why systems matter more now

MoE models are growing on a quarterly or even monthly cadence. Kimi had just released a model of about 2.8 trillion parameters. Training compute is not growing at that pace, but inference demand is, for three reasons: bigger models, reasoning modes that take many intermediate steps before the first visible token, and context windows that went from a couple of thousand tokens to a million. Throwing compute at long context is not efficient, because the return per token falls. Utkarsh's opening point: a good model alone does not make a deployment succeed. The systems part does.

The vocabulary, and the two phases

  • Time to first token (TTFT) is what you see as the first token appears. The industry budget is about 1 to 2 seconds.
  • Inter-token latency is the time between consecutive tokens during decode.
  • FLOPs is how much compute you use and how efficiently. The core operation is matrix multiplication.
  • HBM is the high-bandwidth memory on the GPU package. Attention comes in several kinds, such as MHA, MLA and GQA, each with trade-offs.
  • Prefill processes the whole input at once, such as an entire repository. The work is matrix times matrix, so it is compute-bound, and it sets TTFT.
  • Decode is the autoregressive loop. Each step reads the KV cache and does matrix times vector, so it waits on memory instead of compute, and it sets inter-token latency. The KV cache from prefill lives in memory, possibly across several tiers.

Why MoE inference is harder

An MoE swaps the dense feed-forward layer for a set of experts. A trainable router at each layer decides which experts to activate for each token. That makes an MoE far cheaper in compute than a dense model of the same size, but it creates a communication problem. A model like DeepSeek-R1 has 256 experts. You spread experts across GPUs, so each GPU holds only a few. At scale, every batch triggers a dispatch to send tokens to the GPUs that hold their experts, and a combine to bring the results back. At high load this all-to-all traffic becomes the bottleneck. So deploying Kimi, GLM or Nemotron-Ultra-sized models of 500B and up means budgeting for network bandwidth, not only compute and memory.

He framed four axes to ablate when you plan a deployment: scale-up (one GPU becomes many, at rack scale), scale-out (joining servers), low precision, and the runtime layer, which is Dynamo on top of vLLM, SGLang or TensorRT-LLM.

Non-trivial. Hardware alone does not finish the job. The speaker said the NVL72 rack and the Grace link solve the bandwidth problem in hardware, but without software that uses them you cannot scale the experts in production. That is why the talk pairs every hardware feature with a kernel or communication change.

Hardware: a rack as one GPU

An eight-GPU H200 server cannot hold a frontier MoE's experts, mostly because of memory. The GB200 NVL72, and the GB300, change the unit. It is a single rack with 72 GPUs, four per tray, joined by an NVLink all-to-all switch so that all GPUs share one plane of communication. The aggregate bandwidth is about 130 TB/s.

The second hardware idea is to treat the Grace CPU, an ARM chip, as extra memory for the GPU. Each Grace CPU has 480 GB of LPDDR, 960 GB across two Grace domains. By his count a single GPU has about 184 GB of HBM, which is not enough to hold all the experts. The link between Grace and the GPU is a chip-to-chip interconnect at 900 GB/s bidirectional. He quoted PCIe between host and device as 64, about 120 and up to 256 GB/s, depending on the generation. So you can park expert weights in CPU memory and move hot experts to the GPU often.

Communication: one-sided all-to-all

In a conventional dispatch, sender and receiver both take part and data goes through multiple buffers. In the one-sided version, the sender writes directly into the receiver's GPU memory using puts to dispatch and gets to combine, on shared symmetric memory. The receiver is not involved, and the intermediate FIFO copies disappear. He called the scheme precision-agnostic, meaning the transport does not care about the datatype. In the setup he described, tokens are cast to 4 bits before dispatch, so 4-bit tensors cross NVLink. He did not name the 4-bit format for this step. All of this happens behind the scenes while a coding or agent request is running, so the saving applies to every request.

Kernel level

  • CUDA graphs help on any GPU, not just GB-series. Launching a kernel costs CPU time, and on small workloads that cost shows.
  • PDL (programmatic dependent launch) lets one kernel start its setup while the previous one is still finishing. His example of work that overlaps instead of running serially was routing and computation.
  • Fusion: the QKV concatenation is removed by feeding the Q, K and V tensors straight into the attention kernel.

Wide expert parallelism, and why balance decides everything

Expert parallelism (EP) within one node ends up bound by memory bandwidth, so it helps to spread experts across nodes. He said that past EP 8 the grouped matrix multiply grows, though his own graph below shows GEMM time falling as EP rises, so treat that remark as loosely worded. What is clear is that which experts are hot depends on the data. Routing decides at run time which GPUs are active for a given query, so the load moves. Two measures keep this under control.

  • A custom all-gather prepare kernel replaces the standard all-gather, so communication volume stays flat as EP grows instead of rising with it.
  • Hot experts depend on the data, but the pattern settles over time, so it can be predicted. Background C++ threads move the next hot experts from Grace memory to the GPU ahead of need, so inference never stops for rebalancing.

The numbers on the slide: as EP rises from 4 to 72, the MoE GEMM time falls from 275 microseconds to 75. That sounds tiny, but against a TTFT budget of 1 to 2 seconds, every microsecond is counted. The second graph shows the catch. In the imbalanced case the all-to-all spikes above 400 microseconds. His rule: you have to enforce a balanced load to use wide EP.

Non-trivial. Wide EP is only a win when the load is balanced. With an imbalanced load, he said, there is no point in doing wide EP. The hot-expert prediction and background swapping are how the system tries to stay balanced. By my arithmetic, going from EP 4 to 72 is 18 times more GPUs for a 3.7 times faster GEMM, while one imbalanced all-to-all can cost more than the whole 275 microsecond GEMM at EP 4.

Numerics: block-scaled low precision

Going from 16 or 32 bits down to 8 loses accuracy, so the formats use micro-scaling. Block-scaled formats came out of an industry consortium. A tensor in these formats carries three things: the encoded values, a scaling factor and metadata. In the 8-bit block format (MXFP8), 32 values share one scale, and that scale is limited to powers of two (an E8M0 value). He said block scaling started with the Hopper generation and that Hopper natively supports MXFP8. That is how he put it. Micro-scaling hardware support is usually credited to Blackwell, so check this before relying on it. The talk asked why this needs hardware support at all, when software could do it. The reason: for each block you compute a scale, do a dot product, accumulate and post-process, and that many operations add up. Done in software they cost too much, which is why the feature went into the hardware.

NVFP4 goes to 4 bits with two levels of scaling. The elements are 4-bit E2M1. He also named E3M0 as a second variant used in some cases, which does not match how NVFP4 is usually defined. Each block gets its own scale in E4M3, which is a finer set of values than powers of two, so the encoding is more accurate. But a finer per-block scale narrows the range of values the format can cover. So there is a second level: one FP32 scale for the entire tensor, which restores the range. The constraint that governs every low-bit technique is the same: keep the accuracy intact. NVIDIA open-sources these quantised checkpoints, for its own models and for others.

Non-trivial. Two-level scaling is the trick that makes 4 bits usable. The per-block E4M3 scale buys accuracy, the per-tensor FP32 scale buys back the range that the first one gave up. Neither would work alone.

Sarvam's side: serving at scale

Ashwin said this work came out of the token-factory announcement that morning. A large model like Kimi or GLM is no longer a single-model workload. It is a system of CPUs, RDMA, storage and more, at the scale of thousands of devices. Kimi K3 is the one he used as the example. He listed four challenges.

  1. Sharding. Kimi K3 does not fit on one B200 node even at 4 bits. And even when a model fits, one node is not efficient at both phases, because prefill is compute-heavy and decode is memory-bandwidth-heavy. So how do you split the work across them? Once the model is split sensibly, the next problem is the cache.
  2. The cache. The KV cache is insanely large. With context lengths of a million tokens for GLM and later models, a node holds only a few users. To avoid recomputing a user's cache when their next turn arrives, you need the cache in several offload tiers, shared across nodes over east-west RDMA networking.
  3. Reality. Everything above was tuned on static load tests. In production, users hit the system in unpredictable ways, from co-work agents, coding agents and APIs, at random arrival rates. Arrival rate and input and output lengths change where the optimal operating point is.
  4. Disaggregation. At this model size and context length it has become a necessity. To get the most input tokens per second from a node, and so cut the cost per input token, you must keep all the compute busy without hurting the latency targets of requests already in flight. The recording stops mid-sentence here.

Non-trivial. The best configuration is not a constant. It moves with the arrival rate and the input and output lengths. A system tuned on a static load test is tuned for a workload that production will never send.

What Anvaya is

Anvaya is a platform that builds a unified data layer over any kind of messy data and lets an analyst do multi-hop deep research on it. The demo case: after a recent corporate collapse, SEBI asked whether AI could help prevent something like it in the future. To test it, the team gave Anvaya only open-source data from before the event, as video, audio, PDFs and images, about one company, and asked whether it would spot trouble. How it works

  1. Buckets and an ontology. Anvaya groups the corpus into buckets that are neither too sparse nor too dense. It then uses an efficient sampling algorithm, so it does not read everything. With a million items, it visits each bucket, samples some data and builds the ontology from the samples.
  2. A knowledge graph with citations. For each entity, such as the company, the graph gathers everything from across the documents, writes a summary, shows where the data came from, and maps the entity's relationships. The source is what lets an analyst cite it, and it makes an investigation much simpler.
  3. An agent that questions the graph. The agent starts by asking what it needs to know in order to answer the report question. It then hires a second agent, which they called paranoid, to ask even more questions after seeing the data. These branch out in several directions, for example into the ownership web. While it answers, the graph highlights the nodes it is using, and the run ends in a report. If the analyst is unhappy with an answer, they can ask the system to go deeper on that question, and the graph shows which nodes the answer came from.

Non-trivial. Giving the model a bounded field removes part of the thinking. That is how, the speaker said, a smaller model can match the output of a bigger one. Non-trivial. The team ran a backtest by design. Because Anvaya saw only data from before the event, it could not know the outcome. That is the right way to ask whether AI could have flagged a problem.

The technical session

The technical session (15:20 on the agenda) was a panel on continual learning: how models keep improving after they ship. Sarvam's programme describes it as a panel on continual learning, evals and enterprise improvement loops. It ran about 38 minutes, with the last few minutes for audience questions.

The slide names four panelists: Vivek Raghavan, co-founder of Sarvam; Manish Gupta, Senior Director at Google DeepMind; Devendra Singh Chaplot, Sarvam; and Aaditya Sood, Surge Venture Partner at Peak XV. A fifth person from Sarvam moderated. This page is a question-by-question record, in the order the questions were asked. The moderator addressed most questions to a named person, so those attributions are firm. A few answers came without a name. Where I infer the speaker from the flow of the conversation, I say so in plain words. Where I cannot, I write “another panelist”.

The moderator set the frame at the start. Continual learning is a hotly discussed topic at every layer of abstraction: how can models keep improving as they get deployed more and more? To talk about models as systems that continuously improve, you first have to define the system and its components. So the first question was about the line between the model and the harness.

The technical session panel, on the stage under the Epoch block pattern

Q1. People hold very different views on the model and the harness: will the harness layer eventually fold into the model, or will it always be there to extend what the model can do? How do you define the components of a system that keeps improving, and how do you think about the harness layer? (Put first to Aaditya Sood, given his 20 years of building orchestration layers and watching Kubernetes and cloud infrastructure play out, then to the others.)

Aaditya Sood: Harnesses will never go away. What the industry has learned is that we want to build layered systems with a separation of concerns. He recalled a famous line that the white-haired people in the room will remember from X-Windows: separate the mechanism from the policy. In his view the model holds the non-deterministic logic engine, the intelligence engine. But a lot of business-level matters, such as risk appetite, authority, approvals and what counts as a good outcome, will always live in a layer above the model, because they are so specific to the application. Model capability can keep going up, but by and large there will always be a control loop, a harness.

Manish Gupta: He agreed with the first part: there will always be a need for both the model and the harness. But he did not think you should treat them as a clean separation of concerns, because the relationship between them is very close. One of his pre-doc researchers, now doing a PhD at Berkeley, visited and gave a talk called “training fast and slow”. That researcher is thinking of a startup whose whole thesis is how to co-evolve the model and the harness, and how to train them jointly.

Manish Gupta: He added that his own team already uses harnesses to improve model quality. This is the idea of recursive self-improvement. We are not fully there yet, he said, but many parts of the loop are already closed. Agents are used to improve the quality of the model, and then that higher-quality model is used to improve things further, and so on. For both capability and efficiency, his team is already seeing huge gains from agents that now do a lot of the efficiency work automatically, where people used to do it by hand.

Non-trivial. The panel disagreed on the framing more than on the conclusion. Sood wants a clean split, with policy above and the model below, so that each layer can change at its own rate. Gupta wants the two trained together. Both can be right for different layers: the business-policy layer stays separate, while the layer that talks directly to the model is the one that has to move with it. Sood returns to exactly this point later, with the clutch analogy.

Q2. Devendra, you have taken models through scaling. How do you think about the value a harness adds at different scales, and at different parts of training? How do we include the harness in the training loop?

Devendra Singh Chaplot: He agreed with Manish that the harness and the model need to co-evolve. Models should be trained with the harness in mind, and harnesses are designed for specific models.

Devendra Singh Chaplot: He gave a second reason why harnesses matter now and will matter in future: they let users customise the model for their own application. One-size-fits-all models will not work well for everyone's needs, he said, and that is why harnesses are important.

Devendra Singh Chaplot: On scale, he said that as model size grows, harnesses can become lighter, because the models can do a lot of the work themselves. But that does not mean harnesses go away completely. They remain very important.

Q3. Vivek, Sarvam has been deploying the 105B model inside various harnesses, and it is improving. How does scale change the value of the harness, and how much does the harness need to do? How far can we push a hundred-billion-class model, how do you deploy it in different places and learn from production traces, and how should we think about that loop?

Vivek Raghavan: When the model is smaller, the harness can play a bigger role in what you are trying to do. That is what Sarvam has seen: you can get significantly better outcomes, at least for the specific things you want to do. He agreed with both Manish and Devendra that models and harnesses will co-evolve.

Vivek Raghavan: Then he said he was curious, and honest that he had no real feel for the answer. As the model gets more powerful, one instinct is that the harness layer becomes thinner. But will the nature of the tasks we want to do change so much that we still have a significant harness, just a different one from today's? He was not sure which way it would go. What is very clear, he said, is that putting a harness around a model can dramatically improve outcomes.

Manish Gupta: (The speaker is not named here. The phrase “one aspect I can highlight” matches Gupta's own way of speaking earlier, so I attribute it to him, with some doubt.) One aspect where harnesses are useful whatever the power of the model is when you break the problem into sub-problems and have agents, all powered by those models, work together. Different agents solve different sub-problems.

Manish Gupta: Sometimes agents also compete on the same sub-problem, and you use leaderboards to see which does best. He tied this to AI for science, which Dr. Somanath had spoken about earlier in the day: you can now have many agents exploring different scientific hypotheses in parallel.

Manish Gupta: The analogy is how humans work. A single person does not do all the work, and we do not do it all in one shot. We do it repeatedly. Even when we code, we do not go from the first token to the last. We work as part of bigger teams, and he expects similar things to apply to AI agents powered by models.

Non-trivial. Vivek Raghavan's uncertainty is itself informative. The easy prediction is that a stronger model means a thinner harness. His point is that stronger models take on harder tasks, so the harness may not shrink. It may change shape instead. Gupta's multi-agent examples (decomposition, competing agents, leaderboards) are harness designs that exist because the model is strong, not because it is weak.

Q4. On continual learning, one end of the spectrum holds a purist view that imitation learning is the wrong direction and we need different algorithms and model architectures. The other end says it is simply an infrastructure problem of how quickly you can take production traces and learn online from them. What bets do you see in the research community, and which excite you most?

Manish Gupta: (The speaker is not named here. The reference to his team's language work, and the Haryanvi example, point to Gupta.) One reason companies like Google, Anthropic and OpenAI invest so much capital in these models is their bet that you ultimately get to the place where the models improve themselves: recursive self-improvement. The whole premise rests on that.

Manish Gupta: A lot of his team's own work is on languages, adding support for more languages, and a lot of that work is now done by agents. They do not want to curate all the data by hand. When you add a new language or a new dialect, you do not want to do the data curation and the evaluation manually. Agents do much of that work today.

Manish Gupta: It is not completely automated, because human judgement is still involved. RL has worked very well for verifiable problems, but not nearly as well for non-verifiable domains. Language work is largely non-verifiable. There is a judgement in how well, say, my model speaks Haryanvi. So a human still has to judge, and for that reason the work is not fully automated, though many aspects already are.

Non-trivial. The verifiable versus non-verifiable split explains where automation stops. Wherever a program can check the answer, the loop can close itself. Wherever the answer needs taste, as with a dialect like Haryanvi, a person is still inside the loop. This theme comes back in the answers on evals, traces and interpretability.

Q5. Sarvam has worked very deeply on evals, and for non-verifiable tasks humans have to be involved very deeply. One question that keeps coming up is: who evaluates the evals themselves? How should we think of evals as systems?

Vivek Raghavan: Evals are one of those problems where, each time you do the work a new way, you discover another layer of complexity. In the end, evals are everything. If you cannot get good evals, you are not going to go to a good place.

Vivek Raghavan: As the ambition grows to cover more and more complex tasks, the evals have to become more nuanced. He gave the practical case. When you are looking at something fairly narrow that you want to make better, you can probably build evals that steer the model to do very well on that task. But overall that may not be a good thing. You have to understand how representative the eval is of the real problem you want to solve.

Vivek Raghavan: You do not want to over-specify the eval and end up in places you will never deal with. At the same time, you do not want it to be so narrow that it misses the real problem. In the end, he said, continuous evals to clean up the data are the game. There is nothing else here. The quality of the data is the entire game.

Q6. When a task is very narrow and you optimise in that direction, the model can go far but perhaps at the cost of other things. This is the jagged intelligence we all experience: great at one task, regressing on another. Devendra, how is this handled at large scale? How do you cover a diversity of environments, and how much of that comes from synthetic environments versus real production traces? How do you weigh the value of one against the other?

Devendra Singh Chaplot: It is not one or the other. You have to use both. Real-world production traces give you the distribution of tasks that users care about, and they tell you what the models are good or bad at. But by design, production traces are backward-looking: they tell you only what people use models for today. If you want to think about the future, where you build into models capabilities that people do not use them for yet, you need something else. (Part of this sentence is cut off on the recording, so the second half is my reading of where it was heading.)

Devendra Singh Chaplot: He then described the difficulty of adding new capabilities. It is a very tricky balance. Whenever you add new capabilities, models may regress on the old ones. So you have to be very careful in designing the data mixture, and you continue training on the old capabilities so that the model does not regress on them.

Aaditya Sood: (The moderator invited a speaker with whom the panel had already discussed this, and the answer opens with “completely agree with him”. The framing around business context and outcomes matches Sood's earlier answer, so I attribute it to him with some doubt.) One big challenge with real-world traces is that they are the exhaust pipe of things that have already happened, and the policy at the time was already baked in.

Aaditya Sood: The second challenge, from a purely philosophical point of view, comes from what a code editor like Cursor does. It takes traces, sees when the user intervened and what new suggestions were made. That presupposes that our user was intelligent. At scale, we do not know whether the user made the right choice when they intervened in the model. That challenge will keep showing up in real-world data.

Aaditya Sood: In coding especially, just because your code compiles does not mean it is correct. Just because your unit tests pass does not mean it is correct. Only from a business context, when you deploy at scale and see whether you are meeting business objectives or outcomes, can you say this was the right thing to do. That is again a judgement call. For that reason, other than very narrow algorithmic domains, he expects it to remain an open challenge for at least a while.

Manish Gupta: (Again the speaker is not named. The phrasing “one more aspect that I want to highlight” matches Gupta's turns earlier in the session, so I attribute it to him with some doubt.) Synthetic data is very useful for creating increasingly harder problem instances, so the model can hill-climb. If you give a model too hard a problem, it cannot learn from it. There is a Goldilocks zone: the problem should not be too easy, but still solvable.

Manish Gupta: You make the problems increasingly harder. The model improves, and then you make it work on a newer, harder class of problems, and so on. For a model that keeps learning, this is another process where synthetic data is extremely helpful. As an aside, he said, this is how we teach children. Never teach them calculus in grade one.

Non-trivial. A user who accepts or corrects a model's output is treated as a label, but that label is only as good as the user. A patch that compiles and passes tests may still be wrong for the business. So real traces tell you what users asked for, synthetic tasks tell you what they will ask for, and neither tells you if the answer was right. That needs a deployment outcome, which arrives slowly.

Q7. How do we operationalise this continuous improvement, especially when learning from production traces? Models are often deployed inside enterprise infrastructure, which may have its own improvement loops and security boundaries, and the model labs run their own loops at much larger scale. How should we think about the infrastructure that makes this happen? (Asked first to Devendra, given his experience designing Tinker.)

Devendra Singh Chaplot: He pointed back to the morning conversation. Model building needs a few key things. Compute: of course you need a lot of it. People and talent. And then the question is what the people do.

Devendra Singh Chaplot: People build the infrastructure needed for model training, for iterating and for scaling-law research. People build the training recipe, which includes the data, the model architecture and the optimisation. And people actually train the model. All of these components have to go together.

Devendra Singh Chaplot: Once you deploy the model, you get production data and feedback. You incorporate that feedback and think about how to keep improving the model. At the same time you think about what the next generation of models can do that they cannot do today, and about innovative ways of unlocking new capabilities.

Manish Gupta: (Unnamed in the session; the opening “I just also want to highlight” again matches Gupta, so this is my inference.) For enterprise customers in particular, you have to be very careful. The default position is that whatever the enterprise does in a cloud setting, all that data belongs to the enterprise customer, so you do not use it to improve your model.

Manish Gupta: With consumers it is different. There is a wealth of data, both explicit signals and implicit signals, and the implicit signals come from many things about how users behave. (The rest of this sentence is cut off on the recording.)

Q8. Many enterprises worry that if they invest in post-training a model on their own data now, the next pre-trained model may be much better, and the investment will be wasted. How should enterprises design an improvement loop so that it keeps absorbing the advances happening outside the loop as base models get better?

Another panelist: He had been surprised that most enterprises are actually very concerned about something else. They say, “I don't want my data leaking to my competitors.” That, he said, is usually the higher-order bit. (Probably Manish Gupta, who made the previous point about enterprise data. The voice is not named, so treat this as an inference.)

Aaditya Sood: Going back to his earlier point, he described a layer cake: you build layers, each handling a different thing. Right now, he said, one of the challenges is that we have shoehorned too many things into the harness. Every exception and corner case goes in. Whenever something weird happens, we put a rule in the harness, if this, then that, until the model gets better.

Aaditya Sood: He understands why, because you have to make things work right now and cannot wait. But in the long term, that leads to brittle systems when everything is so tightly coupled. His view is that enterprises would separate these things out.

Aaditya Sood: As the model jumps in capability, the lowest layer, which Manish earlier said is tightly coupled, is the only one that needs to be changed, rewritten or adapted. He thinks of it as the clutch that couples to the engine. The higher-level policy and business goals do not change that fast.

Aaditya Sood: He takes an evolutionary perspective: different layers have different rates of change. The same holds for learning. Doing a model weight update is a very big thing, and an irrevocable one. It is like a human neocortex getting updated. You normally do not do that easily, and it takes a while. So there will be different rates of change, and that is how enterprises can keep up as more and more capability shows up. The abstraction keeps moving.

Non-trivial. The practical advice here is to put the replaceable part at the bottom. The coupling layer is the one that should be easy to rewrite when a better base model arrives. Business policy sits above it and stays put. Thin, swappable glue is what stops an enterprise's earlier investment from being wasted when the base model improves.

Q9. (Follow-up from another panelist.) It is fascinating that you say changing the model is a big deal, and also that the policies change slowly. If both are slow, what is the thing that changes fast?

Aaditya Sood: He clarified what he meant by calling it a big deal. Say you ran your traces for a week from your customers: should you go and update your model weights for that? His answer is that weight updates should be a slow process. You should not do them that frequently or that casually.

Aaditya Sood: One reason is adversarial attacks. He worries about people tricking you into generating bad traces. If you are too quick on the uptake, you might fall into that hole.

Non-trivial. If a model learns from its traces, then whoever can shape those traces can shape the model. Slow, reviewed weight updates are as much a security control as a stability control, which is a different reason from the usual one about forgetting.

Q10. Would you classify the inability to change model weights fast enough from, say, weekly production traces as an infrastructure problem? Or is it not an ideal thing to do in the first place, given that we hear Cursor updates its models every few hours?

Manish Gupta: It is the problem that someone mentioned earlier, catastrophic forgetting. There is a sweet spot you want to reach: you do want these models to learn, but as they learn new capabilities, you do not want them to forget their previous ones. That has fundamentally not been fully solved. A lot of exciting research is happening on how to co-evolve.

Q11. Devendra, would you also chime in on that? (He took it toward how enterprises can run their own improvement loops.)

Devendra Singh Chaplot: It is pretty hard for most enterprises to customise models themselves. That is why it is very important to build the platform and infrastructure that makes it easier. There is a lot of motivation among enterprises, governments, researchers and developers to train their own models, build custom models, fine-tune and specialise them for their application.

Devendra Singh Chaplot: The challenge is how to actually do it. Without a good platform, you have to get your own GPUs, write your own distributed training code base and manage all the hardware yourself. That takes many different skills that most enterprises do not have. If the tools and resources were there, he said, enterprises would do it.

Devendra Singh Chaplot: He listed what they care about. First, ownership of the models. Second, as the earlier speaker said, not having their data leak to their competitors. Third, not having the model training labs become their competitors tomorrow. Fourth, access risk: labs might cut off access or deprecate old models. Fifth, pricing. This is why they are all motivated to train their own models. It is just very hard today, but it will become easier, and more and more enterprises and people will train models.

Manish Gupta: He added a related piece of work. A paper at ICLR 2024 from his team, called CALM, is a way to compose two existing large language models. For his team, the initial motivation was languages. When they added support for Indian languages, there was a concern that better support for them could make the model regress on languages like English. Even a minor regression would not be acceptable. With CALM you take the two models and fuse them into a new one.

Manish Gupta: They were very excited that this is also an approach for enterprises to customise their own model: you can take an existing model and compose it with another. He would like to see this methodology adopted in the real world, and noted that the paper appeared almost two years ago. (The recording around this last sentence is partly unclear.)

Q12. As we near the end: what is the one direction of research or engineering in continual learning, in intelligent systems, models and the systems around them, that you find most exciting and expect to see a lot of progress on in the next six to twelve months?

Devendra Singh Chaplot: He is most excited about algorithms that automatically improve the model as you interact with it. As a user, you should not need to think about how to continually improve the model. You interact with it as you would with any LLM today, and it keeps improving, every week and every day. It learns your preferences and learns from the mistakes it made in the past.

Devendra Singh Chaplot: He thinks this can be done by innovating on how we use interactions with humans to improve the model itself. There are multiple ways. You can use external memory and RAG, as many people already do. But you can also do it with model updates, especially low-rank adapters, which can let every individual have their own model, with their own weight updates in low rank, just by interacting with it.

Manish Gupta: He started from the analogy with humans. The next part of his answer is unclear on the recording. It was about capturing and harnessing all of the learning that has happened. The moderator later said that two panelists had mentioned Newton and Einstein, so this answer evidently touched on them too.

Manish Gupta: He closed with the other half: there is an element of subjectivity, and the question is how to make progress on those problems. That, he said, is another exciting direction his teams are working on. (This ties back to the point about non-verifiable domains such as language quality.)

Aaditya Sood: Before saying what he finds most interesting, he made an observation about the structure of scientific revolutions. Part of what Newton and Einstein did was to throw away some of what they had been taught, regress ten or twenty years, and rebuild. Progress has a sawtooth curve. It will be interesting to see models do that: to say, you have taught me these twenty things, but I am going to suppose ten of them are not true, because they are only conventional wisdom.

Aaditya Sood: What he finds most interesting, and he said he does not know how it relates to continual learning, is interpretability. How do we know whether a trace or a reasoning chain that a model gives us is what actually happened? As we build more and more systems on top, we are taking a non-deterministic worker, putting it inside a shell, and slowly trusting it to make more and more decisions. Unless we can verify, not just by the outcome but through the reasoning chain, that it did the right thing, he thinks it might lead to bad situations.

Non-trivial. Most continual-learning proposals assume knowledge only accumulates. Sood's sawtooth view says that sometimes the right move is to unlearn: drop received wisdom, step back, rebuild. Nobody on the panel had a method for that, and it sits oddly with the earlier worry about catastrophic forgetting. Forgetting the wrong things is a bug; choosing to drop the right ones would be a skill.

The non-trivial ideas, in one place

The same highlights as the gem callouts inside each section, gathered here. Each one is a point that I would not have guessed from the headline of the talk. Tap one to go to where it came from.

  1. The moat is judgement, not an appWhat Sarvam says it owns is the judgement from training models for years. That is why its harness knows where a model needs load-bearing help and where it does not.
  2. Async RL has a second bill: weight syncOverlapping generation and training removes idle GPUs. But every step still ships weights from trainer to generators. LoRA shrinks that to something a network can carry, so one choice fixes both.
  3. Diarisation inside the encoderA pipeline cuts the audio into pieces before transcribing, so it loses the overlap. Sarvam combines diarisation and recognition in one model, with an encoder biased toward speakers, to keep it.
  4. Refusing to act is the real agent testWhether a model stops and asks when a request is ambiguous came from production logs, which is why Sarvam built its own benchmark.
  5. The agent reads and drills down, instead of receiving top-kAn agent that browses a knowledge base like a file system beats vector retrieval. The largest jump on the slide is OfficeQA Pro, from 34% to 52%.
  6. Open models cannot tell when they are doneSarvam did not wait for a better model. It split goal mode into planner, worker and verifier, so the check is made by an agent that did not do the work.
  7. Self-improving agents are evals running in productionDefine success, read thousands of conversations, test variants, keep the winner, and keep a human approval at the end.
  8. Credentials never enter the sandboxThe agent platform is sold on security and operations: a gateway guards everything run in the sandbox, and one agent can be shared with a team.
  9. Hardware needs software to scale a mixture of expertsHardware alone does not finish the job. The NVL72 rack and the Grace link solve bandwidth, but you still need software that uses them.
  10. Wide expert parallelism only wins when balancedGoing from EP 4 to 72 cuts MoE GEMM time from 275 to 75 microseconds. An imbalanced load spikes the all-to-all above 400, which is why hot experts are predicted and pre-loaded.
  11. Two scales make 4 bits usableA per-block E4M3 scale buys accuracy. A per-tensor FP32 scale buys back the range the first one gave up.
  12. The best serving config is a moving targetIt shifts with the arrival rate and with input and output lengths. A system tuned on a static load test is tuned for traffic that production never sends.
  13. A bounded field lets a small model do a big jobAn ontology built from samples gives the model a structure to work inside. The speaker said that is how a smaller model can match a bigger one, and a 30B model did the demo on one machine.
  14. Why the keyboard survivedIt was predictable, reliable and free. He argued no other interface solved reliability, and that a state-of-the-art speech model now does.
  15. ISRO built the applications firstIt learned to use space data, bought launches, and built the launcher once the demand and the skills existed. Capacity to use came before capacity to produce.
  16. AWS says it reinvented the network for trainingAWS argued that bulk-synchronous training needs the whole fabric in lockstep, so it built a protocol with no per-packet acknowledgement and failure detection under a second.
  17. Answering “who evaluates the evals?”Continuously. Evals clean the data, the cleaned data improves the evals. An eval that is too narrow or too over-specified trains a model for a task nobody has.
  18. Traces say what users asked, not what was rightThey are backward-looking exhaust. Code that compiles, or tests that pass, is not proof that the business problem was solved.
  19. Keep policy in the layer that does not moveShoehorning every exception into the harness makes a brittle system. Keep the coupling layer replaceable, and treat a weight update as a rare, reviewed event, because whoever can produce traces can teach the model.

What I am taking away

tok 01Own the whole pathModels, harness, data and infra were shown as one stack, built from model research. The mission was patient: a decade, not a quarter.
tok 02Price and speed are featuresTime to first token, tokens per second and rupees per minute were on the main slides, next to accuracy.
tok 03Test on your own trafficPublic benchmarks miss code-mixing, noisy audio and long policies. Sarvam built its own from real conversations.
tok 04Route, do not pick one model36% of tasks to a small model and 64% to a big one cut the cost by about 40%.
tok 05Count cost per finished taskTokens are the wrong unit for agents. Dollars per solved task, and rupees per call, are what a buyer feels.
tok 06Agents optimise systems tooAn agent with a profiler found a 15x speed-up in a night. The loop is: measure, try, keep what wins.
tok 07The frontier is a sum you can do3T parameters, 100B active, 100T tokens is about 6×1025 FLOPs. It is a compute-and-people problem, not magic.
tok 08Thousands of models, not threeIndia has its own languages, legal systems and public policy. Specialised models, trained as a service, fit that.
tok 09The hard parts repeatPrefill and decode, KV cache reuse, kernels and async RL were on Sarvam's slides, just as at the PyTorch meetup.
tok 10Policy lives outside the modelMechanism in the model, policy in the harness. Approvals, authority and risk rules stay fixed while the model changes.
tok 11Evals are everythingIf you cannot evaluate it, you cannot build it. Subjective tasks, such as a dialect, still need human judges.
tok 12Traces teach, environments stretchProduction traces show real intent and gaps. Synthetic environments teach new skills. Neither proves a result was right.
tok 13Hardware needs softwareThe NVL72 rack and the Grace link solve bandwidth. Without kernels and a balanced expert load, you still cannot scale a mixture of experts.
tok 14Sovereignty is serving the tokensOpen weights can be downloaded by anyone. Serving them from India is what puts the intelligence under local control.
tok 15Judgement is the hard partIn evals, traces, language quality and weight updates, the question that kept returning was who decides what counts as good.

The theme, as at the last event: India is not only using models. It is building the factory under them.
