What made each family capable

In April 2023, LLaVA was a 13B model whose two training stages took under a day on eight GPUs. By late 2025, open vision-language models were mixtures of experts trained on trillions of tokens, with reinforcement learning on top. This post asks the same questions of every open family in between. What did its builders do? And which of those choices made the model capable?

The earlier posts in this series explain each ingredient on its own. Inside a Modern Vision Encoder covers how pixels become visual tokens. Teaching Language Models to See covers pretraining. One Model, Many Visual Skills covers instruction tuning. Here the unit is the family instead of the ingredient.

A family is a line of models from one team, such as LLaVA or Qwen-VL. Each version usually fixes what the last one could not do. So reading a family in order shows what its own builders thought mattered. Reading across families shows which choices recur, and which ones the evidence supports.

The VLM Architecture Gallery already draws 71 architectures side by side. This post does not redraw them. It asks how each model was built: the data, the stages, the compute, and the experiments that show what mattered.

One warning before we start. Technical reports are uneven. Some report careful ablations. Others report only final scores. The words "not reported" appear often below. That is information too: it tells you which claims rest on evidence and which rest on a single final number.

The checklist as a lens

We need a fixed set of questions, or each family will be described in its own terms and nothing will line up. The questions below come from the survey checklist used across this series. They fall into four groups, plus one about scale.

DataWhich existing datasets were used, and what data engines were built. How the data was filtered. What was synthetic. How much data went into each training stage, and in what mixture. These questions come first because, in most ablations below, data choices moved scores more than architecture did. Post 04 covers pretraining data and post 06 covers instruction data.

ModelModel size and the parameter split between vision encoder, connector and language decoder. Why that encoder and decoder. How images are preprocessed: fixed size, tiles or native resolution. How the connector joins the two. How several images and video are handled. How many tokens an image costs, and how they are compressed. Post 02, post 03, post 11 and post 16 cover these.

TrainingPretraining tasks and losses. Fine-tuning tasks. Whether there is a preference or reinforcement learning (RL) stage. Any new training task. The ablations that show what mattered, and the practical tips. Post 08 covers preferences, post 12 covers RL and post 13 covers reasoning.

EvaluationWhich benchmarks were reported. Which capabilities the model has: OCR, visual question answering, grounding, counting, documents, charts, video, agents. Which languages. And, last, what enabled each capability. Post 07 covers benchmarks, post 09 grounding, post 10 documents and post 14 agents.

ScaleHow much compute, how many tokens and how much wall-clock time, where reported. How the family progressed from one version to the next. Post 05 covers scaling laws.

The five benchmarks used for comparison

To compare families, I use one fixed set of five benchmarks wherever the reports allow it. Each one probes a different skill.

  • MMMU (validation split) asks college-level questions across many subjects, with an image in each. It leans heavily on the language model's knowledge.
  • DocVQA (test split, ANLS score) asks questions about scanned documents. It needs fine text reading.
  • ChartQA (test split, relaxed accuracy) asks questions about charts. It needs reading plus a little arithmetic.
  • MathVista (testmini split) asks visual math questions. It needs perception and reasoning together.
  • OCRBench scores text recognition and text-centric questions out of 1,000. Some reports give it as a percentage. I convert those to the 1,000 scale and say so.

Nuance

Scores from different reports are not controlled comparisons, and sometimes not even comparisons of the same model. LLaVA-NeXT-34B scored 51.1 on MMMU in its January 2024 blog and 46.7 in the team's own May 2024 re-evaluation. InternVL2-76B has two official MMMU numbers, 55.2 and 58.2, from two evaluation toolkits. Pixtral's report showed that lenient answer parsing raises some models' scores and not others. In practice: treat a difference between two papers as a rough position, and treat a difference inside one paper, with everything else held fixed, as evidence.

Three years of open VLMs

Before going family by family, it helps to see them all on one time axis. Figure 2 places every version this post covers. The marker shape shows how the model handles image resolution. A ring marks a model with a preference or RL stage.

Four phases stand out.

  • 2023: connect a frozen encoder. LLaVA joined a frozen CLIP encoder to Vicuna with one linear layer. IDEFICS reproduced Flamingo's cross-attention design. Qwen-VL and InternVL took two other bets: more input resolution with grounding data, and a 6B vision encoder.
  • Early 2024: the race for resolution. LLaVA-NeXT, InternVL 1.5, MiniCPM-V and Idefics2 all began cutting large images into tiles. Careful design studies appeared at the same time: Prismatic, MM1, Idefics2 and Cambrian-1.
  • Late 2024: native resolution, open data and video. Qwen2-VL and Pixtral encoded images at their own size. Molmo and Idefics3 released large open datasets. LLaVA-OneVision put single images, multiple images and video in one model.
  • 2025: sparse models and RL. Most new releases used mixture-of-experts language models. Almost all added a preference or RL stage, and many shipped a separate "thinking" variant.

Insight

The major families fixed things in the same order. Resolution came first, then data curation, then preference or RL training. InternVL went from a fixed size (v1) to tiling (1.5), to data filtering (2.5), to preference optimisation (3) and cascade RL (3.5). Qwen-VL went from a fixed size, to native resolution (Qwen2-VL), to DPO (Qwen2.5-VL), to full RL (Qwen3-VL). The order makes sense given two findings later in this post: resolution sets a hard ceiling on document tasks, and GLM-4.1V's authors report that RL gains are bounded by what the base model can already sample. In practice: when a model is weak at reading, more RL will not fix it.

LLaVA

LLaVA (Apr 2023) → LLaVA-1.5 (Oct 2023) → LLaVA-NeXT (Jan 2024) → LLaVA-OneVision (Aug 2024)

LLaVA is the family most later work started from. Its first version had a single idea. A language model can be taught to talk about images if you give it instruction data in the right format. The visual knowledge can come from a frozen CLIP encoder.

DataThe first LLaVA trained a projection on 595K image-caption pairs filtered from CC3M. Then it trained the projection and the language model on LLaVA-Instruct-158K: 58K conversations, 23K detailed descriptions and 77K complex-reasoning questions. A text-only GPT-4 wrote these from COCO captions and bounding boxes. GPT-4 never saw a pixel. LLaVA-1.5 replaced most of that with academic data: a 665K mix that includes VQAv2 (83K), GQA (72K), OCR-VQA (80K), RefCOCO (48K), Visual Genome (86K) and 40K text-only ShareGPT conversations. LLaVA-NeXT added document and chart sets (DocVQA, ChartQA, AI2D, DVQA, SynthDOG) and 15K real user requests answered by GPT-4V. It removed TextCaps because it shares images with the TextVQA benchmark. LLaVA-OneVision grew to a 4M-sample "knowledge" stage, 99.8% of it synthetic, plus 3.2M single-image and 1.6M mixed samples.

ModelThe encoder moved from CLIP ViT-L at 224 px, to 336 px, to SigLIP SO400M at 384 px. The connector moved from one linear layer to a two-layer MLP and stayed there. The language model moved from Vicuna to Qwen2. Resolution grew through tiling. LLaVA-NeXT's "AnyRes" cuts an image into a grid of 336 px tiles plus a downsized copy, up to 2,880 visual tokens. OneVision allows up to nine 384 px crops, up to 7,290 tokens. The Hugging Face processor can exceed that, because it also adds a base image and row newlines: it returned 8,667 tokens for a 600 × 2000 image. When a grid would exceed the budget, it shrinks each crop's token grid by bilinear interpolation.

TrainingEvery version keeps the same skeleton: a short connector-only stage, then full training. LLaVA-1.5 kept the encoder frozen. From LLaVA-NeXT on, the encoder is trained too, but with a learning rate 5 to 10 times lower than the language model's. OneVision adds a curriculum: knowledge data first, then single images, then a mix of single images, multiple images and video. No version has an RL stage, except a DPO-trained video variant of LLaVA-NeXT.

EvaluationLLaVA-1.5 reported state-of-the-art results on 11 of its 12 benchmarks at the time. Its full training took about a day on one 8-A100 node. OneVision-72B reached 56.8 on MMMU, 91.3 on DocVQA, 83.7 on ChartQA and 67.5 on MathVista. The training data was English. Chinese ability appeared anyway, through ShareGPT: LLaVA-1.5-13B scored 63.6 on MMBench-CN. OneVision added Chinese data explicitly.

LLaVA-1.5's paper includes an ablation chain that adds one change at a time to the original LLaVA. It is one of the clearest records of what moved an early VLM.

Insight

In LLaVA-1.5's ablation chain, adding VQAv2 together with a one-line answer-format prompt ("Answer the question using a single word or phrase") raised the MME score from 809.6 to 1,323.8. Swapping the linear connector for an MLP then added about 31 points. Moving from 224 to 336 px added about 24. In practice: the first large gains came from task data and from telling the model what answer format to use, not from the architecture.

The LLaVA-NeXT team later ran a separate ablation study. Scaling the language model helped more than scaling the vision encoder. Among encoders, SigLIP SO400M gave the best cost-to-quality trade-off. And when tokens had to be cut, it was better to keep the high resolution and shrink each tile's tokens than to feed a low-resolution image. That last point comes back in the synthesis.

Qwen-VL

Qwen-VL (Aug 2023) → Qwen2-VL (Aug 2024) → Qwen2.5-VL (Jan 2025) → Qwen3-VL (Sep 2025)

The Qwen-VL line from Alibaba is the clearest case of a family rebuilding its image pipeline with each version. Figure 3 walks through what changed.

Qwen-VLThe first model paired a 1.9B OpenCLIP ViT-bigG with a 7.7B Qwen-7B. A single cross-attention layer with 256 learned queries compressed the encoder's 1,024 patch features to 256 tokens. Images were resized to a 224 px square, then 448 px. The data work was serious from the start. The team cleaned 5B raw image-text pairs down to 1.4B, keeping 28%. A second stage trained everything on seven tasks at once, including 3.5M grounding and 24.8M OCR samples. Boxes were written as plain text on a 0 to 1,000 grid. The result was strong for its time on text reading and grounding: 65.1 on DocVQA and 89.4 on RefCOCO validation for the 7B base model.

Qwen2-VLThe second generation removed the fixed size. A 675M encoder with 2D rotary positions takes the image at its own resolution, then an MLP merges each 2 × 2 group of patch tokens into one. A 224 × 224 image costs 66 tokens. A large page can cost up to 16,384. Positions in the language model became M-RoPE: each token gets a time, a height and a width index. Video is sampled at 2 frames per second, and two frames form one 3D patch. Training used about 600B tokens for the encoder alone, then about 800B for everything. In the 72B model this reached 96.5 on DocVQA and 877 on OCRBench.

Qwen2.5-VLThe third generation trained a new encoder from scratch. Most of its 32 layers use window attention over 112 px windows. Only four layers attend globally. This keeps cost roughly linear in image size. Video positions now track real time, so the gap between two frames' indexes says how far apart they are in seconds. Boxes are written in the image's actual pixel coordinates. Pretraining grew to about 4.1T tokens over three stages, the last at 32K context. The data added long documents parsed into an HTML format with a box on every element, 1M synthetic charts, 6M real tables and agent trajectories. Post-training used about 2M SFT samples, half of them text, and then DPO. The text ability survived: 71.2 on MMLU-Pro against 71.1 for the Qwen2.5-72B language model alone.

Qwen3-VLThe fourth generation switched to a SigLIP-2 encoder and added DeepStack. Features from three encoder depths are added into the first three layers of the language model, without adding tokens. The rotary positions became "interleaved": time, height and width now each cover both high and low frequencies. Video timestamps moved into the text as tokens such as "<3.0 seconds>". Boxes went back to a 0 to 1,000 grid. Pretraining ran in four stages up to a 256K context. Post-training added a long chain-of-thought cold start, distillation from a text teacher and two RL stages. The report says OCR now covers 39 languages. The flagship 235B-A22B Instruct model scores 78.7 on MMMU, 97.1 on DocVQA and 920 on OCRBench.

Two Qwen ablations are worth knowing. In Qwen2-VL, native resolution with an average of 1,924 tokens scored 866 on OCRBench. A fixed budget of 3,136 tokens scored 786. Letting each image use the tokens its size needs beat giving every image more. In Qwen3-VL, DeepStack raised a small model's average from 74.7 to 76.0, with InfoVQA up 2.3 points.

Nuance

Qwen reversed two of its own choices. Qwen2.5-VL wrote boxes in absolute pixels; Qwen3-VL went back to a 0 to 1,000 grid "for robustness across resolutions and aspect ratios". Qwen2.5-VL tied video positions to absolute time; Qwen3-VL replaced that with text timestamps because the position indexes became large and sparse on long videos. Earlier, Qwen-VL had tested window attention and dropped it because loss was "significantly higher". Qwen2.5-VL then adopted it, in an encoder trained from scratch with that design. In practice: a design that fails when retrofitted, or that works at one video length, can flip at another scale. Read each choice together with the scale it was tested at.

InternVL

InternVL 1 (Dec 2023) → 1.5 (Apr 2024) → 2 (Jul 2024) → 2.5 (Dec 2024) → 3 (Apr 2025) → 3.5 (Aug 2025)

InternVL from Shanghai AI Lab started with a contrarian bet. Vision encoders were around 0.3B to 1B parameters while language models were 7B to 70B. The team argued that this gap was the problem, and built a 6B vision encoder.

InternVL 1InternViT-6B was trained contrastively on 4.98B image-text pairs, cleaned from 6.03B. It was then joined to "QLLaMA", an 8B language-model-sized middleware with 96 learned queries. That connector was about 42 times larger than a BLIP-2 Q-Former. Chat versions used Vicuna at 224 or 336 px.

InternVL 1.5This version made the recipe that later families copied. Images are cut into 448 px tiles. The number of tiles, from 1 to 12, is chosen to match the image's aspect ratio among 35 options, and a 448 px thumbnail of the whole image is added. A "pixel shuffle" step folds each 2 × 2 block of patch features into the channel dimension, so each tile costs 256 tokens instead of 1,024. A simple MLP feeds the result to InternLM2-20B. The 6B encoder was retrained at 448 px with dynamic resolution, and kept improving as the language model changed. The 26B model reached 90.9 on DocVQA. The authors noted a trade-off: more tiles helped OCR tasks but slightly hurt MMMU, AI2D and MMBench.

InternVL2 and 2.5InternVL2 spread the recipe over seven sizes. Small models used a 300M encoder (InternViT-300M), and large ones kept the 6B. InternVL 2.5 added two ideas. "Progressive scaling" trains the encoder once with a mid-size language model, then reuses it with a larger one without retraining. And a strict filtering pipeline removed repetitive samples, because, in the authors' words, "merely thousands" of them can make long outputs loop. The SFT set grew from 7.3M to 16.3M samples, about 40% of tokens from video. InternVL2.5-78B used about 120B training tokens and became the first open model above 70 on MMMU (70.1, with chain-of-thought).

InternVL3This version replaced the "adapt a finished text model" approach with native multimodal pretraining. Text and multimodal data are trained together in one stage, about 200B tokens at a text-to-multimodal ratio of 1:3. Visual tokens get a smaller position step than text tokens (V2PE), so long multimodal inputs fit better in the context. A preference stage, mixed preference optimisation (MPO), combines a DPO-style preference loss, a quality loss and a plain generation loss. At test time, a reward model called VisualPRM can pick the best of several answers. InternVL3-78B scored 72.2 on MMMU and 906 on OCRBench. MPO alone raised its MathVista from 74.0 to 79.0.

InternVL3.5The latest version added mixture-of-experts language models up to Qwen3-235B-A22B and a two-part RL stage called Cascade RL. An offline MPO stage on about 200K preference pairs comes first. An online GSPO stage on about 70K queries follows. A "visual resolution router" decides, tile by tile, whether 256 or 64 tokens are enough. The authors report about 50% fewer visual tokens at nearly unchanged scores. Serving the encoder and the language model on separate machines adds further speed.

Insight

InternVL3.5 reports what RL cost, not only what it gained. On the 8B model, from an SFT score of 53.6 on its reasoning suite, two episodes of online GSPO reached 58.2 at about 11,000 GPU-hours. Cascade RL, a cheap offline MPO stage (about 300 GPU-hours) followed by one GSPO episode, reached 60.3 at about 5,800 GPU-hours. The gain was also larger for small models: +12.2 points at 2B against +6.5 at 241B. In practice: run a cheap offline preference stage before online RL, and expect RL to matter most where the model is smallest.

Molmo and PixMo

Molmo and PixMo (Sep 2024): MolmoE-1B, Molmo-7B-O, Molmo-7B-D, Molmo-72B

By 2024, most strong open VLMs were trained partly on outputs of closed models such as GPT-4V. The Allen Institute for AI asked a different question. How good can an open model get if no VLM is used anywhere in building its data? Molmo is the answer, and PixMo is its data.

DataThe core set, PixMo-Cap, has 712K images with 1.3M long captions, about 196 words on average. Annotators spoke instead of typing. Speaking is faster and gives more detail, and the audio recording proves no VLM wrote the text. PixMo-Points has 2.3M question-point pairs on 223K images. PixMo-Docs renders 255K charts, tables and diagrams from code written by Claude 3.5 Sonnet, with 2.3M questions written by GPT-4o-mini from the code, never from the image. Smaller sets cover clocks (826K rendered), counting and free-form questions. During fine-tuning, each dataset is sampled in proportion to the square root of its size, with pointing up-weighted.

ModelThe encoder is OpenAI CLIP ViT-L/14 at 336 px. The authors say in hindsight they should have used the fully open MetaCLIP. Images are cut into up to 12 overlapping crops plus a low-resolution view. Each crop's 2 × 2 patch windows are pooled by attention into 144 tokens. Features come from two encoder layers, not just the last one. The language models range from OLMoE-1B-7B to Qwen2-72B.

TrainingThere is no connector-only stage. All parameters train on PixMo-Cap for four epochs, then on the fine-tuning mix. One trick saves a lot of compute: all annotations of an image are packed into one sequence with masked attention. That processes two thirds fewer images and more than halves training time. Molmo-72B took about 4,200 H100-hours of pretraining and 8,300 of fine-tuning.

EvaluationMolmo-72B averaged 81.2 over 11 academic benchmarks. In a human preference study with about 325K ratings, it ranked second only to GPT-4o (Elo 1,077 against 1,079). It reads documents well (93.5 DocVQA) and counts well, because it can point first and count the points. It is weaker at MMMU (54.1) and MathVista (58.6). The authors attribute this to a training mix with little reasoning data.

Insight

Molmo's ablations rank design choices by size of effect. Replacing a single crop with overlapping multi-crop raised the 11-benchmark average from 62.8 to 76.9. Swapping CLIP for SigLIP or MetaCLIP moved it by at most 0.3 points. Captions from their own speech pipeline beat ShareGPT4V-style captions from GPT-4V by 17.8 points on a caption-quality score. In practice: how the image is cut and how good the captions are matter far more than which contrastive encoder you start from.

Idefics and SmolVLM

IDEFICS (Aug 2023) → Idefics2 (Apr 2024) → Idefics3 (Aug 2024) → SmolVLM (Nov 2024, report Apr 2025)

Hugging Face's line is valuable less for its scores than for its openness about what did not work. Each report reads like a lab notebook.

IDEFICSThe first model was an open reproduction of DeepMind's Flamingo at 9B and 80B. A perceiver resampler squeezed each image into 64 tokens. Gated cross-attention layers inserted every four language-model layers read those tokens. The backbones stayed frozen during pretraining. The team built OBELICS for it: 141M interleaved web documents with 353M images and 115B text tokens, filtered from 41.2B Common Crawl documents. Training on OBELICS matched LAION-trained performance with about ten times fewer images. The 80B model took 512 A100s for 28 days.

Idefics2The second model came with a paper titled "What matters when building vision-language models?". It ran controlled ablations and then built an 8B model from the winners. SigLIP-SO400M joined Mistral-7B. The image enters the language model as ordinary tokens, not through cross-attention. A perceiver resampler reduces 729 patch tokens to 64. Images keep their aspect ratio up to 980 px. In SFT, an image can be split into four crops plus the whole, for 320 tokens. The SFT data is The Cauldron, 50 vision-language datasets in one format.

Idefics3The third model swapped in Llama 3.1 8B Instruct and dropped the perceiver. Pixel shuffle now gives 169 tokens per 364 px tile, on images up to 1,820 px in the report (the released processor caps the long side at 1,456 px). The team also built Docmatix: 2.4M images and 9.5M question-answer pairs generated by Phi-3-small from 1.3M PDF transcriptions. DocVQA rose from 74.0 to 87.7. Training took five days on 32 H100 nodes, including restarts. The report also found that at least 6.6% of MathVista questions use images that also appear in common SFT sets.

SmolVLMThe small line shrinks the same design to 256M, 500M and 2.2B parameters. Its findings are specific to small models. A large encoder on a tiny language model wastes parameters. More aggressive pixel shuffle (16 times fewer tokens) helps small models. Learned position tokens for tiles beat tile positions written as text. Reusing text SFT data from the language model hurt. The 256M model runs in 0.8 GB of GPU memory.

Idefics2's main ablations are among the most cited in the field. At a fixed parameter count, a better language model mattered more than a better encoder: Mistral-7B beat LLaMA-1-7B by 5.1 points. With frozen backbones, cross-attention won (66.7 against 60.3). Once the backbones were trained with LoRA, the simpler decoder-only design won (69.5 against 67.3).

Nuance

Idefics2 found that 64 visual tokens per image scored as well as 128 on its benchmark average (71.7 against 71.2). One version later, Idefics3 dropped the perceiver because 64 tokens were too few for OCR, and DocVQA rose by 13.7 points. The average had hidden a ceiling that only text-dense tasks hit. In practice: when deciding how many tokens an image gets, look at document and OCR scores on their own, never only at an average.

PaliGemma and Gemma 3

PaliGemma (Jul 2024) → PaliGemma 2 (Dec 2024) → Gemma 3 with vision (Mar 2025)

Google's open line was built with a different goal. PaliGemma is not a chat assistant. It is a base model meant to be fine-tuned for one task at a time.

PaliGemmaA SigLIP So400m encoder joins Gemma-2B through one zero-initialised linear layer. The model uses a "prefix-LM" mask: image tokens and the task prompt attend to each other in both directions, and only the answer is generated left to right. Images are a fixed square of 224, 448 or 896 px, giving 256, 1,024 or 4,096 tokens. Stage 1 trains everything, nothing frozen, on 1B examples at 224 px, about 350B tokens. Stage 2 trains 50M examples at 448 px and 10M at 896 px. The data is multilingual from the start: WebLI captions in over 100 languages, plus detection and segmentation written with 1,024 location tokens and 128 mask tokens. No outputs of commercial VLMs were used. Stage 1 took just under three days on 256 TPUv5e chips.

AblationsFreezing the language model in stage 1 was significantly worse. Freezing the encoder made no difference after transfer but hurt spatial tasks. An MLP connector was no better than a linear one (77.1 against 77.2). A 10 to 30 times shorter stage 1 still did fine on popular benchmarks, but skipping it was the worst choice.

PaliGemma 2The second version crossed three model sizes (3B, 10B, 28B) with three resolutions. That grid separates two effects. Text, document, screen and chart tasks gained mainly from resolution. AI2D, counting and multilingual captioning gained mainly from language-model size. Raising resolution cost about as much compute as raising model size. It also opened new tasks: table structure recognition (98.9 TEDS on FinTabNet), molecule and music-score transcription, and radiology reports.

Gemma 3Vision then moved into a general model. Gemma 3's 4B, 12B and 27B models share a 417M SigLIP encoder at 896 px that stays frozen. Its output is average-pooled to 256 tokens per image. Because the encoder is frozen, its embeddings can be computed once and reused. "Pan & Scan" cuts a large image into crops, but only at inference. Gemma 3 has an RL stage, and the 27B model scores 64.9 on MMMU and 86.6 on DocVQA.

Insight

Gemma 3's report has an unusually clean ablation. It holds the number of image tokens fixed at 256 and changes only the encoder's input resolution. DocVQA rises from 31.9 at 256 px, to 45.4 at 448 px, to 59.8 at 896 px. LLaVA-NeXT's ablations point the same way: when tokens must be cut, keep the high resolution and shrink the tokens. In practice: what documents need is a high-resolution encoding. Pooling that encoding down is much cheaper than encoding a small image.

DeepSeek-VL and DeepSeek-OCR

DeepSeek-VL (Mar 2024) → DeepSeek-VL2 (Dec 2024) → DeepSeek-OCR (Oct 2025)

DeepSeek's three models each answer a different question. How do you add vision without losing language? How do you get a strong VLM at low inference cost? How few tokens does a page of text really need?

DeepSeek-VLThe 7B model used two encoders. SigLIP-L at 384 px gives semantics. SAM-B at 1,024 px gives fine detail, reduced by convolutions to the same 576-token grid. The language model was an intermediate DeepSeek LLM checkpoint, so text pretraining could continue during multimodal training. The joint stage used about 70% text and 30% multimodal data. The authors found that pure multimodal training caused severe language forgetting. They introduced a "modality warm-up": start with 100% text and decay linearly to the target share. Two other findings: scaling the connector-only stage did not help, and batches of a single modality were 20% faster at no cost. Training took 5 days on 512 A100s. The data included 1.4M rendered arXiv papers, e-books and 1.1M plot-to-code pairs from Jupyter notebooks.

DeepSeek-VL2The second generation switched to mixture-of-experts language models: 3B, 16B and 27B total, with 1.0B, 2.8B and 4.5B active. A single SigLIP-SO400M encoder sees up to nine 384 px tiles plus a thumbnail. A 2 × 2 pixel shuffle cuts each tile to 196 tokens. Stage 2 trained about 800B tokens, now with 70% vision-language data and 30% text. The 4.5B-active model scored 93.3 on DocVQA and 95.1 on RefCOCO validation. Training the largest model took 14 days on 336 A100s.

DeepSeek-OCRThe third model is a research probe, not a chat model. Its encoder chains SAM-base with window attention, a 16-times convolutional compressor and a CLIP-large with global attention. A 1,024 px page becomes 256 tokens. The decoder is a 3B mixture of experts with about 570M active parameters. Training used 30M PDF pages in about 100 languages, plus rendered charts, chemical formulas and geometry. The question it answers: how many text tokens can one vision token carry?

Insight

DeepSeek-OCR's results show that the tokens a page needs depend on how much text it holds, not on its pixel count. Slides decoded well with 64 vision tokens. Newspapers, with 4,000 to 5,000 text tokens per page, needed the tiled mode with about 800 tokens. On OmniDocBench, that tiled mode (edit distance 0.127 in English) beat Qwen2.5-VL-72B, which used about 3,949 tokens per page (0.214). In practice: document pipelines should set the token budget by text density, and the right compressor can cut the budget several times over.

MiniCPM-V

MiniCPM-V 1.0 (Feb 2024) → 2.0 (Apr 2024) → Llama3-V 2.5 (May 2024) → 2.6 (Aug 2024) → 4.5 (Sep 2025)

The MiniCPM-V line from OpenBMB and Tsinghua optimises one thing above all: running on a phone. That target shapes every choice.

ModelThe encoder is SigLIP SoViT-400m. Unlike most families after 2024, MiniCPM-V kept a learned-query resampler. One cross-attention layer turns each slice's 1,024 patch features into 64 or 96 tokens. Images are cut into slices by the LLaVA-UHD scheme. The number of slices follows the image area, at most nine, plus the whole image. The grid is chosen to keep each slice's shape close to the encoder's square. MiniCPM-Llama3-V 2.5 spends 96 to 960 tokens on an image. The paper contrasts this with 1,728 to 2,880 for LLaVA-NeXT on the same Llama-3-8B.

Data and trainingPretraining uses 410M English and 110M Chinese captions plus OCR data, in three stages. The language model stays frozen while the encoder adapts to high resolution, so that noisy pretraining data cannot damage it. SFT comes in two parts, short answers first, then long ones. The alignment stage, RLAIF-V, is a DPO variant with AI feedback. The model samples 10 answers. Llama-3-8B splits each answer into atomic claims. An open VLM checks each claim as a yes-or-no question, and an answer's score is minus the number of rejected claims. Only 6K pairs from 3K images were used. Hallucination on Object HalBench fell to 10.3% of responses, below GPT-4V's 13.6%.

DeploymentThe paper documents the work of fitting an 8B VLM on a Snapdragon phone. 4-bit weights cut memory from 16 GB to about 5 GB. Compiler and configuration work raised decoding from 1.3 to 8.2 tokens per second. Running the encoder on the phone's NPU cut image encoding from 3.7 to 1.3 seconds.

MiniCPM-V 4.5The 2025 model adds a 3D resampler for video. Frames are grouped into packages, and six 448 px frames become just 64 tokens, a 96-times compression. It learns OCR in an unusual way. Text regions in document images are corrupted at random levels and the model must reproduce the original text. Lightly corrupted text teaches reading. Fully masked text teaches inferring from context. A hybrid RL stage mixes short and long reasoning rollouts and reached the long-only score using 70.5% of the tokens. The 8B model scores 94.7 on DocVQA and 890 on OCRBench.

Insight

MiniCPM-Llama3-V 2.5 was pretrained only on English and Chinese. Adding 90K multilingual SFT samples, under 0.5% of the SFT data, lifted its multilingual LLaVA Bench score in German from 22.8 to 76.5 and in Japanese from 13.8 to 88.0. The vision side was already language-neutral; the language model already knew those languages. In practice: a thin multilingual SFT layer can unlock conversation in many languages. Note the limit: this is a chat benchmark. Reading text written in those scripts still needs OCR data in those scripts.

Apple MM1 and MM1.5

MM1 (Mar 2024) → MM1.5 (Sep 2024)

Apple's MM1 weights were not released. The family belongs here anyway, because its two papers are among the most systematic ablation studies of VLM pretraining and fine-tuning.

MM1The team ran its ablations on a small base model and then trained 3B, 7B and 30B dense models and two mixture-of-experts models. The encoder was a ViT-H CLIP trained on DFN-5B at 378 px. A C-Abstractor connector gave 144 tokens. Everything trained from the start, on about 400B tokens: 45% interleaved documents, 45% image-caption pairs and 10% text. The findings, in the authors' order: image resolution has the highest impact on the encoder side, then encoder size, then encoder pretraining data. Connector type matters little; the number of tokens and the resolution matter most. Interleaved data drives few-shot and text performance. Caption data drives zero-shot performance. The paper also fits a learning-rate rule from model size: η = exp(−0.4214 ln N − 0.5535).

MM1.5The follow-up focused on fine-tuning and text-rich images. A new continual-pretraining stage used 45M OCR samples at 1,344 px. Images are split into 4 to 9 sub-images of 144 tokens each. The pretraining mix changed to 50% captions, 10% interleaved and 40% text. SFT ablations showed that text-rich data helped knowledge benchmarks too, and that grounding data added a capability at a slight cost elsewhere. Training with up to 16 sub-images instead of 4 raised DocVQA by 6.3 points on the 7B model. The 30B model reached 91.4 on DocVQA.

Nuance

MM1 picked its 45:45:10 mix using few-shot scores right after pretraining. MM1.5 re-chose the mix by scores after fine-tuning and cut interleaved data from 45% to 10%. The Idefics3 report makes the same warning: pretraining ablations without instruction data can mislead. A related one is MM1.5's finding that grounding broke (74.0 to 24.5) when the minimum number of sub-images was changed at inference. In practice: judge data and preprocessing choices on the model as it will be used, after SFT and with the inference settings fixed.

NVIDIA NVLM and Eagle

Eagle (Aug 2024) → NVLM 1.0 (Sep 2024) → Eagle 2 (Jan 2025) → Eagle 2.5 (Apr 2025)

NVIDIA's two lines asked two architecture questions under controlled conditions.

NVLMNVLM trained three 72B designs on the same data with the same frozen InternViT-6B encoder and Qwen2-72B. NVLM-D feeds visual tokens to the decoder. NVLM-X reads them through gated cross-attention. NVLM-H puts the thumbnail in the decoder and the tiles in cross-attention. The cross-attention design trained fastest, 50.6 samples per second against 28.8 for the decoder design. The decoder design read documents best: 92.6 on DocVQA against 82.9. Two further findings stand out. First, quality and task diversity mattered more than scale even in pretraining; large noisy sets and interleaved web data had little effect. Second, the team curated a text-only SFT set. NVLM-D's average on MMLU, GSM8K, MATH and HumanEval rose 4.3 points above its own Qwen2-72B backbone. By the same measure, InternVL2-76B fell 6.7 points and LLaVA-OneVision-72B fell 6.3.

EagleEagle studied mixtures of vision encoders. Five encoders (CLIP, ConvNeXt, SAM, Pix2Struct, EVA-02) were each aligned to a frozen LLM first, then joined. Concatenating their features along the channel dimension matched placing them one after another in the sequence, at half the tokens. Eagle 2 turned to data. From over 180 sources it deduplicated, filtered, selected by clustering and augmented. Each step was ablated, and the 13-benchmark average climbed from 67.0 to 73.5. Selection cut the SFT set from 8.2M to 4.6M samples and raised the score. Eagle 2.5 extended the context to 128K for long video, with progressive context stages and a new long-video set of 110K samples.

Insight

NVLM tested how to tell the model where each tile came from. With dynamic tiles and no tags, DocVQA was 80.2. Adding a one-dimensional text tag such as <tile_1> before each tile raised it to 87.4 and OCRBench from 728 to 806, with the same visual tokens. SmolVLM found a twist for small models: tile positions written as text strings caused an "OCR loss plague", and learned position tokens fixed it. In practice: tiles need explicit position markers, and small models need them as dedicated tokens rather than text.

Meta Llama vision

Llama 3 vision experiments (Jul 2024) → Llama 3.2 Vision (Sep 2024) → Llama 4 (Apr 2025)

Meta's two releases took opposite architectural positions, which makes them useful to compare.

Llama 3.2 VisionThe Llama 3 paper describes a compositional design. A ViT-H/14 encoder (630M parameters, trained on 2.5B pairs) gets 8 extra gated layers, about 850M in total. Cross-attention layers after every fourth language-model layer read the image features. The language model stays frozen through every stage. The goal, in Meta's words, was a "drop-in replacement for Llama 3.1": text behaviour unchanged. Pretraining used about 6B image-text pairs, filtered for English and CLIP score, deduplicated and rebalanced. An annealing stage used about 500M higher-quality examples. Post-training added SFT, rejection sampling and DPO. The released 11B and 90B models took 2.02M H100-hours in total. Image tasks are supported in English only. The 90B model scores 60.3 on MMMU and 90.1 on DocVQA.

Llama 4Seven months later, Llama 4 Scout (109B total, 17B active) and Maverick (400B total, 17B active) used early fusion. Visual tokens enter the mixture-of-experts backbone directly. The MetaCLIP-based encoder was trained separately against a frozen Llama. Pretraining covered more than 30T tokens of text, images and video, and up to 48 images per sequence. Post-training was a light SFT, then online RL, then light DPO, after removing more than half of the data that Llama models judged "easy". Maverick scores 73.4 on MMMU and 94.4 on DocVQA. The two models took 7.38M H100-hours, most of it for the whole language model, not vision.

Nuance

Cross-attention with a frozen language model is not simply worse. It guarantees that text behaviour does not change, and NVLM measured it as the fastest design to train. But the evidence on quality points one way. Idefics2 found cross-attention best only while backbones are frozen. NVLM found the decoder design much better on documents. Meta itself moved from cross-attention to early fusion. In practice: choose cross-attention when the text model must stay exactly as it is; otherwise put the image tokens in the decoder.

Other families and two design studies

Several more families each contribute one idea worth knowing. They are shorter here because their reports say less about data or ablations, not because they matter less.

PixtralMistral's Pixtral 12B (Sep 2024) trained a 400M encoder from scratch for native resolution. It uses 2D rotary positions and a break token after each row of patches, so the language model knows where rows end. The report's other contribution is evaluation. It showed that explicit answer-format prompts raise scores, and that lenient parsing inflates some models. It introduced MM-MT-Bench, a multi-turn benchmark that correlates at 0.91 with human Elo ratings. Pixtral Large (124B) followed in November 2024. Neither report gives data or compute.

PhiMicrosoft's Phi-3.5-vision (4.2B) pairs a CLIP ViT-L with Phi-3.5-mini. It was pretrained on 0.5T tokens and trained on 256 A100s for 6 days. Phi-4-multimodal (5.6B) keeps the Phi-4-Mini language model fully frozen and adds a 370M vision LoRA and a separate speech LoRA. Text performance is unchanged by construction. It scores 93.2 on DocVQA and 844 on OCRBench. Its vision side is English only.

AriaRhymes AI's Aria (Oct 2024) is a mixture of experts trained for multimodality from the start. It has 24.9B parameters, 3.5B active per text token and 3.9B per visual token. Multimodal pretraining used 400B multimodal tokens alongside 1T text tokens. The authors found experts that specialise in visual inputs in most layers.

OvisAlibaba's Ovis replaces the continuous connector with a "visual embedding table". Each patch becomes a probability distribution over a 131,072-entry visual vocabulary, and its embedding is the weighted sum of table rows, just as text tokens look up a table. Against an MLP connector with everything else equal, MMMU rose from 34.8 to 39.7 and MathVista from 36.3 to 41.4. Ovis2.5 (Aug 2025) adds a native-resolution encoder, a thinking mode, DPO and GRPO. The 9B model scores 71.2 on MMMU.

Kimi-VLMoonshot's Kimi-VL (Apr 2025) pairs a 400M native-resolution encoder (MoonViT) with a 16B mixture-of-experts model that has 2.8B active parameters. All parameters train with the Muon optimiser. Joint pretraining starts text-only, and the multimodal share then rises to about 40%. The total after the language model's own pretraining is 4.4T tokens. The thinking variant adds long chain-of-thought SFT and RL. Its MathVision score rises from 18.7% with 1K thinking tokens to 36.8% with 16K.

GLM-VZhipu's GLM-4.1V-Thinking (9B, Jul 2025) and GLM-4.5V (106B total, 12B active, Aug 2025) are built around RL. Pretraining data was filtered hard: over 10B raw pairs, a CLIP-score threshold of 0.3, concept-balanced resampling and factual recaptioning. A long chain-of-thought cold start precedes "RL with curriculum sampling", which down-samples prompts that are too easy or too hard. Each domain has its own answer checker. GLM-4.1V-9B beats Qwen2.5-VL-72B on 29 of 42 benchmarks; GLM-4.5V scores 75.4 on MMMU.

Insight

GLM's RL study isolates cross-domain effects. RL on GUI-agent data alone improved every other domain, including STEM and grounding. RL on grounding alone raised grounding by 30.4 points but lowered OCR and charts by 4.6. One weak answer checker, for multi-image questions, collapsed training across all domains at once. In practice: in multi-domain RL, every domain's reward checker is a single point of failure for the whole run. Audit each checker before mixing domains.

Two design-space studies: Prismatic and Cambrian-1

Two academic projects trained families of models mainly to answer design questions.

Prismatic (Stanford and Toyota Research, Feb 2024) swept the design space with significance tests on a fixed 12-benchmark suite. A single training stage was as good as two and saved 20 to 25% of compute. Fine-tuning the encoder hurt, especially localisation. Fusing DINOv2 and SigLIP features helped localisation by 5 to 10%. A base language model was as good as an instruction-tuned one. The resulting Prism 7B trains in under 9 hours on 8 A100s.

Cambrian-1 (NYU, Jun 2024) tested over 20 vision encoders and introduced CV-Bench, a vision-centric benchmark. It found that several popular benchmarks barely need the image: MMMU, MathVista, AI2D and ScienceQA showed under 5% difference with vision turned off. Its connector, the Spatial Vision Aggregator, fuses four encoders into 576 tokens with local cross-attention. It also showed that capping each data source at about 250K to 350K samples, and curating 10M samples down to 7M, raised scores.

Nuance

Prismatic and Cambrian-1 disagree on two rules. Prismatic found one stage enough and encoder fine-tuning harmful. Cambrian-1 found two stages with more connector data better and unfreezing helpful. Their setups differ: Prismatic trained on LLaVA-scale data, while Cambrian varied adapter data up to 1.2M samples and used 737K to 7M SFT samples. Later families resolved the encoder question with a 5 to 10 times lower encoder learning rate. In practice: "freeze the encoder" and "skip alignment" are rules about small data and full learning rates, not universal laws.

How the families handle images

Now we can answer the checklist question by question, across families. This section covers the model side: the connector, preprocessing, multiple images and video, token compression, the parameter split and the new architectural ideas.

The connector

Four patterns cover almost every family.

  • A linear layer or small MLP maps each patch feature into the language model's embedding space. LLaVA, PaliGemma, Qwen2-VL and later, InternVL 1.5 and later, DeepSeek-VL2, NVLM-D, Pixtral and Kimi-VL use it. It is usually paired with a 2 × 2 merge or pixel shuffle.
  • A learned-query resampler uses a fixed set of queries that cross-attend to the patches. Qwen-VL (256 queries), IDEFICS and Idefics2 (64), MiniCPM-V (64 to 96), Aria and InternVL 1's QLLaMA use it.
  • Cross-attention inside the language model keeps the image out of the token sequence. IDEFICS, Llama 3.2 Vision and NVLM-X use it.
  • Early fusion trains the backbone on image and text tokens together from the start. Llama 4 is the open example.

A few families tried something else. Cambrian-1's aggregator fuses four encoders. Ovis looks up visual words in an embedding table. Qwen3-VL's DeepStack adds features from several encoder depths. Phi-4-multimodal adds LoRA adapters to a frozen model.

The evidence on connector choice is consistent. MM1 found connector type mattered little next to resolution and token count. PaliGemma found an MLP no better than a linear layer. Learned-query resamplers lost out on documents. Cambrian-1 measured 27.1 on its OCR and chart group with a resampler against 55.5 with its aggregator. NVLM chose not to use a perceiver because it could not even overfit 30K OCR samples. Idefics3 dropped its perceiver for the same reason.

Nuance

MiniCPM-V kept a resampler and still reached 890 on OCRBench in version 4.5, at 64 tokens per 448 px slice. So the resampler penalty is not absolute. One hypothesis, not tested in any of these reports, is that it reflects training: the failing resamplers were trained with frozen encoders or modest OCR data, while MiniCPM-V trains on large OCR sets with the encoder unfrozen. In practice: if you need very few tokens, a resampler can work, but budget for heavy OCR training.

Vision preprocessing: fixed, tiled or native

Every family had to decide how an image of arbitrary size becomes a fixed-size input. There are three answers. A fixed square resize is simple but squashes tall pages and loses detail. Tiling cuts a large image into encoder-sized crops plus a thumbnail. Native resolution feeds the encoder the image at its own size and lets the token count vary. Figure 6 lets you compare what each scheme spends on the same image.

The evidence on preprocessing is the strongest in this survey. Qwen2-VL's native resolution beat a fixed budget with more tokens. InternVL 1.5 showed more tiles helping OCR tasks. MM1.5 showed more sub-images helping DocVQA and InfoVQA. Molmo's overlapping crops beat a single crop by 14 points. Gemma 3's Pan & Scan raised InfoVQA by 17.0 points at 27B, only by cropping at inference. There are costs, though. More tiles slightly hurt MMMU and AI2D in InternVL 1.5. Native resolution needs activation memory that grows with image size; Qwen2.5-VL answered with window attention, and DeepSeek-OCR's authors cite it as a reason to compress early.

Multiple images and video

Families handle several images in three ways. Most simply place the images' tokens one after another with markers. Some share a fixed budget: InternVL 2.5 divides its tile budget across the images, and DeepSeek-VL2 stops tiling when there are more than two images. LLaVA-OneVision sets similar token totals for one image, several images and video, so skills transfer between them.

Video adds time. Qwen2-VL samples 2 frames per second and folds pairs of frames into 3D patches. Qwen2.5-VL samples at a dynamic rate and ties positions to real time. Qwen3-VL writes timestamps into the text instead. MiniCPM-V 4.5 compresses six frames into 64 tokens with a 3D resampler. Eagle 2.5 and Qwen3-VL train with contexts of 128K and 256K tokens to hold long videos. Seeing in Time covers these methods in depth.

Token compression

The default is a fourfold cut: a 2 × 2 merge or pixel shuffle. Qwen2-VL and later, InternVL 1.5 and later, DeepSeek-VL2, Idefics3, Kimi-VL and Llama 4 all use it. Some go further. Gemma 3 pools 16 times to a fixed 256 tokens. SmolVLM uses 9 to 16 times on small models. MiniCPM-V's resampler gives 64 tokens per slice. DeepSeek-OCR compresses 16 times inside the encoder. And InternVL3.5's router chooses 4 times or 16 times per tile. The Cost of a Picture covers compression and serving in depth.

Parameter split and the choice of encoder and decoder

Figure 7 shows how parameters divide between the vision encoder, the connector and the language model.

Two patterns stand out. Most families settled on a vision encoder of 0.3B to 0.7B parameters while their language models grew from 7B to 235B. The vision share of total parameters fell from about 20% for Qwen-VL in 2023 to between 1% and 3% for the largest recent models, and under 1% for Qwen2-VL-72B, Molmo-72B and Pixtral Large. InternVL is the exception among large models: it keeps a 6B encoder for its largest ones. Small models go the other way. SmolVLM-256M spends 41% of its parameters on vision by the report's component sizes (34% when counted from the released checkpoint), and its report warns that a larger encoder on a tiny language model wastes parameters.

The encoders themselves converged too. SigLIP So400m, or a model initialised from it, appears in LLaVA-OneVision, Idefics2 and 3, PaliGemma, Gemma 3, DeepSeek-VL2, MiniCPM-V, Eagle 2, Aria, Kimi-VL, Phi-4-multimodal and Qwen3-VL. The usual reasons given are strong results for its size (LLaVA-NeXT, Idefics2, Cambrian-1) and a convenient 384 px input. Teams that trained their own encoder from scratch (Qwen2.5-VL, Pixtral) did it for native resolution.

Nuance

Does a bigger encoder help? The evidence splits. Idefics2 found the language model mattered more than the encoder at fixed size. MM1 found doubling encoder size usually gave under 1%. LLaVA-NeXT found language-model scaling more useful. SmolVLM found a big encoder wasteful on a tiny language model. On the other side, InternVL 2.5 argues its 6B encoder let the 78B model beat Qwen2-VL-72B with a tenth of the training tokens. That comparison changes the data and the language model too, so it does not isolate the encoder. And InternVL3.5 itself uses the 300M encoder for every model up to 30B-A3B. In practice: spend parameters on the language model and on resolution first.

Architectural innovations and new training tasks

Beyond the main choices, each family added smaller ideas that others then copied or tested.

  • Positions. 2D rotary positions in the encoder (Qwen2-VL, Pixtral, GLM-V, Kimi-VL). Multimodal rotary positions with time, height and width (Qwen2-VL, refined in Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen3-VL). Smaller position steps for visual tokens (InternVL3's V2PE). Tile tags (NVLM) and row-break tokens (Pixtral, Idefics3).
  • Features. Features from several encoder layers (Molmo, Llama 3 vision, Qwen3-VL's DeepStack). Two encoders for semantics and detail (DeepSeek-VL, Eagle, Cambrian-1).
  • Masks and losses. A prefix-LM mask (PaliGemma). Loss averaged by the square root of response length (InternVL 2.5, Qwen3-VL). Answers that keep both short and long reasoning modes (MiniCPM-V 4.5, GLM-4.5V, Qwen3-VL).
  • New output tasks. Grounding as text coordinates (Qwen-VL onward). Pointing with points instead of boxes (Molmo, then used in Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen3-VL). Location and segmentation tokens (PaliGemma). Document parsing into HTML with a box on every element (Qwen2.5-VL). Reading text from deliberately corrupted documents (MiniCPM-V 4.5). Decoding a page from very few vision tokens (DeepSeek-OCR).

How the families were trained

This section covers the data and training side: where data came from, how much went into each stage, the RL stages, the ablations that taught the most, the recurring tips and the languages covered.

Data engines and synthetic data

Five kinds of data engine recur.

  • A text-only language model writes from annotations. LLaVA wrote conversations from captions and boxes. PixMo-Docs wrote questions from rendering code. Docmatix wrote questions from PDF transcriptions. Cambrian-1 wrote science questions from Wikipedia.
  • Rendering. Text, documents and charts are drawn from known sources, so the label is exact. Qwen-VL rendered PDFs and HTML pages. DeepSeek-VL rendered arXiv papers. Qwen2.5-VL drew 1M charts. GLM-V collected 220M OCR images. DeepSeek-OCR rendered charts, chemical formulas and geometry.
  • Recaptioning with a VLM. A strong VLM rewrites weak web captions. LLaVA-NeXT and OneVision recaptioned 3.5M images. DeepSeek-VL2, GLM-V and Qwen3-VL each built a recaptioner.
  • Human engines. PixMo used spoken captions and clicked points. Llama 3 used human preference ratings on a 7-point scale.
  • AI feedback for preferences. RLAIF-V checks claims one by one. InternVL's MMPR pipeline builds rejected answers by letting the model finish a truncated answer without the image.

Filtering is as important as generation. Qwen-VL kept 28% of its raw pairs. InternVL 2.5 removed repetitive samples. Eagle 2 deduplicated across 180 sources and selected by clustering. Cambrian-1 capped each source. GLM-V balanced concepts the way MetaCLIP does. Qwen3-VL dropped math questions that a text model could solve without the image. Decontamination against benchmarks appears in NVLM, PaliGemma and Idefics3.

Data scale per stage and mixing

Figure 8 lays out the stages of each family's training, with what was trained and on how much data.

Three patterns emerge. First, the connector-only stage is small everywhere, and several studies found it barely matters. DeepSeek-VL found that scaling it did not help. Prismatic and Molmo skipped it. Second, the bulk of compute goes either into full-model multimodal pretraining or into very large SFT sets. Qwen2.5-VL pretrained on 4.1T tokens. InternVL 2.5 used about 120B tokens in total but 16.3M SFT samples. Third, everyone now mixes text-only data into multimodal training. The share varies widely: 70% in DeepSeek-VL pretraining, 40% in MM1.5 pretraining, 50% in Qwen2.5-VL's SFT, a third in Qwen3-VL's SFT, about 25% in InternVL3 pretraining and over 20% in Eagle 2.

Several mixing findings carry over between families.

  • Interleaved documents help few-shot and text skills, while captions help zero-shot skills (MM1).
  • Start with text and phase in multimodal data, so language is not damaged early (DeepSeek-VL's modality warm-up, Kimi-VL's rising share).
  • Sample datasets in proportion to the square root of their size, and cap the largest sources (Molmo, Cambrian-1).
  • Use format prompts or style tags so short academic answers do not leak into chat (LLaVA-1.5, Molmo, Cambrian-1).
  • Balance token budgets across single images, multiple images and video (LLaVA-OneVision).
  • On small models, reusing the language model's own SFT text hurt (SmolVLM).

RL and preference stages

Before 2025, few open VLMs had a preference stage. MiniCPM-V used RLAIF-V, Llama 3.2 used DPO with rejection sampling, Phi-3.5-vision used DPO and LLaVA-NeXT-Video had a DPO variant. In 2025, nearly every new family added one. Qwen2.5-VL used DPO. InternVL3 used MPO and InternVL3.5 used Cascade RL. Kimi-VL-Thinking used online policy mirror descent. GLM-V used GRPO with curriculum sampling. MiniCPM-V 4.5 used GRPO with mixed short and long rollouts. Ovis2.5 used DPO then GRPO. Qwen3-VL used SAPO plus a general RL stage. Llama 4 used online RL between two light SFT and DPO stages. Gemma 3 used reward-model-based RL methods (BOND, WARM, WARP).

Three findings recur. RL gains are largest on reasoning benchmarks and on small models (InternVL3.5). A model's pass@k before RL sets an upper bound on what RL can reach (GLM-V). And a thinking mode can cost perception: Qwen3-VL's Thinking model scores 875 on OCRBench against 920 for the Instruct model, while gaining on MMMU and MathVista. Rewarding What the Model Sees and Thinking With Images cover these methods.

Training tips that recur

  • Train the encoder, gently. Use a learning rate 5 to 10 times lower than the LLM's (LLaVA-NeXT and OneVision), or a 0.1 multiplier (DeepSeek-VL2).
  • Pack sequences. Packing gave 2 to 3 times speed-ups in MiniCPM-V and Eagle 2. Molmo packs all annotations of one image into one sequence.
  • Group batches by modality. LLaVA-1.5 reported 25% faster training and DeepSeek-VL 20%, at no cost.
  • Normalise the loss over response length. InternVL 2.5 weights tokens by one over the square root of response length, and Qwen3-VL uses a square-root-normalised per-token loss. Both aim to stop long or short answers from dominating.
  • Remove degenerate data. Filter repetitive outputs before they teach loops (InternVL 2.5).
  • Tell the model the format. Use format prompts, style tags or length hints (LLaVA-1.5, Molmo).
  • Average checkpoints. Llama 3's vision models averaged the top models from hyperparameter sweeps.
  • Scale the learning rate with size. MM1 fits the learning rate as a function of parameter count.

Languages

Language coverage splits the families into three groups. English-only on the image side: LLaVA (by data), IDEFICS, Idefics2 and 3, Prismatic, Llama 3.2 Vision ("image+text: English only"), Phi-3.5-vision ("primarily English") and Phi-4-multimodal (vision English only). English and Chinese: Qwen-VL, InternVL, DeepSeek-VL, Kimi-VL and GLM-V, each with some extra languages. Broadly multilingual: PaliGemma (WebLI in over 100 languages), Qwen2-VL and later (OCR in 10 extra languages, then 39), MiniCPM-V (30+ from a thin SFT layer), DeepSeek-OCR (about 100 languages for PDFs) and Llama 4 (12 supported languages). Many reports give no language list at all.

What correlates with capability

We can now ask the question the checklist builds toward. Which design and data choices go with which capabilities? There are two kinds of evidence. Within one paper, a team changes one thing and measures the effect. Across papers, we line up many models and look for patterns. The first kind is strong. The second is suggestive at best, because every model differs in many ways at once.

Figure 9 collects the within-paper evidence. Each row is one controlled change from one report, drawn from its "without" score to its "with" score.

Figure 10 shows the cross-family view. Pick a design variable for the horizontal axis and a benchmark for the vertical axis.

Here is what the two kinds of evidence support, from strongest to weakest.

StrongResolution handling and document scores. Five independent papers changed resolution or tiling and measured large gains on document tasks: PaliGemma, Gemma 3, Idefics2, NVLM and Qwen2-VL. Across families, every model at or above 90 on DocVQA uses tiling or native resolution. Fixed-size models reach the mid-80s only with a large input: Gemma 3 at 896 px with Pan & Scan crops (86.6) and PaliGemma at 896 px after task fine-tuning (84.8). Those with 576 tokens or fewer from inputs of 448 px or less sit below 80 where reported.

StrongPreference and RL stages and math reasoning. InternVL3's MPO raised MathVista from 74.0 to 79.0 at 78B. InternVL3.5's Cascade RL added 6.7 points to the 8B reasoning suite. GLM-V reports RL gains of up to 10.6%. Kimi-VL's thinking variant beats its instruct variant on MathVista. The same evidence shows the cost: thinking can lower OCR scores (Qwen3-VL).

StrongGrounding and pointing data and localisation. Qwen-VL trained on millions of grounding samples and reached 89.4 on RefCOCO validation in 2023. LLaVA-1.5, with 48K RefCOCO samples, scored 55.1 in Prismatic's evaluation. MM1.5 added grounding data and gained the capability. Molmo's point-then-count beat counting directly on both counting benchmarks. GLM-V's grounding-only RL raised grounding by 30.4 points.

ModerateMultilingual data and multilingual skill. MiniCPM-V's 90K-sample experiment is controlled and large. The cross-family pattern fits it: families trained on English data report English-only image support. But few reports evaluate many languages, so the evidence is thin beyond chat.

ModerateData curation and broad scores. Eagle 2's ablation trail, Cambrian-1's 7M-over-10M result, InternVL 2.5's filtering and Molmo's caption ablation all show curation helping. The effects are a few points each, not tens.

HypothesisLanguage-model size and MMMU. Across families, MMMU tracks the language model more than anything visual. Cambrian-1 showed MMMU barely needs the image. The LLaVA-NeXT team found multimodal scores tracked the language model's MMLU. PaliGemma 2 found knowledge tasks gained from model size. But release date, data and RL all rise with model size in this sample, so the cross-family slope overstates the effect.

Insight

Each capability has its own driver, and they barely overlap. Document reading follows resolution and text-rich data. Localisation follows grounding and pointing data. Math reasoning follows the language model and RL. Knowledge benchmarks such as MMMU follow the language model. Connector type and contrastive encoder family track none of them strongly. In practice: decide which capability you need, then spend on its driver. A model can be excellent at one and mediocre at another; Molmo is near the top on documents and counting but well behind on MMMU and MathVista.

Compute, tokens and time

How Vision-Language Models Scale covers scaling laws for VLMs. The family reports contain few laws of their own. MM1 fits a learning-rate rule as a function of parameter count. PaliGemma 2 shows that raising resolution costs about as much as raising model size. Qwen2-VL notes that math improves with model size while OCR is already strong at small sizes. What the reports do give, sometimes, is compute and data scale. Figure 11 collects every figure I could verify.

The spread is enormous. Prism 7B trains in under 72 A100-hours. LLaVA-1.5-13B took about 208. Molmo-72B took about 12,500 H100-hours. DeepSeek-VL2's largest model took about 113,000 A100-hours. Llama 3.2's two vision models took 2.02M H100-hours. Data scale spans a similar range: LLaVA-1.5 trained on 1.2M samples, InternVL2.5-78B on about 120B tokens, Qwen2.5-VL on 4.1T tokens and Kimi-VL on 4.4T.

Capability does not follow this axis closely. Molmo-72B, at about 12,500 H100-hours, beats Llama 3.2 90B on DocVQA (93.5 against 90.1), at well under 1% of the compute. InternVL2.5-78B used about a tenth of Qwen2-VL-72B's tokens and scored higher on MMMU. Within a family, scale helps reliably. Across families, data targeting and resolution explain more than compute.

Nuance

These compute numbers measure different things. Llama 4's 7.38M H100-hours include pretraining the whole language model on over 30T tokens. Phi-4-multimodal's 28 days on 512 A100s include speech. LLaVA's numbers count only VLM training on top of a finished language model. Most families (Qwen, InternVL, Kimi, MM1, NVLM, Eagle) report no total at all. In practice: compare compute only within one report, or after checking that both numbers cover the same stages.

What the reports leave out

Surveying these families along one checklist exposes the same gaps again and again.

  • Compute. Most reports from the largest teams give no GPU-hours. Without them, "data efficiency" claims cannot be checked.
  • Mixtures in tokens. Many reports give sample counts per source, but loss is computed per token. A one-word answer and a 200-word caption count the same as samples and very differently as tokens.
  • Contamination. Idefics3 found at least 6.6% of MathVista questions use images from common SFT sets. Few reports check this.
  • Evaluation settings. Chain-of-thought or not, prompt wording, parsing rules and toolkit all change scores. Pixtral's report measured this. Most reports do not state all of them.
  • Negative results. Idefics, Prismatic, MM1, Molmo and InternVL3.5 report what did not work or what it cost. They are the most useful reports in this survey for that reason.

Key insights and nuances

  • Families fixed things in the same order: resolution, then data curation, then RL. RL cannot repair weak perception. (Three years of open VLMs)
  • Format and task data moved early models most. In LLaVA-1.5, VQAv2 with a format prompt moved MME by 514 points; the MLP connector by 31. (LLaVA)
  • Cutting the image well beats choosing the encoder. Molmo's overlapping crops added 14 points; swapping contrastive encoders moved under half a point. (Molmo and PixMo)
  • Encode at high resolution, then pool. Gemma 3 at a fixed 256 tokens nearly doubled DocVQA by raising encoder input from 256 to 896 px. (PaliGemma and Gemma 3)
  • Averages hide token ceilings. Idefics2's 64 tokens looked enough on average; Idefics3 gained 13.7 DocVQA points by removing that limit. (Idefics and SmolVLM)
  • A page needs tokens in proportion to its text. DeepSeek-OCR decodes about 10 text tokens per vision token at about 97% precision, falling to about 60% at 20. (DeepSeek-VL and DeepSeek-OCR)
  • Tag your tiles. One text tag per tile raised NVLM's DocVQA from 80.2 to 87.4 at the same token count. (NVIDIA NVLM and Eagle)
  • Offline preference before online RL is cheaper and better. InternVL3.5 got more gain at about half the GPU-hours. (InternVL)
  • In multi-domain RL, one bad reward checker can sink every domain. (GLM-V)
  • Choose mixtures after SFT, not before. MM1.5 cut interleaved data from 45% to 10% once it judged by post-SFT scores. (Apple MM1 and MM1.5)
  • Each capability has its own driver. Documents follow resolution, localisation follows grounding data, math follows the LLM and RL. Connector type and encoder family follow none strongly. (What correlates with capability)
  • Compute does not rank capability across families. Molmo-72B beat Llama 3.2 90B on DocVQA at under 1% of the GPU-hours. (Compute, tokens and time)

With these in hand, you should be able to read a new VLM report and place it quickly. Ask how it handles resolution, what its data engines are, what it trains in each stage and on how much, whether it has an RL stage, and which capability each choice serves. Where the report does not say, note that too. This post closes the series; the posts linked below go deeper on each axis.
