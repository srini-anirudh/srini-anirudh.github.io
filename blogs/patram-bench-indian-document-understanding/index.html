Overview

Most of what we know about how well vision-language models read documents comes from English pages. This post asks a narrower question: how well do they read the documents people in India actually handle?

Think of a ration card form printed in Hindi and English. A Telugu newspaper with six stories on one page. A Bengali bank statement. A government gazette scanned decades ago, with a seal printed over the text. A question about any of these might arrive in Hindi, in English, in Hindi typed with Latin letters, or in a sentence that switches between two languages halfway through.

The benchmarks the field reports on rarely contain such pages. DocVQA, the dataset that made document question answering a standard task, is built on English industry documents. Multilingual benchmarks exist, but the ones that include Indian languages cover one or a few tasks. So a model can top every leaderboard and still fail on a Marathi application form, and nobody would know.

Patram-Bench is our attempt to measure this. It is a benchmark for Indian document understanding, published as a poster at ICDAR 2026. Its scale:

52,607documents
23languages
13domains
71document classes
52tasks
2.4M+QA annotations
29VLMs evaluated

The tasks run from perception (reading text, finding regions, converting a page to Markdown or HTML) to reasoning (multi-hop questions, arithmetic, questions asked across languages). Two new metrics measure how much a model keeps when the language changes between document and question, and how much it keeps when a question is code-mixed or transliterated.

The post follows the paper's logic. It starts with why Indian documents are hard for a model, and what existing benchmarks cover. It then builds the benchmark one decision at a time: the three axes, the tasks, how the data was collected and checked, and how answers are scored. The second half reads the results by task, by language, and by whether the model looked at the page at all.

Five findings stand out. Each is developed later in the post.

  • No single model is best. Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5 and Claude 4 Sonnet each lead different task families, and on layout, specialist detectors beat every general model (results across tasks).
  • The low-resource cliff is steep and uneven. Every plotted model loses about 40% of its English score on Sindhi, while Kashmiri, also low-resource, costs far less (results across languages).
  • The document's language matters more than the question's. Asking about an English page in an Indic language barely changes accuracy. Moving the page itself into an Indic language costs 31% to 61% (cross-lingual questions).
  • Code-mixed questions break almost every model. Twelve of thirteen open models score 0.10 to 0.21 on code-mixed input, against 0.52 to 0.57 on the same content in one language (code-mixed questions).
  • The largest open models read the page least. With the image removed, Qwen3-VL-235B and InternVL3.5-241B still give plausible answers. The most image-dependent open models are mid-sized (do models read the page?).

Why Indian documents are hard to read

Start with a single word. In English, the letters of a printed word sit side by side with small gaps between them. A model that has seen enough English learns where one letter ends and the next begins. Most Indian scripts break this assumption in at least one way. Figure 1 walks through six of them, from the shape of one word to the condition of a whole page.

Connected headlines. Devanagari, the script of Hindi, Marathi, Nepali and several other languages, joins the letters of a word along a horizontal bar called the shirorekha. Inside a word there are no gaps. A system that finds characters by looking for white space between them sees one long shape.

Stacked conjuncts. Brahmic scripts, such as Bengali, combine consonants into a single shape. The Bengali ক্ষ is one visual unit but three Unicode code points: two consonants and a joiner. A model must learn that one picture maps to three characters.

Visual order differs from stored order. In Hindi कि (ki), the vowel sign is drawn to the left of the consonant, but it is stored after it. Some Bengali vowel signs are split into two parts on both sides of the consonant. Reading left to right and writing what you see produces the wrong string.

Several scripts on one page. A government circular can carry Devanagari, Latin, Tamil and Perso-Arabic text side by side. The model has to switch scripts mid-page, without being told.

Mixed and romanised language. People often write Hindi in Latin letters (transliteration) and mix English words into Hindi sentences (code-mixing). The question "Applicant ki monthly income kitni hai?" is perfectly normal. A benchmark that only asks questions in native script never tests it.

Real-world pages. Many documents are degraded scans with skew, noise and fading. Seals and stamps overlap text. Newspapers pack many columns onto one page.

Why do these hurt a vision-language model more than a person? A model learns to read a script from images paired with text in that script. The web has a lot of such pairs for Hindi and Bengali, and far fewer for Santali or Kashmiri. The language side has a similar gap. Many tokenizers split words in Indian languages into more pieces than English words, so the same answer takes more tokens to write and gives more chances to slip.

Finally, the documents themselves differ from the English pages benchmarks are built from. A large share of Indian paperwork is government forms, notices and certificates; healthcare bills and prescriptions; and school and exam material. Their layouts and vocabulary are specific to the country. A model trained mostly on English receipts and scientific papers has seen few of them.

If Indian documents are hard, why not measure them with an existing benchmark? Because each existing benchmark trades one kind of coverage for another. Two benchmarks in the table are close relatives. IndicDLP, from IIIT Hyderabad, is a layout dataset in 11 Indian languages and English across 12 domains. It measures where regions are, not what they say. IndicVisionBench covers English and 10 Indian languages with OCR, translation and VQA, but on culturally grounded images rather than documents. Neither tests parsing, structured extraction, multi-hop reasoning or code-mixed questions on documents.

There is a cheaper route to a multilingual benchmark: take an English one and translate its questions. MTVQA's authors pointed out the flaw. Translating the question and answer leaves the text inside the image in the original language, a "visual-textual misalignment". A translated DocVQA asks a Hindi question about an English page. It measures whether the model understands Hindi questions. It does not measure whether the model can read Hindi.

Insight

Translated benchmarks test the easy half of the problem. In Patram-Bench's cross-lingual results, changing the question's language on an English page moved ANLS by at most 0.05 for every model reported. Changing the page itself to an Indic language cost between 31% and 61% of the score (details below). A benchmark built by translating questions would have missed the larger loss. In practice: multilingual document evaluation needs documents written in the target language, not translated prompts.

Nuance

The "Annotations" column does not count the same thing in every row. For Patram-Bench it counts question–answer pairs. For IndicDLP it counts layout regions. For XFUND it counts labelled form entities. A layout box and a multi-hop question differ a lot in effort and in what they test. In practice: compare benchmarks on documents, languages and tasks first, and read annotation counts only within one benchmark.

Three axes: languages, tasks and domains

A benchmark is a sample of the situations a model will face. For Indian documents, three things vary at once, and each can make a model fail on its own.

Language and script. Patram-Bench covers 23 languages, from high-resource ones such as Hindi and Bengali to low-resource ones such as Santali and Kashmiri. For the language-robustness study, the paper groups five Indian languages as high-resource (Hindi, Bengali, Telugu, Tamil, Marathi) and five as low-resource (Meitei, Santali, Sindhi, Kashmiri, Konkani), with English as the anchor.

Task. The 52 tasks fall into eight families in two groups. Perception tasks ask the model to see: recognise text, find regions, convert a page to a structured format, extract a chart or table. Reasoning tasks ask it to think with what it sees: answer extractive and abstractive questions, do arithmetic and multi-hop reasoning, reason over charts and tables, and cope with cross-lingual, transliterated and code-mixed questions.

Domain. The documents come from 13 domains and 71 fine-grained classes: government circulars and tenders, exam papers and marksheets, bank statements and payslips, prescriptions and hospital bills, comics and religious texts, street signs and shop boards. A model that reads a printed circular well may fail on a handwritten prescription in the same language.

Explore the three axes in Figure 2. Each chip opens what the public sources say about it, with real examples from the project page where they exist.

The three axes are not a full grid. Not every task makes sense for every domain (there is no table-to-LaTeX task on a street sign), and not every language has every document type. The axes are best read as three independent ways of slicing one large pool of documents, each of which can expose a different failure.

One form, many tasks

The task list is easier to understand on a single page. Figure 3 is a drawn ration card application, in Hindi and English, of the kind Patram-Bench's forms domain contains. It is an illustration, not a benchmark item. Step through it to see what each task family asks of the same page, and what output it expects.

Two things stand out on this one page. First, the output formats differ a lot. Line recognition expects a string. Layout detection expects labelled boxes. Parsing expects HTML. Extraction expects JSON. Question answering expects a short answer. A benchmark that spans these needs a different scoring rule for each, which we come to below.

Second, the last four questions all have the same answer source but different surface forms. The Hindi question, the transliterated one and the code-mixed one ask the same thing. A model that understands the form should answer all three. Patram-Bench uses exactly this property to measure script robustness: same content, different form of the question.

How the data was built and checked

A benchmark is only as good as its data. Patram-Bench combines two sources: real Indian documents, and synthetic charts, tables and diagrams made by a pipeline called Patram-Syn.

Native documents

The real documents come from four kinds of sources. Governance and administrative documents (notifications, circulars and forms) come from national portals across several states and languages. Educational documents include competitive exam papers, school textbooks and university theses in Indian-language mediums. Financial documents include annual reports, tax filings and regulatory filings published on BSE and NSE in regional languages. Archival and cultural documents include scan-heavy regional newspapers, historic novels, advertisements and recipe books spanning decades.

The corpus covers handwritten, printed and mixed documents, with degraded scans and multi-column layouts. This is deliberate. Clean, single-column pages are where models already do well.

Patram-Syn: synthetic charts, tables and diagrams

Real charts and diagrams in Indian languages are rare, and they come with no record of the numbers behind them. So their ground truth must be typed by hand, and errors creep in. Patram-Syn generates them instead, in five stages:

  1. Persona and topic. Pick one of 200,000 synthesised Indian personas, such as an educator, a public health officer or an accountant, and a topic that persona would care about.
  2. Source-aware retrieval. Retrieve background facts from public Indian sources (government portals, regional news, public reports) so that the content is grounded, not invented.
  3. Render from code. Generate data and code, and render it with Matplotlib (charts), LaTeX (tables), HTML (documents) or Mermaid (diagrams), with Indic font support.
  4. Translate and pose questions. Translate the text and re-render it in 22 languages, generate instruction pairs, and translate both question and answer.
  5. Human verification. 14 native-speaker experts review the layout, the script rendering and the correctness of each QA pair.

Rendering from code is the key decision. When a chart is drawn from a data table, the table is the ground truth for chart-to-JSON and chart questions. Nothing has to be read back off the image by a person.

Nuance

Synthetic Indic documents can be wrong in a way synthetic English documents rarely are: the text can be rendered incorrectly. A renderer that does not shape a script properly draws broken conjuncts or puts vowel signs in the wrong place. The data table is still right, but the picture no longer says what the table says, and the benchmark would mark correct reading as wrong. That is why Patram-Syn's human check covers script rendering, not just the QA pairs. In practice: when you synthesise documents in complex scripts, check the rendered image, not the source text.

Questions, answers and the gold set

With 52,607 documents and 52 tasks, writing every question by hand was not feasible. The QA annotation pipeline uses a model to write and a mix of models and people to check:

  1. Gemini 3 Pro seeds question–answer pairs from each document.
  2. An LLM quality-control pass flags hallucinations and grounding errors (answers not supported by the page).
  3. 14 annotators review a stratified subset, so every task, language and domain is represented in the review.
  4. The result is a human-validated gold set of 51.3k QA pairs, inside the full set of more than 2.4 million annotations.

Figure 4 animates both pipelines and shows where each kind of check sits.

Nuance

The gold set is about 2% of the annotations (51.3k of 2.4M+). The rest were written by Gemini 3 Pro and checked by an LLM, not by a person. Two consequences follow. Results on the full set inherit any systematic error the generating and checking models share. And a model from the same family as the generator may find the questions' phrasing familiar. Gemini 3 Pro leads several task families, which is consistent with its strength but also with this effect; the public sources do not separate the two. In practice: for headline comparisons, prefer numbers on the human-validated gold set, and report which split a number comes from.

Scoring answers: ANLS and an LLM judge

Once a model has answered, the answer must be scored. The simplest rule, exact match, fails on documents. If the page says "₹ 9,500" and the model writes "₹9,500", exact match gives zero for a correct reading.

Document VQA therefore uses ANLS, the Average Normalized Levenshtein Similarity, introduced with the ST-VQA benchmark. Build it up in three steps. The Levenshtein distance counts the fewest single-character insertions, deletions and substitutions that turn one string into the other. Dividing by the longer string's length gives the normalised distance NLD, between 0 and 1. Then ANLS turns distance into similarity, with a cut-off:

ANLS(a, o) = 1 − NLD(a, o)   if NLD(a, o) < τ,   otherwise 0 NLD(a, o) = Levenshtein(a, o) / max(|a|, |o|), where a is the model's answer and o the ground truth. With τ = 0.5, any answer that needs edits to half or more of its characters scores 0. The score for a question is the best over its reference answers, averaged over questions.

The threshold is what makes ANLS work. Below it, a small slip costs a small amount. Above it, the answer is treated as a different answer, not a noisy copy of the right one. Try it in Figure 5, including the cases that expose what ANLS cannot see.

Nuance

ANLS has blind spots that matter more in Indian documents than in English ones. Indian scripts have their own digits, so "₹ ९,५००" and "₹ 9,500" are the same amount but differ in four of seven characters: ANLS gives 0. A wrong amount that differs by one digit, "₹ 9,800", gets 0.857. And a generative model that answers in a full sentence scores 0 even when the sentence contains the right value. Low absolute ANLS numbers for chatty models partly measure verbosity. In practice: normalise digits and strip answer templates before computing ANLS, and use an exact or numeric check for amounts and dates.

ANLS suits short extractive answers. It cannot score an abstractive answer, a summary or an explanation, where many wordings are correct. For these, and for the language-robustness study, Patram-Bench uses G-Eval: a strong language model reads the question, the reference and the answer, and scores the answer on a rubric. The language-robustness scores below are G-Eval scores on a 0 to 100 scale. The per-task results are reported on a 0 to 1 scale with metrics suited to each family; the public sources do not list the metric for every task, so this post reads those numbers as relative comparisons.

Nuance

An LLM judge is itself a model that reads Indian languages unevenly. When it scores a Santali answer, it may be less reliable than when it scores a Hindi one. Then some of the measured drop from high-resource to low-resource languages could come from the judge, not the model being judged. The public sources do not report judge–human agreement per language. In practice: when an evaluation uses an LLM judge across languages, check its agreement with native speakers in the weakest language before trusting the slope.

Two new metrics: DUCLI and SRS

Averages hide the two failures this benchmark cares about most. A model can have a good average and still collapse whenever the question and the document are in different languages, or whenever the question is code-mixed. Patram-Bench adds two metrics to isolate these failures. Both are built from G-Eval scores.

DUCLI: how much survives a change of language

Start from a baseline: the model's score when both the document and the question are in English. Call it S(E, E). Then measure four transfer settings: Indic → Indic, English → Indic, Indic → English, and high-resource Indic → low-resource Indic. If a model understood documents independently of language, all four would equal the baseline. The Document Understanding Cross-Lingual Index is the average of the four, divided by the baseline:

DUCLI = ¼ · [ S(I, I) + S(E, I) + S(I, E) + S(IH, IL) ] / S(E, E) S(X, Y) is the G-Eval score in a setting where the two sides of the task are in languages X and Y. E is English, I an Indic language, IH a high-resource and IL a low-resource Indic language. DUCLI = 1 means nothing is lost when the language changes.

SRS: how much survives a change of script

The second metric holds the language fixed and perturbs the script. The same question is asked in pure Indic form, in code-mixed form, and in transliterated form (the Indic language written in Latin letters). The Script Robustness Score averages the two perturbed scores, each relative to the pure score:

SRS = ½ · [ Scm / Spure + Stl / Spure ] Scm is the score on code-mixed input, Stl on transliterated input, Spure on pure Indic input. SRS = 1 means the model is indifferent to the script of the question. Above 1 means it did better on the perturbed input than on the native one.

Both metrics are ratios, and ratios have a property worth seeing before reading any results. Figure 6 lets you pick any of the thirteen open models the paper reports and see its components. The slider changes the English baseline and shows how DUCLI responds.

Nuance

DUCLI and SRS measure how much is kept, not how much is there. LLaVA-v1.6 has an English baseline of 0.70; if its baseline were 0.82, like Gemma-3-12B's, its DUCLI would fall from 0.441 to about 0.38 with the same Indic scores. A model that is uniformly weak can look robust. In practice: always report DUCLI and SRS next to the absolute scores they are built from. Read a high DUCLI with a low S(I, I) as "consistently weak", not "robust".

The evaluation protocol

The paper evaluates 29 vision-language models, and compares them with OCR services and specialist document models. The poster groups the systems by how they are built and what they are for:

  • Closed models: Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5 and Claude 4 Sonnet.
  • OCR services: Sarvam Vision and Chitrapathak.
  • Large open models: Qwen3-VL-235B, InternVL3.5-241B, GLM-4.5V and LLaMA 4 Scout.
  • Medium open models: Qwen3-VL 30B and 32B, InternVL3.5 30B and 38B, Gemma 3 27B, DeepSeek-VL2 and LLaVA 1.6.
  • Small open models: Qwen3-VL 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, InternVL 8B, Molmo2 8B, Gemma 3 12B, Pixtral 12B and MiniCPM-V 4.5.
  • Specialist document models: Surya, Marker, Chandra, DeepSeek-OCR, dots.ocr, DocLayout-YOLO, IndicDLP, Hi-SAM and DocTr.

Specialists matter here. A general vision-language model might be beaten on layout by a small detector trained for that one job. Leaving specialists out would make general models look better than they are relative to what a practitioner could deploy. The language-robustness study also includes multilingual and Indic-focused models such as PALO, Maya, PANGEA and Chitrarth.

Each evaluation setting changes one thing and holds the rest fixed, so that a drop can be attributed. Table 2 lists the settings and the question each one answers.

Results across tasks

Begin with the broadest view: which models do well on which task families. Figure 7 shows every number the project page publishes in three views. The first is per task family. The second is per language. The third is the cross-lingual ANLS study.

The leader depends on the task

On the task families, Gemini 3 Pro is best on text recognition (0.88), element parsing (0.85) and linguistic robustness (0.88). Claude 4 Sonnet is best on document parsing (0.90) and text-centric DocVQA (0.88). GPT-5 is best on structured element reasoning (0.89) and analytical and knowledge reasoning (0.91). The paper summarises this as Gemini 3 Pro leading overall, with Claude 4 Sonnet winning text-heavy parsing.

On the language-robustness study the order changes again. GPT-5 has the highest G-Eval score in every one of the eleven languages, from 82 in English to 48 in Sindhi. Gemini 3 Pro's language scores (74 in English, 41 in Sindhi) are below Qwen3-VL-235B's (75 and 46).

Insight

"Best model" is a property of an axis, not of a model. Gemini 3 Pro leads three task families but trails GPT-5 and Qwen3-VL-235B on the language-robustness G-Eval. Three different closed models win task families, and a specialist wins a fourth. A single aggregate score would hide all of this. In practice: choose a model for an Indian document workload by its score on that workload's task family and language, not by its overall rank.

Specialists still win on layout

On document segmentation, the best system is not a vision-language model at all. Dedicated layout and text-region models, IndicDLP, Surya and Hi-SAM, beat every general model outright. The best open system for segmentation is also IndicDLP. The same family has the worst system: PP-DocLayout, a specialist tuned on non-Indian layouts, generalises poorly here.

Insight

The training distribution decides layout quality more than the model category. The best and the worst segmentation systems are both specialists. The difference is that IndicDLP was trained on Indian documents and PP-DocLayout was not. General VLMs sit in between. In practice: for layout on Indian documents, a small detector trained on in-domain data is still the strong baseline to beat, and an out-of-domain specialist can be worse than a general model.

The open–closed gap, and a mid-sized surprise

On the reasoning families, the best open model trails the best closed one by three to five points: element parsing 0.82 against 0.85, text-centric DocVQA 0.85 against 0.88, structured reasoning 0.85 against 0.89, analytical reasoning 0.86 against 0.91, and linguistic robustness 0.84 against 0.88.

The best open model is often not the largest. Qwen3-VL-32B beats its 235B sibling on element parsing (0.82 against 0.80) and text-centric DocVQA (0.85 against 0.84). GLM-4.5V leads the open models on analytical reasoning (0.86), and Qwen3-VL-30B on linguistic robustness (0.84). The gaps are small, but the pattern recurs, and it lines up with the image-removal result later in the post.

Isolated collapses are probably harness failures

Three cells in the task view look strange. Pixtral-12B scores 0.15 on element parsing but 0.64 to 0.66 on every other family. Molmo-2-8B scores 0.12 on linguistic robustness and 0.55 to 0.60 elsewhere. InternVL3.5-8B scores 0.14 on structured reasoning and 0.50 to 0.55 elsewhere. The authors flag the Pixtral cell as more likely a parsing or harness artifact than a real capability gap.

Nuance

When one model drops to near zero on exactly one family, the likely cause is the output format, not the skill. If the evaluation parser expects JSON and the model wraps it in prose, every answer fails. Such a cell measures instruction following on the harness's format. In practice: inspect raw outputs for any single-cell collapse before reporting it, and report format-failure rates separately from accuracy.

At the bottom, LLaVA-1.6 is worst on four task families (text recognition 0.21, document parsing 0.18, text-centric DocVQA 0.17, analytical reasoning 0.15). LLaVA-1.6 and Pixtral-12B also sit near the bottom of the DUCLI and SRS rankings. Weak models fail consistently, which is reassuring about the benchmark's internal agreement.

Results across languages

Now hold the task fixed and vary the language. Figure 8 plots the G-Eval scores of five models across English, five high-resource and five low-resource Indian languages. The left-to-right slope is the robustness story.

Three things are visible. First, the high-resource languages cost little. GPT-5 goes from 82 in English to between 75 and 79 in Hindi, Bengali, Telugu, Tamil and Marathi. Second, the low-resource group is a cliff. GPT-5 scores 50 in Santali and 48 in Sindhi. LLaVA-1.6-13B scores 37 and 35.

Third, the cliff has the same relative depth for every plotted model. On Sindhi, the five models keep between 57% and 61% of their English score: GPT-5 48 of 82, Qwen3-VL-235B 46 of 75, Qwen3-VL-32B 42 of 69, Gemma-3-27B 38 of 66, and LLaVA-1.6-13B 35 of 61.

Insight

On these published numbers, the drop on a low-resource language is close to proportional and close to model-independent. A frontier closed model and a two-year-old open model lose almost the same fraction on Sindhi. Scale and recency raise the whole curve but do not flatten it. In practice: improving low-resource languages is a data problem, not something the next larger model will fix by itself.

Nuance

"Low-resource" is not one level of difficulty. Kashmiri and Konkani are in the low-resource group, yet GPT-5 scores 72 and 67 on them, close to the high-resource languages and far above Santali (50) and Sindhi (48). The public sources do not break down why. Script is one candidate (Konkani is usually written in Devanagari, like Hindi and Marathi), but Kashmiri and Sindhi are both commonly written in Perso-Arabic and still differ. In practice: report low-resource languages individually. An average over the group hides a 24-point spread.

One more pattern appears in the full language table in Figure 7. The models built specifically for multilingual or Indian use, PALO, Maya, PANGEA and Chitrarth, score 48 to 51 overall, below general open models such as Qwen3-VL-8B (58). Their shape across languages is the same as everyone else's. Coverage of a language in training does not substitute for overall model strength, at least for the models evaluated here.

Nuance

Most open models in the published language table have nearly parallel profiles. For example, Qwen3-VL-235B, Gemma-3-27B and Qwen3-VL-8B all score 4 points below English in Hindi and 6 below in Bengali. So the language view mostly separates models by overall level, and its language-to-language differences are shared across models. In practice: use the language table to rank languages by difficulty, and use DUCLI, SRS and the cross-lingual study to compare models' robustness.

Cross-lingual and code-mixed questions

Reading the page, not the question, is the bottleneck

The cross-lingual study asks questions in a different language from the document. For English documents, the question is in English, a high-resource Indic language or a low-resource one. For Indic documents, the question is in English, in the same Indic language, or in a different Indic language.

The ANLS numbers split cleanly. On English documents, the question's language barely matters. Gemma-3-27B scores 0.323 with English questions, 0.327 with high-resource Indic questions and 0.337 with low-resource ones. For every model reported, switching the question's language on an English page moves ANLS by at most 0.047.

On Indic documents, everything falls, even with an English question. Gemma-3-27B drops from 0.323 to 0.193, keeping 60% of its score. Qwen3-VL-32B keeps 68% (0.197 against 0.289). InternVL-38B keeps 39% (0.075 against 0.194), and Pixtral-12B keeps 44% (0.081 against 0.183). Across the seven models, the loss from moving the page into an Indic language is 31% to 61%.

Insight

The models understand Indian-language questions much better than they read Indian-language pages. Changing the question costs almost nothing; changing the page costs a third to well over half of the score. The weaker the model, the larger the share it loses. In practice: to improve Indian document understanding, invest in visual text data in Indian scripts (OCR-rich pretraining, rendered documents), not in translated instruction data.

The project page summarises the ordering as English → cross-lingual → cross-Indic, with a different-language Indic question on an Indic page as the floor. On the published ANLS numbers this holds for most models in the low-resource case: Qwen3-VL-32B scores 0.138 and Qwen3-VL-30B 0.128 with a different low-resource language. It is not strict for every model; Gemma-3-27B scores 0.190 in that setting, above its 0.161 with a same-language low-resource question.

Code-mixing and transliteration

The script-robustness study is the starkest result in the paper. Figure 6 above holds the numbers; here is what they say. On pure Indic questions, all thirteen open models score between 0.52 and 0.57. The spread is tiny. On code-mixed and transliterated versions of the same questions, twelve of the thirteen fall to between 0.10 and 0.21. That is a loss of roughly 60% to 80% from a change in how the question is written, not in what it asks.

Insight

Performance on clean Indic input does not predict performance on the way people actually type. The models are almost indistinguishable on pure Indic questions (0.52 to 0.57) and almost all fail on code-mixed ones. Since Spure is nearly constant, the SRS ranking is essentially a ranking of code-mixed scores. In practice: an evaluation of an Indian-language assistant that uses only native-script questions will overestimate it badly. Include code-mixed and transliterated queries from real users.

The exception is Gemma-3-12B. It scores 0.63 on code-mixed and 0.65 on transliterated input, against 0.55 on pure Indic, giving an SRS of 1.160. It also has the highest DUCLI of the open models, 0.767, ahead of LLaMA 4 Scout at 0.664 and its larger sibling Gemma-3-27B at 0.644. The paper reads this as evidence that cross-script robustness tracks script diversity and cross-lingual supervision in training, not parameter count.

Nuance

Gemma-3-12B's result is a single outlier, and its cause is not isolated. The project page suggests a tokenizer or training-mix effect. A tokenizer alone is unlikely to explain the gap to Gemma-3-27B, because the Gemma 3 technical report states that all Gemma 3 models share the same tokenizer. An SRS above 1, where perturbed input beats native input, is also not what any notion of robustness predicts. In practice: treat this as a lead to replicate (other seeds, the gold split, a second judge), not as evidence that a 12B model is the right choice.

Do models read the page?

A model can answer a document question in two ways. It can read the answer off the page. Or it can produce something plausible from what the question and its training suggest. On a benchmark both give credit, but only the first is reading. Patram-Bench separates them with a simple test: withhold the image and ask again. If accuracy barely drops, the model was not using the image much.

The result: among closed models, Gemini 3 Pro relies most on the page, then Claude 4 Sonnet. Among open models, the most image-dependent are Qwen3-VL-32B, then Qwen3-VL-30B and Gemma-3-27B. The heaviest reliance on language priors comes from the two largest open models, Qwen3-VL-235B and then InternVL3.5-241B. They still produce plausible answers with the image removed.

Insight

More parameters bring stronger language priors, and on Indian documents those priors can substitute for reading. The largest open models guess most confidently without the page, and the mid-sized Qwen3-VL models stay more grounded than their 235B sibling. This matches the task results, where Qwen3-VL-32B beat the 235B model on extraction and text-centric DocVQA. In practice: on document tasks, run an image-removal control before choosing the larger model, and treat a small drop as a warning, not a strength.

This failure is not specific to India. MMStar found that Gemini Pro scored 42.9% on MMMU with no image at all, and that some models answer questions that do need the image, apparently from memorised training data. What Patram-Bench adds is that the effect grows with scale within a model family, on documents in Indian languages, where a confident guess is hard for a user to check.

Limits of the benchmark

Every benchmark is a set of choices, and each choice limits what its numbers mean. These are the ones to keep in mind when citing Patram-Bench.

  • Model-written questions. Gemini 3 Pro seeded the QA pairs and an LLM checked them. Only the 51.3k gold set is human-validated. Errors the generator and checker share can survive, and the generator's own family may find the phrasing familiar.
  • An LLM judge across uneven languages. G-Eval scores depend on a judge that also reads low-resource languages less reliably. Some of the low-resource drop may be the judge's.
  • Metric blind spots. ANLS gives 0 to correct answers in Indic digits and to correct answers in full sentences, and gives high scores to wrong amounts that differ by one digit.
  • Contamination. Many native documents come from public government portals, exchange filings and newspapers. Such pages are exactly what web-scale pretraining crawls. A model may have seen a page, or a near copy, during training. The public sources do not report a contamination check.
  • Harness sensitivity. Isolated near-zero cells (Pixtral on element parsing, Molmo-2-8B on linguistic robustness, InternVL3.5-8B on structured reasoning) are more likely output-format failures than skill gaps.
  • Unexplained outliers. Gemma-3-12B's SRS above 1 and its DUCLI lead are not explained, and a single result should not drive model choice.
  • What the public sources omit. Per-task metrics, the full list of 23 languages, and per-split results are in the paper, not on the project page. This post uses only what the page, poster and research card publish.

Nuance

Several of these limits push in the same direction. Model-written questions, a judge that favours fluent text, and contamination from public documents all tend to flatter large, well-trained models. The image-removal finding is the one result built to resist this, because it measures the model against itself. In practice: when a benchmark's numbers and its control experiments disagree, trust the controls.

How it relates to our other work

Patram-Bench sits next to Patram, the vision-language model for Indian document understanding built at BharatGen over BharatDocs, a corpus of more than 20 million documents and 250 million pages in English and 22 Indian languages. The models evaluated in the paper do not include Patram; it is a benchmark of the field, not of our model.

Some of its findings connect to the grounded document intelligence work in Building Grounded Document Intelligence:

  • Layout. Specialists trained on Indian layouts beat general models on segmentation. DocLayout-VL, a promptable layout model from that work, reports 77.5 mAP zero-shot on IndicDLP.
  • Reading the page. The image-removal test catches models that answer without looking, but only in aggregate. A model that must mark its evidence on the page, as M3Grounder does with masks, exposes the same failure on each answer: with no page there is nothing to point at.
  • Rendering from code. Patram-Syn renders charts and tables from code so that the ground truth is known. REPLICA's data engine and M3Grounder's chart data rely on the same idea: label by construction, not by reading back.
  • Parsing targets. Patram-Bench's parsing tasks score Markdown, HTML and LaTeX output, because that is what models produce today. A parsing benchmark can only reward what its target format can hold; position and style are outside these targets.

For the general picture of how vision-language models are evaluated, see Measuring What a VLM Can Do. For how document and OCR models are built, see How VLMs Read Documents.

Key insights and nuances

  • The document's language matters more than the question's. On English pages, changing the question's language moved ANLS by at most 0.047; moving the page into an Indic language cost 31% to 61%. Translated benchmarks miss the larger loss. (Cross-lingual questions)
  • Code-mixing is the sharpest failure. Twelve of thirteen open models fall from 0.52–0.57 on pure Indic questions to 0.10–0.21 on code-mixed ones. Native-script evaluation alone overestimates real use. (Code-mixing)
  • The low-resource drop is proportional and shared. All five plotted models keep 57% to 61% of their English score on Sindhi. Larger and newer models raise the curve but do not flatten it. (Languages)
  • "Low-resource" is not one level. Kashmiri and Konkani score far above Santali and Sindhi for every plotted model. Report languages individually. (Languages)
  • The best model depends on the axis. Three closed models win different task families, a specialist wins layout, and the language ranking differs from the task ranking. (Tasks)
  • In-domain training decides layout. The best and worst segmentation systems are both specialists; only the training data differs. (Tasks)
  • The largest open models lean on priors. Qwen3-VL-235B and InternVL3.5-241B answer most plausibly without the image; mid-sized Qwen3-VL models are more grounded and beat the 235B model on extraction. (Reading the page)
  • Ratios measure retention, not ability. DUCLI and SRS reward a weak English baseline; always read them with absolute scores. (DUCLI and SRS)
  • ANLS fails on Indic digits and wrong amounts. Correct answers in Devanagari digits score 0, and a one-digit wrong amount scores 0.857. (ANLS)
  • Know which split a number comes from. Only about 2% of annotations are human-validated gold, and the generator's family leads several tasks. (Data)
  • Single-cell collapses are format failures. Check raw outputs before reporting a model as unable to do a task. (Tasks)

After this post you should be able to read a multilingual document benchmark critically: ask whether its documents or only its questions are in the target language, whether its metric understands the target script's digits, whether its ratios hide a weak baseline, and whether it checks that models read the page at all.
