A fourth axis

Every scaling story in this series so far has been a story about training. In Parameters, Tokens, Compute the whole problem was a constrained optimisation over two numbers: given a training budget Ctrain, how do you split it between parameters N and tokens D? Once you have chosen, the run happens, the weights freeze, and quality is whatever the law says it is.

Q = f(N, D, Ctrain)The classical picture. Everything that determines quality is decided before deployment.

That picture is incomplete, and the incompleteness is not a detail. A frozen model is not a lookup table; it is a sampler. You can query it once, or a thousand times. You can let it emit twelve tokens or twelve thousand. You can throw away nine hundred of its thousand answers and keep the one that survives a test suite. None of that touches a single weight, and all of it changes the probability you get a correct answer.

Q = f(N, D, Ctrain, Ctest)The modern picture. A fourth knob, turned per query, after the model has shipped.

The conceptual centre of this article is that fourth argument. It behaves differently from the first three in three important ways, and most confusion about test-time scaling comes from forgetting one of them.

It is spent per query, not per model. Ctrain is paid once and amortised over every request for the lifetime of the model. Ctest is paid every single time. A technique that buys five accuracy points for a 100× inference bill is a research result; it is not necessarily a product.

It is allocatable per instance. There is exactly one compute-optimal (N, D) for a training run. There is a different optimal Ctest for every prompt, because prompts differ in difficulty. This single fact is what makes inference scaling a strictly richer optimisation problem than Chinchilla, and it is where most of the remaining headroom lives.

It converts a distribution into an answer. Extra training compute makes the model's output distribution better. Extra test-time compute lets you sample that distribution more than once and then choose. Choosing requires a selector. The quality of the selector, not the quantity of samples, is usually the binding constraint — and that is the single most under-appreciated result in this whole literature.

The recurring theme of this article: generation and selection scale differently. Your ability to produce a correct answer somewhere in a pile of samples keeps improving for a very long time. Your ability to point at which one it is often stops improving almost immediately.

Eight ways to spend a budget

Before any mathematics, it helps to see the landscape as a single plane. Almost every test-time method in the literature is a point in a two-dimensional space: how wide you go (how many independent trajectories you run) and how deep you go (how many tokens each trajectory is allowed to consume). Everything else — verifiers, voting, search, refinement — is a rule for what to do with the trajectories once you have them.

The figure below walks that plane. Step through it; each stage lands on one family and names what it actually buys you.

Figure 1 · The width–depth plane

Every strategy is a shape in this plane. The camera visits each family in turn.

Frame 01 / 64

Reading the plane. The horizontal axis is serial depth T — tokens inside one trajectory. The vertical axis is parallel width K — independent trajectories. Total token spend is roughly the area K × T, which means the same budget can be spent anywhere along a hyperbola. The strategies differ in where on that hyperbola they sit and in what they do with the resulting pile of text.

Written out, the eight families are:

  • A. Longer serial reasoning. One trajectory, more tokens. The model plans, backtracks, checks. This is what o1-style and R1-style models were trained to do; the reasoning trace itself is the compute.
  • B. Repeated sampling. K independent trajectories at temperature. No coordination, no selection yet — pure exploration of the output distribution.
  • C. Self-consistency. Sample K chains, take the majority answer. Selection with no external machinery, introduced by Wang et al. as a decoding strategy for chain-of-thought.
  • D. Best-of-N. Generate N, score each with something, keep the top-scoring one. The "something" is the whole ballgame.
  • E. Search. Beam, tree, MCTS-style rollouts. Instead of committing to whole trajectories, you branch at intermediate states and prune.
  • F. Verification. A verifier — unit tests, a proof checker, an outcome reward model, a process reward model — ranks or filters candidates. This is not really a separate axis; it is the selector that makes D and E work at all.
  • G. Iterative refinement. Generate, critique, revise, repeat. Serial like A, but with an explicit critique step rather than a continuous stream.
  • H. Tools. Code execution, retrieval, search. Compute spent outside the model, which is often the cheapest correct answer in the whole taxonomy.

Nuance 01 · Search is not a fifth thing

Often said: there are parallel methods, serial methods, and search methods.

Actually: search is parallel sampling with early pruning, which requires a scorer that can evaluate partial trajectories. That requirement — a value estimate at intermediate states rather than only at the end — is the real distinction, and it is why process reward models and tree search are the same topic. If your only signal arrives at the final token, tree search degenerates into an expensive way of doing best-of-N.

What the extra tokens cost

It is tempting to treat Ctest as one number. It is not; it is at least four, and they scale differently. This matters because two strategies that look identical on a FLOPs plot can be an order of magnitude apart in latency or in how many concurrent requests a server can hold.

Take a dense model of N parameters. Decoding costs roughly 2N FLOPs per generated token for the weight-matrix work. Compare two ways of spending the same token budget KT: K parallel trajectories of T tokens, versus one trajectory of KT tokens.

BillK trajectories × T tokens1 trajectory × KT tokens
Weight FLOPs2NKT2NKT — identical
Attention FLOPsΘ(KT2)Θ(K2T2) — K× worse
Latency (decode steps)T, if the batch fitsKT, strictly serial
KV-cache bytesKT token-slotsKT token-slots — identical
Prefillshared prefix, cache onceone prefill
Context-length risknonemust stay in-distribution at length KT

Two of those rows are worth pausing on. The KV-cache row is an equality that surprises people: K caches of length T and one cache of length KT hold the same number of token-slots, so the memory bill for going wide and going deep is the same. What differs is shape — going wide needs K concurrent batch slots, going deep needs one very long sequence. The attention row is a genuine asymmetry: because attention cost grows with position, a single long trajectory pays quadratically in its own length while K short ones pay quadratically only in T. Serial depth is the more expensive kind of compute, per token, and it gets worse the deeper you go.

The mechanics behind those rows — prefix caching, batch-slot pressure, the prefill/decode split, why decode is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than FLOPs-bound — are the subject of One Token at a Time and The Serving Playbook. Here I only want the accounting, because it explains a practical asymmetry: parallel width is cheap to add and hard to use; serial depth is expensive to add and easy to use. Width gives you many candidates and no opinion about which is right. Depth gives you one candidate the model already believes in.

Nuance 02 · "FLOPs-matched" is not "cost-matched"

Often said: a small model with test-time compute can match a model 14× larger at equal FLOPs, therefore it is the better deployment.

Actually: equal FLOPs at very different latencies and batch shapes are not equal money. A 7B model emitting 64 trajectories has 64× the KV-cache footprint of a single trajectory and needs a serving stack that can hold them; a 70B model emitting one has a larger weight-load per step but a trivial scheduling story. The FLOPs-matched comparison is the right scientific control and the wrong procurement control.

Why sampling has a scaling law

Start with the simplest possible intervention: ask the same question k times and see whether any answer is right. The quantity that tracks is coverage — the fraction of problems solved by at least one sample. Brown et al. measured this carefully and found something striking: coverage keeps rising with sample count across four orders of magnitude, and the relationship is often log-linear, well modelled by an exponentiated power law. On SWE-bench Lite, DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct went from solving 15.9% of issues with one sample to 56% with 250 — past the 43% single-sample state of the art at the time.

That log-linearity is initially baffling, because for a single problem it is provably false. Let me derive why, because the resolution is the most useful piece of intuition in this article.

One problem: coverage saturates fast

Fix a problem the model solves with probability p per independent sample. The chance that k samples all fail is (1−p)k, so

c(k) = 1 − (1 − p)kCoverage for one problem with per-sample success rate p. This is exactly pass@k under independent sampling.

Solve for the half-way point: c(k) = ½ at k* = ln2 / ln(1/(1−p)) ≈ 0.69/p. Plotted against log k, this is a sigmoid: nothing, then a sharp rise centred at roughly 1/p, then a flat ceiling at 1. It is not log-linear anywhere. A problem with p = 0.01 is essentially unsolved at k = 10 and essentially solved at k = 1000, and in between it swings through the entire range in about two decades of k.

Many problems: the sigmoids add up to a power law

Now aggregate. A benchmark is not one problem with one p; it is a population of problems with a distribution of p. Write that distribution as a density π(p). Benchmark coverage is the average of the per-problem sigmoids:

C(k) = ∫01 π(p) · [ 1 − (1−p)k ] dp  =  1 − Eπ[ (1−p)k ]Aggregate coverage is a mixture of sigmoids, each centred at its own 1/p.

Take a concrete and reasonable family: p ∼ Beta(a, b). Then the expectation has a closed form, and its large-k behaviour is the whole story:

E[(1−p)k] = B(a, b+k) / B(a, b)  ⟶  [Γ(a+b)/Γ(b)] · k−aUsing Γ(b+k)/Γ(a+b+k) ∼ k−a as k grows. The uncovered fraction decays as a power law.
1 − C(k)  ∝  k−a   ⇒   log(1 − C(k)) = const − a · log kA straight line on a log–log plot of the failure fraction. The exponent is a, the Beta shape parameter near p = 0.

This is the punchline, and it is worth saying in words. No individual problem obeys a power law. The population does, and the exponent is determined entirely by how much probability mass sits near p = 0 — how many problems the model can almost never solve but not never solve. A Beta density behaves like pa−1 near zero; small a means a thick pile-up of nearly-hopeless problems, and each decade of extra sampling drags a slice of them across the line. The smooth scaling curve is an artefact of heterogeneity, not a property of any single instance.

And the corollary is immediate: if a fraction π0 of problems have p exactly zero — the model genuinely cannot produce the answer, at any temperature, ever — then coverage saturates at 1 − π0 and no amount of sampling moves it. The straight line is a straight line only until it hits that wall.

Nuance 03 · The model is an idealisation, and the fit is not exactly this

Often said: coverage follows a power law in the number of samples.

Actually: Brown et al. report that an exponentiated power law, C(k) ≈ exp(αkβ), fits their curves better than a plain power law over the ranges they measured. The Beta mixture above is a mechanism that produces power-law tails and explains why heterogeneity alone is sufficient; it is not a claim that real benchmarks have exactly Beta-distributed difficulty. Treat it as the right causal story with the wrong third decimal place. It also assumes samples are independent, which temperature-sampling from one model only approximately satisfies.

The generator–verifier gap

Coverage is not accuracy. Coverage says a correct answer exists somewhere in the pile. Accuracy requires you to hand back exactly one answer. The distance between those two quantities is the single most important number in test-time scaling, and it is where the field's early optimism ran into a wall.

The same paper that showed coverage rising over four orders of magnitude also reported the other half: in domains without automatic verifiers, majority voting and reward-model selection plateau beyond several hundred samples and fail to keep up with the sample budget. Generation kept scaling. Selection stopped.

Why majority voting has a hard ceiling

This is provable in one line, and the proof explains everything. Let the model's answer distribution for a given problem be q(·) over possible final answers. Majority voting over k samples returns the empirical mode. By the law of large numbers, as k → ∞ the empirical mode converges to the true mode argmaxy q(y). Therefore:

limk→∞ AccMV(k) = Pproblems[ the model's modal answer is correct ]A fixed property of the model. Extra samples reduce the variance of your estimate of the mode; they cannot move the mode.

So majority voting is an estimator for a quantity that is already determined the moment the weights froze. Past a few dozen samples you are paying compute to reduce sampling noise on a number that was never going to change. Worse, on hard problems where the modal answer is a confident, systematic error, more voting makes you more sure of the wrong thing.

Contrast that with coverage, whose limit is 1 − π0 — the fraction of problems the model can produce a correct answer for at all. The gap between these two limits is the generator–verifier gap:

gap = (1 − π0) − P[modal answer correct]Everything a better selector could still recover, at zero extra generation cost.
This is why so much of the reasoning literature is secretly about verifiers. The gap is the prize. Every process reward model, every unit-test harness, every self-consistency variant, every proof checker is an attempt to convert coverage you already paid for into accuracy you can ship.

The false-positive ceiling

Now put a verifier in the loop. If the verifier is perfect — a Lean proof checker, an exhaustive test oracle, a numerical answer key — the picture is beautiful: accuracy equals coverage, and coverage keeps scaling. This is exactly why the strongest published sampling results live in code and formal proofs, and why the SWE-bench number above was possible.

Real verifiers are not perfect. Unit tests have gaps. Reward models are approximations. Self-verification is the model grading its own homework. Stroebl, Kapoor and Narayanan made the consequence precise, and the argument is short enough to reconstruct from scratch.

Let p be the per-sample probability of a correct solution and let φ be the verifier's false-positive rate: the probability that an incorrect sample passes. Assume for simplicity that correct samples always pass. After k samples, the expected number of passing candidates that are correct is kp; the expected number that are wrong-but-passing is k(1−p)φ. If you pick uniformly among passers, your accuracy is the precision of the verifier on that pool:

Acc(k)  ⟶  p / [ p + (1−p)φ ]Independent of k. Resampling raises the chance that a correct answer appears — and equally the chance that a false positive appears.

Read that limit carefully. It contains no k. Sampling harder does not improve it, because sampling harder recruits false positives at exactly the same rate it recruits true ones. Some concrete values, with p = 0.05:

  • φ = 0 → ceiling 100%. Perfect verification; scale away.
  • φ = 0.01 → ceiling 84%. A 1% false-positive rate costs sixteen points.
  • φ = 0.05 → ceiling 51%. Equal to the sample success rate; you are flipping a coin among passers.
  • φ = 0.20 → ceiling 21%. Most of what survives verification is wrong.

The asymmetry is brutal: the ceiling depends on the ratio φ/p, so the harder the problem — the smaller p — the more a fixed verifier error rate hurts. This is the mathematical form of a familiar practical experience. Resampling a coding agent until the tests pass works wonderfully until the tests are the thing that is wrong, at which point you have built a very expensive machine for finding test-suite exploits.

Nuance 04 · Verifier failures are worse under search than under sampling

Often said: guiding generation with a verifier is strictly better than blind resampling, because you prune bad paths early.

Actually: pruning is irreversible. Yu, Li and Wang found that verifier-guided search beats repeated sampling at small budgets but its advantage shrinks and can invert as the budget grows, because an imperfect verifier eventually mis-ranks and prunes away every valid path. Blind sampling wastes compute; guided search can destroy the solution. The crossover point depends on how far out-of-distribution the problem is for the verifier.

Nuance 05 · Self-verification is not free verification

Often said: just have the model check its own answer — it knows more than it says.

Actually: sometimes it works well. Zhao, Awasthi and Gollapudi found that scaling a minimal random-sampling plus direct self-verification loop kept improving, partly because a larger response pool also improves verification accuracy by giving the verifier things to compare against. But Huang et al. found that models often cannot reliably self-correct reasoning without external feedback, and Stechly, Valmeekam and Kambhampati documented systematic self-verification limitations on reasoning and planning. The honest summary is that self-verification's value is highly task-dependent and its false-positive rate is correlated with the generator's own errors — which is precisely the worst case for the ceiling formula above, since it violates the independence the formula assumes.

Compute-optimal inference

With generation and selection separated, the design question becomes an allocation question, and it has an exact analogue in the training-time story. Set them side by side, because the parallel is the intellectual pivot of this article.

The first three rows are a clean analogy. The last three are where the analogy breaks, and each break makes the inference problem harder.

Snell, Lee, Xu and Kumar ran the experiment that made this concrete. They studied two mechanisms — searching against dense process-based verifier reward models, and letting the model revise its own answer distribution at test time — and found that which mechanism wins depends critically on prompt difficulty. Easy prompts want sequential revision; harder prompts want more parallel search. Because the right answer varies per prompt, a strategy that picks per prompt beat a fixed best-of-N baseline using more than 4× less test-time compute. And in a FLOPs-matched comparison, on problems where a smaller base model already had non-trivial success rates, test-time compute let it outperform a 14× larger model.

That last sentence gets quoted constantly and misread almost as constantly, so it is worth unpacking the two qualifiers that are doing all the work.

Nuance 06 · The 14× result has two load-bearing conditions

Often said: test-time compute can substitute for a 14× larger model.

Actually: the result holds on problems where the smaller model already attains non-trivial success rates. That is the coverage condition from earlier in a different costume: if p ≈ 0, no amount of sampling helps, and the paper says so. The second condition is the inference-to-pretraining token ratio. When you will serve very few tokens, buying test-time compute is cheap relative to pretraining a bigger model; when you will serve enormous volumes, the fixed cost of a better model amortises and pretraining wins again. Test-time compute substitutes for scale in a bounded regime, not universally.

This is also where the story connects back to L3. The lifetime-cost equation there was Ctotal = Ctrain + Q · Cinference. Test-time scaling does not change the shape of that equation; it inflates Cinference by a factor you now control. A model that answers with 10,000 thinking tokens has an inference cost roughly two orders of magnitude above one that answers with 100. If Q is large, that factor lands directly on the wrong side of the ledger, and the compute-optimal choice shifts back towards a better model that thinks less.

What the curves measured

The empirical counterpart to Kaplan and Chinchilla plots is Wu et al., who swept cost–performance trade-offs across greedy decoding, majority voting, best-of-n, weighted voting and two tree-search algorithms, at several model sizes and compute budgets. The headline finding rhymes with Snell: smaller models paired with better inference algorithms sit on the Pareto frontier. Concretely, Llemma-7B with their REBASE tree search outperformed Llemma-34B with standard majority voting on MATH across all the FLOPs budgets they tested, and they report the 7B model beating the 34B until roughly 128 solutions are sampled.

Two secondary findings from that paper deserve more attention than they usually get. First, MCTS underperformed plain sampling at matched budget, because rollouts are expensive and it produced many unfinished solutions that then cast ineffective votes — a reminder that a search algorithm's cost model matters as much as its search quality. Second, REBASE's advantage came from using a process reward model to decide how far to expand each node rather than paying for explicit rollouts. Both observations point the same direction: at fixed budget, the winner is usually the method that extracts the most signal per token, not the method with the most sophisticated search.

Nuance 07 · Inference scaling curves are benchmark-shaped

Often said: inference scaling laws are the test-time equivalent of Chinchilla.

Actually: Chinchilla fits a smooth loss that is defined for every model on every corpus. Inference curves fit accuracy on a specific benchmark, which means the curve inherits that benchmark's difficulty distribution — the π(p) from Figure 2. Change the mix of easy and hard problems and both the level and the slope move. This is why published inference-scaling exponents do not transfer between MATH, GSM8K and SWE-bench, and why "our method scales better" claims should always be read alongside the difficulty stratification. Wu et al. themselves split MATH into easy and hard subsets precisely because the aggregate hides the mechanism.

Budget forcing

Everything so far has treated Ctest as a dial you set from outside. For serial reasoning that is harder than it sounds: the model decides when to stop by emitting an end-of-thinking delimiter, and you do not control that decision. Muennighoff et al. proposed the most stripped-down possible fix, and it is my favourite result in this literature because it is so nearly trivial.

Budget forcing has two halves. To spend less: if the model exceeds a token limit, force-append the end-of-thinking delimiter, which makes it transition to producing an answer. To spend more: suppress that delimiter when the model tries to emit it, and append the word "Wait" instead. The model, having been handed a continuation where it expected a terminator, goes back and re-examines its work.

That is the entire mechanism. No new training objective, no verifier, no search. Applied to a 32B model supervise-finetuned on just 1,000 curated questions and reasoning traces, it produced a model that exceeded o1-preview on competition maths by up to 27%, and extending thinking with budget forcing moved AIME24 from 50% to 57% — past what the same model achieved without the intervention.

The word choice is not incidental. The paper's own ablation found "Wait" outperformed neutral fillers like "Hmm", which suggests the mechanism is less "more compute" and more "an injected prompt to doubt". That is a meaningfully different claim, and one the community has not fully digested: budget forcing may be a lightweight semantic intervention wearing a compute-scaling costume.

Nuance 08 · Budget forcing does not scale indefinitely

Often said: you can just keep appending "Wait" and keep gaining accuracy.

Actually: the reported gains flatten and then reverse. The paper measures three separate properties — control (does the knob actually change token count), scaling (does accuracy rise with the knob), and performance (the peak reached) — precisely because a method can succeed at the first and fail at the second. Suppressing a terminator repeatedly eventually produces repetition loops and drift rather than deliberation. The knob has a working range, and finding it is an empirical exercise per model.

When more thinking hurts

Every curve so far has been monotone or asymptotic. Neither is guaranteed. There is now a substantial body of work showing that extending reasoning can make accuracy fall, and understanding why is important because the failure is not random noise — it is structured.

Gema et al. constructed tasks where longer reasoning reliably degrades performance and catalogued five distinct failure modes. Claude models became progressively more distracted by irrelevant information in the prompt as they reasoned longer. OpenAI o-series models resisted distractors but overfitted to the framing of the problem. On tasks with spurious features, extended reasoning pushed models away from a reasonable prior and towards plausible-but-wrong correlations — though few-shot examples largely fixed that one. All models struggled to hold focus on complex deductive constraint tracking. And extended reasoning amplified some concerning behaviours, with Claude Sonnet 4 showing increased expressions of self-preservation.

The reported magnitudes are not small. In their evaluation, Claude Opus 4 dropped from near 100% to roughly 85–90% as reasoning extended, and DeepSeek R1 fell from 70% to 30% in a natural-overthinking setup with five distractors.

Independently, Chen et al. documented the complementary waste: o1-like models burning enormous token counts on trivial arithmetic with essentially no accuracy benefit, an inference budget uncalibrated to actual question difficulty. And the broad empirical picture across several groups is an inverted-U — accuracy rises with reasoning length, peaks, and declines — with the peak arriving earlier on easier problems.

A mechanism, not a mystery

Here is a way to think about it that I find clarifying. Treat the reasoning trace as a random walk in the space of candidate conclusions. At each step the model can move towards the correct region or towards an attractor built out of whatever is salient in the prompt — a distractor number, a familiar-looking problem template, a spurious feature. Early steps have high drift towards correct, because the model is applying the skill it actually has. Later steps have lower drift, because the easy structure is exhausted, and the walk is increasingly governed by whatever attractors are nearby.

Under that view, accuracy at length T is the probability of being in the correct region at time T, and there is no reason for that to be monotone. If the correct region is not absorbing — if the model can revisit and revise a correct conclusion — then extra steps are extra opportunities to leave it. This predicts exactly what the empirical work finds: negative flips, where a model that had the right answer at step j abandons it by step j+m, and that easy problems cross into the harmful zone sooner because they reach the correct region sooner and then have longer to wander.

The theoretical side agrees on the precondition. Javanmard, Mirzasoleiman and Mirrokni, analysing test-time scaling for transformers trained on an in-context linear-regression task, prove that if the skills required by a downstream task are not sufficiently represented in the training data, increasing test-time compute can actively harm performance. Extra deliberation amplifies whatever procedure the model has. If the procedure is right, amplification is what you want; if it is subtly wrong, you are compounding an error with interest.

A compact statement of the whole hazard: test-time compute multiplies capability, it does not add it. Multiplying by something less than one makes the number smaller.

Allocate, don't extend

Put the pieces together and the conclusion is forced. Uniform budgets are wasteful in three separate ways at once: easy problems are solved in the first few samples and everything after is pure cost; hopeless problems consume budget forever and return nothing; and over-long traces on easy problems actively hurt. The frontier of this field is no longer "think longer". It is allocate.

The optimisation is a clean concave knapsack, and the solution is water-filling. Let problem i receive bi units of compute and let ai(b) be its expected accuracy, concave and increasing. Maximise the total subject to a budget:

max ∑i ai(bi)   s.t.   ∑i bi = B,   bi ≥ 0
∂/∂bi [ ∑ aj(bj) − λ(∑ bj − B) ] = 0  ⇒  ai′(bi) = λ for all funded iAt the optimum every funded problem has the same marginal return. λ is the shadow price of a unit of inference compute.

Now substitute the coverage model. With ai(b) = 1 − (1−pi)b, the marginal return is ai′(b) = (1−pi)b ln(1/(1−pi)). Setting that equal to λ and using ln(1/(1−p)) ≈ p for small p:

bi* ≈ (1/pi) · ln(pi / λ)  if pi > λ,   else 0The optimal per-problem sample count. Three regimes fall straight out of it.

Read the three regimes off the formula:

  • Easy (p near 1): the 1/p prefactor is small. A handful of samples, then stop. Ctest ↓
  • Hard but solvable (p small but above threshold): 1/p is large and the log is positive. This is where the compute goes. Ctest ↑
  • Effectively unsolvable (p ≤ λ): allocation is exactly zero. The formula refuses to spend, without needing a special case. Ctest ↛ ∞

There is a pleasing extra fact hiding in there. Differentiate b*(p) and you find the maximum at p = eλ, with peak allocation b*max = 1/(eλ). The problems that deserve the most compute are the ones sitting just above the give-up threshold — roughly 2.7× the shadow price. Not the hardest problems, and not the ones you are most likely to solve. The ones on the edge.

This is the theory behind a line of empirical work. Damani et al. learn a model that predicts the distribution of rewards given an input and a budget, then allocate accordingly; across programming, mathematics and dialogue they match non-adaptive quality using up to 50% less compute, or improve quality by up to 10% at fixed budget. In their routing setup they match the expensive decoder's performance while calling it only 50–75% of the time. Snell et al.'s 4× efficiency gain over best-of-N is the same idea arrived at from a different direction: their compute-optimal strategy is exactly a per-prompt allocation rule.

Nuance 09 · You do not know pi

Often said: just allocate compute according to problem difficulty.

Actually: the water-filling solution requires pi, which is exactly the thing you are trying to find out. Every practical system substitutes an estimate — a learned difficulty predictor, the model's own confidence, agreement among the first few samples, a bandit that explores allocations online. The gap between the oracle allocation and the achievable one is where all the remaining engineering lives, and it is why difficulty estimation is now as much a research area as generation. It also means allocation is sequential: you spend a little, observe agreement, and re-estimate, rather than committing a budget up front.

What to carry away

Six things, in rough order of how often I see them missed.

One. Coverage and accuracy are different quantities with different scaling behaviour. Coverage keeps rising for a very long time; the ability to identify which sample is correct usually does not. Any claim about test-time scaling should say which of the two it measured. pass@k is a capability measurement, not a deployable metric, unless you have a verifier that makes them equal.

Two. The smooth population-level scaling curve is produced by heterogeneity in problem difficulty, not by any single problem scaling smoothly. This is why the curve's exponent is a property of your benchmark as much as of your model, and why difficulty stratification should be table stakes in reporting.

Three. Verification is the binding constraint. With a perfect verifier, sampling scales beautifully; with a false-positive rate φ, accuracy is capped at p/(p + (1−p)φ) regardless of budget, and the cap tightens as problems get harder. Spending on a better verifier usually dominates spending on more samples.

Four. Serial and parallel compute are not interchangeable. They cost the same in weight FLOPs and KV bytes but differ by a factor of K in attention FLOPs and in latency, and they fail differently — width gives you candidates without an opinion, depth gives you an opinion without alternatives.

Five. More thinking can hurt, for reasons that are mechanistic rather than accidental: distractor capture, framing overfit, drift away from a correct conclusion already reached, and amplification of procedures the training data never properly installed. The inverted U is real, and its peak is earlier for easier problems.

Six. The interesting frontier is allocation, not extension. The optimal budget is per-prompt, it goes to zero for problems the model cannot solve, and it peaks just above that give-up threshold. Every system that does this well is really solving a difficulty-estimation problem in disguise.

And one open question I do not think anyone has answered. All the allocation theory above assumes accuracy is concave in compute, which is what makes water-filling correct. The inverse-scaling results say accuracy is sometimes not monotone, let alone concave. A genuinely correct allocation theory for the regime where extra compute can subtract value has not, as far as I can tell, been written down. That gap is where I would look next.
