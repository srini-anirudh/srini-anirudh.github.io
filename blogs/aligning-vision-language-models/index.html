Why text preferences are not enough

Show a vision-language model a photograph of a street and ask it to describe the scene. It might answer: “Three friends chat beside a shiny red car while a golden retriever waits by the door of a charming Parisian bakery.”

The sentence is fluent. It is specific. It reads like the work of a careful observer. The photograph shows two people, not three. There is no dog. Nothing in the picture says Paris. Only the red car is real.

This article asks one question. How do we teach a vision-language model to prefer answers that the image actually supports, and not merely answers that sound good?

For text-only language models, the standard answer is preference learning. People compare two responses. A reward model or a direct objective learns to make the preferred one more likely. Teaching a Model What We Prefer derives that machinery from first principles: the Bradley–Terry model, the reward model, the KL-regularised objective, and DPO. This article assumes it. Here we look only at what changes when the preference depends on an image.

What a text-only judge rewards

Imagine a judge who cannot see the photograph. It reads the question and two answers. One answer is short and plain. The other is long, confident and full of detail. Which one does the judge prefer?

Usually the detailed one. In text, detail and confidence correlate with quality, because a writer who knows more tends to say more. That correlation is exactly what a preference model learns. It is also exactly what breaks for images. A detailed description of a photograph is only good if the details are in the photograph. A judge that cannot see cannot tell.

So the first change is in the signature of the reward. For a language model the reward is a function of the prompt x and the response y. For a vision-language model it must also take the image I:

r = r(y, x, I)a reward that ignores I cannot measure whether y is true of I

This looks like a small change in notation. It is not. It gives us a simple test for any reward, any judge and any training signal in this article. Change the image and see whether the score changes. If r(y, x, I) = r(y, x, I′) for every pair of images, the reward is blind. It may still measure fluency or helpfulness. It does not measure grounding.

Nuance

A helpfulness score can rise while grounding gets worse. In LLaVA-RLHF, the policy trained against a standard reward model learned to write longer answers. Those scored better on LLaVA-Bench, a helpfulness benchmark, and worse on MMHal-Bench, a hallucination benchmark. In practice, never judge a multimodal alignment run by a helpfulness benchmark alone. Pair it with a hallucination benchmark.

Where this sits in the series

Alignment comes after visual instruction tuning. By then the model can follow instructions about images. It can also invent things about them. Evaluation of those skills is the subject of Measuring What a VLM Can Do. This article goes deeper into one part of evaluation, hallucination, because alignment methods are built around it. Later posts take the ideas further. When Language Must Point makes answers checkable by tying words to regions. Rewarding What the Model Sees replaces learned rewards with verifiable ones.

Language priors and visual evidence

Before we fix hallucination, we need a precise idea of what it is. The cleanest definition comes from looking at the two sources of information the model has.

A vision-language model computes a distribution over responses, P(y | x, I). Its language decoder was pretrained on trillions of text tokens. That pretraining gave it a strong opinion about what usually follows a question, even with no image at all. Call that opinion the language prior, P(y | x).

Take the question “What colour is the car?” Without an image, a model should spread its belief over common car colours: white, black, silver, grey. With an image of a red car, the belief should move almost entirely to red. The size of that move is the image’s contribution.

Splitting the log-probability

We can write the move exactly. Add and subtract the prior inside the logarithm:

log P(y | x, I) = log P(y | x) + log [ P(y | x, I) / P(y | x) ]

The first term is the language prior. The second term is the visual information gain of the answer. Information theorists call it the pointwise mutual information between the answer and the image, given the question. We will write it as g(y; I | x).

The split is an identity. It holds for every model. Its value is in what it lets us say:

  • If g is large and positive, the image made the answer more likely. The model looked.
  • If g is near zero, the answer is as likely with the image as without it. The model answered from the prior.
  • If g is negative, the image argued against the answer, but the prior was strong enough to win anyway.

This gives us a definition of grounding failure. A response is ungrounded when P(y | x, I) ≈ P(y | x) for the parts of y that make claims about the image. The whole distribution version of the same idea is a KL divergence, KL( P(· | x, I) ‖ P(· | x) ). When it is near zero, the image changed nothing.

Evidence that the prior often wins

This is not a hypothetical worry. It has been measured many times, in different forms.

In the visual question answering era, Goyal and colleagues built VQA v2 by pairing each question with two similar images that have different answers. Models scored much worse on the balanced data. They had been answering from question statistics. Agrawal and colleagues went further with VQA-CP, which changes the answer distribution between training and test. Models again dropped sharply.

Large vision-language models show the same pattern. The MMStar authors found that many benchmark questions can be answered without the image. GeminiPro scored 42.9% on MMMU with no visual input at all. Rohrbach and colleagues, who introduced the CHAIR metric for captioning, found that models which hallucinate more tend to make errors driven by language priors.

The details of which objects get invented also point to priors. The POPE study found that objects which occur often in training data, or often co-occur with objects in the image, are the ones most likely to be hallucinated. LURE added two more factors: hallucinations cluster on tokens where the model is uncertain, and they appear more often later in a long response. M3ID measured the last effect directly. As a model generates more tokens, its reliance on the image decreases, and that decline correlates with hallucination.

The intuition for the last finding is simple. Early in a response, the image is the only thing in context that says what to write. Later, the model’s own previous sentences are also in context. They are text, and the decoder is very good at continuing text.

Insight

Image reliance is not constant along a response. M3ID measured it falling as more tokens are generated, and LURE found hallucinations concentrated in the later part of long descriptions. A yes-or-no benchmark such as POPE asks for one token right after the image, so it never tests the regime where most hallucinations happen. In practice, evaluate and train on long-form descriptions as well as short questions.

Two different causes

It helps to separate two causes that produce the same symptom.

The first cause is missing evidence. The visual tokens never contained the information. Tong and colleagues found pairs of images that CLIP embeds almost identically even though they differ clearly to a person. They called them CLIP-blind pairs. Models built on CLIP encoders fail on questions about those differences. No amount of preference training on the language side can recover information the encoder threw away. That is an encoder problem, covered in Inside a Modern Vision Encoder.

The second cause is outweighed evidence. The information is in the visual tokens, but the decoder trusts its prior more. This is the case that alignment can fix. Most of the methods in this article try to increase g for true claims and decrease it for false ones.

For missing evidence, alignment can still help in one way. It can teach the model to notice that the evidence is weak and to say so. We return to that in the section on abstention.

Contrastive decoding as a diagnostic

The split also suggests a direct fix at inference time. If the problem is the prior, subtract it. Visual contrastive decoding (VCD) runs the model twice per token. Once with the real image I, and once with a distorted copy Ĩ made by adding noise. It then samples from

softmax[ (1 + α) · logit(yt | x, I, y<t) − α · logit(yt | x, Ĩ, y<t) ]

The noisy image keeps the prior and removes most of the evidence. Subtracting it amplifies whatever the clean image contributed. VCD adds a plausibility constraint so that it only reweights tokens the original model already found reasonable.

A family of related methods uses the same move. M3ID amplifies tokens with high mutual information with the image. Instruction contrastive decoding (ICD) contrasts against a disturbed instruction instead of a disturbed image. PAI increases attention to image tokens directly. OPERA penalises a pattern in which the model attends to a few summary tokens and neglects the image. Woodpecker and LURE take a different route and correct the text after generation.

For this article, these methods matter less as fixes and more as a lens. Each one defines grounding as the difference between what the model says with the image and what it says without it. The training methods in the section on multimodal DPO use exactly the same difference, but inside the loss instead of the decoder. Whether the model internally uses the image at all is the subject of What Happens Inside a VLM.

A taxonomy of visual hallucination

“Hallucination” covers many different errors. They have different causes, different detectors and different fixes. A preference dataset that only contains one kind will only teach the model to avoid that kind. So we need a list.

First, a definition. A visual hallucination is a claim about the image that the image does not support. There are two flavours. A contradicted claim conflicts with what the image shows: “the car is blue” when it is red. An unsupported claim cannot be checked against the image at all: “this bakery is famous for its croissants.” Both are failures of grounding. Only the first is a factual error about the pixels.

The seven kinds

Object existence. The response mentions an object that is not there. “A dog sits by the door.” This is the most studied kind, because it is the easiest to check against object annotations. It is strongly driven by co-occurrence. Dogs appear near doors and cars in many training images.

Attributes. The object exists but a property is wrong: colour, material, size, state. “A blue car.” Attribute errors are subtler, because the noun is correct and only one word is false.

Count. “Three people stand on the sidewalk.” Counting is hard for vision encoders that pool or compress patches. It is also hard for the decoder, which has a strong prior for round, common numbers.

Spatial relations. “The woman stands to the right of the car.” Both objects exist. The relation between them is wrong. Relations need the model to bind two objects and their positions, which is harder than recognising either one.

Text in the image. “The sign says it opens at 9.” The sign says 8. Reading errors matter a great deal for documents, charts and screenshots. The decoder’s prior for common phrases (“9 to 5”) competes with the pixels. Document reading is covered in How VLMs Read Documents.

Actions and events. “The man is crossing the street.” He is standing on the sidewalk. Action claims often go beyond a single frame. A still image rarely shows an event, so the model fills in a story.

External knowledge presented as evidence. “This Paris bakery is famous for its croissants.” The claim might even be true of some bakery. It is not something the image shows. The problem is not that the model used world knowledge. Using knowledge is often useful. The problem is that it presented knowledge as if it were observation.

How hallucination is measured

Benchmarks follow the same split. Some check object existence cheaply. Others try to cover more kinds at higher cost. The broader benchmark landscape is covered in Measuring What a VLM Can Do. Here are the ones that alignment papers report most often.

CHAIR (Rohrbach and colleagues, 2018) counts invented objects in captions against COCO object annotations. It has two forms:

CHAIRi = |hallucinated object mentions| / |all object mentions|     CHAIRs = |captions with a hallucinated object| / |all captions|

CHAIR has a known blind spot. A model that says less hallucinates less. A one-word caption scores perfectly. So CHAIR should always be reported next to a coverage or recall measure.

Object HalBench is the variant used by RLHF-V and many later papers. It applies the same COCO-based check to detailed descriptions produced under several prompts, and reports hallucination at the response level and the mention level.

POPE (Li and colleagues, 2023) turns existence into yes-or-no questions: “Is there a dog in the image?” The negative objects are sampled three ways: at random, from the most frequent objects, and adversarially from objects that co-occur with the real ones. The adversarial split targets the co-occurrence prior directly.

MMHal-Bench came with LLaVA-RLHF. It has 96 image-question pairs over eight question types, including attributes, adversarial objects, comparison, counting, spatial relations and holistic description. A text-only GPT-4 judge scores each answer against a human-written reference and the image’s object categories.

AMBER avoids an LLM judge. It evaluates both generative descriptions and discriminative questions, and covers existence, attribute and relation hallucination. It also reports coverage, which addresses CHAIR’s blind spot.

HallusionBench has 346 images and 1,129 hand-written questions arranged in control groups. Some questions are asked with an edited version of the image. That design separates two failure modes. In language hallucination the model answers from its prior and ignores the image. In visual illusion the model looks but misreads. In the original paper, GPT-4V reached 31.42% question-pair accuracy, and every other model scored below 16%.

Nuance

CHAIR and Object HalBench reward saying less. A caption that names fewer objects cannot score worse, and a one-word caption scores perfectly. A method can therefore “reduce hallucination” mainly by shortening its answers. In practice, report a coverage or recall measure, as AMBER does, next to any hallucination rate, and check average response length.

What a multimodal preference record contains

We now know what we want to discourage. The next question is what data teaches it. The unit of preference data for a vision-language model is a four-part record:

(I, x, yw, yl)image, prompt, preferred (“winning”) response, dispreferred (“losing”) response

It looks like the text record with one extra field. The extra field changes how the record is made, what it costs, and what can go wrong.

Judging requires looking

A text annotator can often judge two answers by reading them. A multimodal annotator must check each claim against the image. A sixty-word description might contain ten checkable claims. Each one needs a look at the right part of the picture. Small text and counts need a careful look. The cost of a label grows with the length of the response, not just with the number of pairs.

That cost shapes what annotators actually do. When checking is expensive, people fall back on cues they can see without checking: length, confidence, tone. Those are the same cues a text-only judge uses. A labelling protocol has to push against this.

Five things a judge can care about

When two responses about an image are compared, a judge may be weighing several separate qualities:

  • Correctness. Is every claim about the image true?
  • Grounding. Is every claim supported by the image, rather than by outside knowledge presented as observation?
  • Completeness. Does the response mention the things that matter in the image?
  • Helpfulness. Does it answer the question that was asked, in a useful form?
  • Safety. Is it appropriate to answer at all, given what the image shows and what was asked?

These qualities conflict. The sharpest conflict is between completeness and correctness. Every extra claim is another chance to be wrong. A model that describes less hallucinates less. So a preference signal that only punishes hallucination will push the model toward short, vague answers. A signal that only rewards detail will push it toward invention.

One practical answer is to give the qualities an order. LLaVA-RLHF, the first large multimodal model trained with RLHF, asked crowd workers to compare two responses and first pick the one with fewer hallucinations. Only if neither was clearly better on that count did they pick the more helpful one. The study collected about 10,000 such comparisons.

Correcting instead of ranking

RLHF-V changed the task itself. Annotators did not choose between two responses. They took one model response and edited the hallucinated segments to make it correct. The original becomes yl. The corrected version becomes yw.

This has two benefits. First, the annotator’s effort goes into the claims that are wrong, not into reading two whole responses. Second, the pair differs only where the error is. Everything else is identical. We will see in the section on fine-grained feedback why that matters so much for the gradient.

The RLHF-V data also tells us what goes wrong in practice. The authors collected 1.4k corrected responses, averaging 64.4 words with 2.65 corrected segments each. Of the corrections, 41.2% fixed objects, 20.3% positions, 16.5% numbers, 10.0% attributes and 5.3% actions. Object errors dominate, but more than half of the fixes are about something else.

Where the two responses come from

A preference pair needs two responses. Where they come from matters as much as how they are labelled.

On-policy pairs are sampled from the model being trained. They contain the errors that model actually makes. Off-policy pairs come from somewhere else: another model, a human writer, or a rewriting step. They are easier to produce in bulk. They may contain errors the model would never make, and miss errors it makes all the time.

Recent work argues that this choice is decisive. The OPA-DPO paper analysed several hallucination-focused DPO pipelines and concluded that results depend heavily on whether the data is on-policy with respect to the reference model. Off-policy responses have low probability under the reference, so the KL term in the objective resists learning from them. OPA-DPO uses expert corrections but first brings them onto the policy’s own distribution. With 4.8k examples it reported lower hallucination rates on AMBER and Object HalBench than the previous best method trained on 16k.

A second issue is style. If the preferred response was written by GPT-4 and the dispreferred one by the model, the two differ in vocabulary, length and rhythm, not only in truth. A learner can separate them by style alone. Several pipelines remove this shortcut on purpose. HA-DPO asks GPT-4 to rewrite both responses in a shared style. RLAIF-V samples both responses from the same model with the same decoding settings and different random seeds, and drops pairs whose lengths differ too much. We will see in the next sections why a style shortcut is especially damaging for images.

Insight

Two results point the same way. RLHF-V’s 1.4k corrections of the model’s own outputs beat LLaVA-RLHF’s roughly 10k comparisons. OPA-DPO’s 4.8k on-policy examples beat the previous best method, which used 16k samples. Neither paper isolates a single cause, but both datasets are small, on-policy and made of minimal corrections. In practice, spend an annotation budget on correcting the model’s own responses before spending it on more pairs.

How large these datasets are

Multimodal preference datasets are small by text standards, and they vary a lot in what each record contains. RLHF-V has 1.4k corrected responses. LLaVA-RLHF has about 10k comparisons. VLFeedback has over 82k instructions with responses from 12 models rated by GPT-4V. MM-RLHF has 120k fine-grained, human-annotated comparison pairs. SPA-VL, a safety dataset, has 100,788 records. The numbers are not directly comparable. A corrected response carries more information per record than a binary choice.

One more effect is easy to miss. Visual instruction tuning can degrade the language model’s text abilities, because visual question answering data is less varied than the text data the language model was trained on. Li and colleagues found that DPO on a small, 5k-sample VQA preference set annotated by Gemini restored and even improved text instruction following. Multimodal alignment repairs the language side too. The broader problem of keeping capabilities while adding new ones is covered in Mixing Data Without Losing Capabilities.

Reward models that must look at the image

In the classic RLHF pipeline, preference records train a reward model rφ(I, x, y). A policy is then optimised against it. For a vision-language model, the reward model is usually another vision-language model with a scalar head. It is trained with the same Bradley–Terry loss as in text:

L(φ) = − log σ( rφ(I, x, yw) − rφ(I, x, yl) )

The image appears in both terms. Nothing in the loss forces the model to use it.

The shortcut problem

A reward model will use whatever features separate yw from yl most cheaply. If preferred responses tend to be shorter, it learns length. If hallucinated responses tend to contain more adjectives, it learns adjectives. If the losing responses mention objects that are statistically unlikely, it learns object plausibility. None of these require looking at the image.

A reward model that learned these shortcuts reaches a good training loss. It may even score well on held-out pairs drawn from the same pipeline. Then the policy is optimised against it. The policy finds the shortcut quickly, because shortcuts are easy to produce. This is reward hacking, and it is the multimodal version of the pathologies described in the reward model section of the RLHF article.

LLaVA-RLHF documented this directly. Its reward model was initialised from the same supervised model whose hallucinations it was meant to detect, so it inherited the same blind spots. Under PPO, the policy learned to write longer responses. Those scored well on LLaVA-Bench and worse on MMHal-Bench.

Their fix is instructive. Factually Augmented RLHF gives the reward model extra text facts alongside the image: the five human captions for COCO images, and annotated rationales for A-OKVQA questions. It also adds a rule-based reward for questions with a known answer, and a length penalty. The extra facts make it easier for the reward model to notice a false claim. In effect, they hand it some of the evidence it was failing to extract from the pixels.

How well do reward models see?

Two recent benchmarks measure this directly. VL-RewardBench has 1,250 examples covering general queries, hallucination detection and reasoning. In the original paper, GPT-4o reached 65.4% accuracy, and strong open models struggled to beat random guessing. The authors found that judges fail mostly on basic visual perception, not on reasoning. Multimodal RewardBench has 5,211 expert-annotated triplets across six domains. The best models in that study, Gemini 1.5 Pro and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, reached about 72% overall.

These numbers matter because every pipeline in this article uses some kind of judge. A judge that is right seven times in ten is a noisy label source. Its errors are not random. They concentrate on the perception problems we most want to fix.

The field has moved toward generative reward models that write a critique before scoring. LLaVA-Critic is an open model trained to evaluate multimodal responses and to produce reward signals for preference learning. MM-RLHF trains a critique-based reward model on its human annotations. InternLM-XComposer2.5-Reward, Skywork-VL Reward and UnifiedReward are further examples of dedicated multimodal reward models. A critique makes the judgement inspectable. It does not, by itself, guarantee the judge looked.

Insight

Reward models fail at seeing, not at reasoning. VL-RewardBench found that judges fail mostly on basic visual perception tasks rather than on reasoning tasks. That is the opposite of what one might expect from strong language backbones, and it is exactly the failure that lets hallucinations through. In practice, a bigger or better-reasoning judge does not fix a perception gap. Give the judge evidence (as Fact-RLHF does), or audit it on perception directly.

An image-ablation test for any reward

The test from the first section turns into a simple audit. Score a set of responses three times: with the true image, with no image, and with a different image. Then ask two questions.

Does the reward move when the image is removed? A useful summary is the average absolute change, S = E | r(I, x, y) − r(∅, x, y) |. A reward with S ≈ 0 is not reading the image.

Does the ranking flip when the image is swapped for one in which a different response is correct? A grounded reward should change its mind. A shortcut reward will not.

The same audit applies to AI judges and to the implicit reward of a DPO-trained policy. It is cheap. It needs no new labels. It catches the most common failure before it is amplified by optimisation.

Multimodal DPO and the ignored image

Most recent work on hallucination skips the explicit reward model and uses Direct Preference Optimization. DPO turns the KL-regularised RLHF objective into a classification loss on preference pairs. The derivation is in the DPO section of the RLHF article. For images, we simply add I to every condition:

LDPO = −log σ( β log [πθ(yw|x,I) / πref(yw|x,I)] − β log [πθ(yl|x,I) / πref(yl|x,I)] )

Here πθ is the policy being trained, πref is the frozen starting model, and β sets how strongly the policy may move away from it. Each log-ratio term is DPO’s implicit reward for one response.

This is the obvious adaptation. It is also the one that early work used. And it has a hidden flaw.

Where the margin comes from

Apply the split from the section on language priors to the policy. The log-probability of any response is a text part plus a visual information gain:

log πθ(y | x, I) = log πθ(y | x) + gθ(y; I | x)

Do the same for the reference model and substitute both into the DPO margin. The margin separates into two parts:

margin = β · ( Δtext + Δimage )Δtext compares how the policy’s text-only probabilities moved for yw versus yl; Δimage compares how their visual information gains moved

The loss only sees the sum. It does not care which part supplies the margin. If the two responses can be told apart without the image, for example because one is longer, smoother or uses more common objects, then Δtext can grow and drive the loss down by itself. Gradient descent follows the easiest direction. It has no reason to grow Δimage.

Wang and colleagues called this the unconditional preference problem in the mDPO paper. They ran a direct test. They trained a model with standard multimodal DPO on 10k preference pairs. Then they trained the same model on the same pairs with every image removed. On MMHal-Bench, the version without images performed about the same, or slightly better. Standard DPO had been learning a text preference that happened to have images next to it.

Putting the image on the contrast

The fix follows from the algebra. We need pairs in which the text part cannot supply the margin. Keep the response fixed and change the image:

marginimage = β log [πθ(yw|x,I) / πref(yw|x,I)] − β log [πθ(yw|x,I′) / πref(yw|x,I′)]

Now substitute the split. The response and prompt are the same on both sides, so the text parts log πθ(yw | x) and log πref(yw | x) appear once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign. They cancel exactly. What is left is

marginimage = β [ gθ(yw; I | x) − gθ(yw; I′ | x) ] − (the same for πref)

The only way to lower this loss is to make the correct response depend more on the correct image. That is the property we wanted all along.

mDPO implements this as a conditional preference term. The rejected image I′ is the original with a random crop of up to 20% of its area. In its ablation, larger crops did slightly worse, and unrelated random images did worse still. A random image is too easy to tell apart, so it teaches little. A small crop removes some evidence while keeping the scene, which forces a finer distinction.

mDPO adds a second term, an anchor. DPO only enforces a relative margin. It can satisfy the loss by pushing both responses down, as long as the losing one falls further. That is the likelihood displacement problem discussed in the RLHF article. The anchor adds − log σ(β log [πθ(yw | x, I) / πref(yw | x, I)] − δ), with δ = 0 by default, which keeps the chosen response’s implicit reward positive. The full objective is the sum of the standard DPO term, the conditional term and the anchor. In the ablation, removing the conditional term hurt more than removing the anchor.

Insight

The no-image ablation is a cheap test of any multimodal DPO run. Train once with images and once with the images removed. If the two runs reach similar scores, as they did in mDPO’s experiment, the model learned a text preference. In practice, add image-side pairs until the run with images clearly beats the run without them.

Other ways to put the image on the contrast

Several methods reach the same idea by different routes. They differ mainly in how they make the second image, or in how they make the policy depend on the image.

V-DPO borrows classifier-free guidance from image generation. It reweights the policy by the ratio π(y | x, I) / π(y | x), where the no-image distribution is computed by zeroing the visual features. It also trains on image-contrast pairs made by inpainting: a language model proposes an unexpected object swap, a diffusion model edits that region, and CLIPScore filters the results. The authors found it learns especially well from those image-contrast pairs.

Re-Align retrieves images that are similar to the original according to the vision encoder. A retrieved image serves as the rejected image, with the chosen response held fixed. Its loss, rDPO, adds this visual term to the standard DPO term.

CHiP adds a visual preference module, so the model learns from textual and visual preferences together. It also optimises textual preferences at three granularities: response, segment and token.

TPO uses the image contrast inside each token, as we will see in the section on fine-grained feedback.

The common move is the one in the algebra. A preference pair that differs only in the image isolates the image’s contribution. A pair that differs only in the text lets the text do the work.

Constructing negatives

The last section showed one specific case of a general rule. Here is the rule. Preference optimisation learns the distinction that the negatives draw, and nothing else.

If every rejected response differs from the chosen one by an extra dog, the model learns not to invent dogs. If every rejected response differs by style, the model learns style. If the rejected response is a different image entirely, the model learns to tell a street from a kitchen, which it already knew. The quality of the negatives decides which errors the model can learn to avoid.

So negative construction is the core design decision in multimodal alignment, not a data-engineering detail. There are three families.

Text-side corruptions

Start with a correct response and edit it to introduce one specific error. Each edit type teaches one distinction:

  • Insert a non-existent object. Teaches existence. HALVA’s data-augmented phrase-level alignment (DPA) substitutes objects in two ways. Closed-set substitutes are concepts that often co-occur with the true ones. Open-set substitutes are sampled from a language model. Co-occurring substitutes are hard negatives, because they are exactly what the prior suggests.
  • Change a count. Teaches counting. “Two people” becomes “three people”.
  • Swap an attribute. Teaches attribute binding. “Red car” becomes “blue car”.
  • Flip a relation. Teaches spatial binding. “Left of” becomes “right of”.
  • Corrupt text in the image. Teaches reading. “Open from 8 to 6” becomes “open from 9 to 5”. The corruption should be a plausible phrase, or the model learns to reject odd strings rather than to read.
  • Add an unsupported detail. Teaches the line between evidence and knowledge. “… famous for its croissants.”

Several pipelines generate such edits with a strong model. POVID prompts GPT-4V to inject plausible hallucinations into the correct answer. BPO uses a text-only LLM to inject “erroneous but common” elements. HA-DPO uses GPT-4 to find and correct hallucinated sentences in model outputs, against Visual Genome annotations. Before preference learning became common, LRV-Instruction used the same idea in supervised data. It added negative instructions that ask about non-existent objects, manipulate existing objects, or manipulate knowledge.

Model-generated negatives

The second family samples the rejected response from the model itself and labels it. RLAIF-V, CSR and OPA-DPO work this way. The errors are real errors of this model, which is what we want to remove. The responses are on-policy. The cost is that something must label them, which moves the problem to the judge (see the section on AI feedback).

Image-side negatives

The third family changes the image, not the text. There are two uses. One is the conditional pair from the last section: same response, worse image. The other uses the worse image to elicit a bad response, which then becomes the rejected text.

  • Noise. SeVa feeds the model a noised image and takes its answer as the rejected response. Its ablation found that medium-strength diffusion noise worked best. Very weak augmentations, such as random flips, and very strong noise both did worse. About half of its pairs were discarded because the two answers were identical. POVID and BPO also use noise to trigger the model’s prior-driven answers. TPO adds noise to compute its token rewards.
  • Crop. mDPO removes up to 20% of the image. The scene stays recognisable and some evidence disappears.
  • Retrieval. Re-Align picks a visually similar image from a pool. The rejected image is natural, not synthetic.
  • Editing. V-DPO inpaints a region to swap one object for another. This is the most targeted image-side negative. It changes exactly one fact.
  • Mismatched images. Pairing the response with an unrelated image is the easiest option. mDPO’s ablation suggests it is too easy to be useful.

How hard should a negative be?

Two ablations in this section point the same way. SeVa found medium noise best. mDPO found a small crop better than a large crop, and both better than a random image. A negative that is too easy carries little information, because the model already separates it. A negative that is too hard becomes label noise. A heavily noised image may still support the original caption, or may not. Neither the model nor the label knows.

A useful working rule is to aim negatives at the model’s actual mistakes. On-policy sampling does this automatically. Co-occurrence substitutions do it for existence errors. Small crops and targeted edits do it for image dependence.

Nuance

Harder negatives are not always better. SeVa found medium-strength noise better than both weak and strong noise. mDPO found a crop of up to 20% better than a crop of 20–50%, and both better than an unrelated image. Past some point, a corrupted image may no longer contradict the caption, so the “negative” is mislabelled. In practice, treat negative difficulty as a hyperparameter and sweep it.

Fine-grained feedback

Consider a sixty-word description with one wrong phrase: “holding a red umbrella”, when the woman is holding a phone. A response-level preference says only that this response is worse than another. It does not say which words made it worse. The learner has to work that out from many examples. This is a credit-assignment problem.

Where the DPO gradient goes

The log-probability of a response is a sum over its tokens:

log πθ(y | x, I) = ∑t log πθ(yt | x, I, y<t)

So the DPO gradient pushes up every token of yw and pushes down every token of yl, all with the same weight. Most of those tokens are not about the error.

There is a useful exception. Suppose the two responses share an identical prefix. For each prefix token, the context is the same in both responses and the token is the same. Its gradient appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign. It cancels exactly. Tokens after the edit have slightly different contexts, so they almost cancel. The signal concentrates on the edit.

This is the deeper reason why correction-based data, like RLHF-V’s, works so well with so few examples. A minimal edit is a minimal pair. A rewritten response is not. If yw was written from scratch, nothing cancels, and the gradient is spread over every word.

Insight

The cancellation of the shared prefix is a property of the loss, not of the data source. It gives correction-based data a built-in advantage: the gradient is already concentrated on the edit before any weighting is applied. Rewritten pairs lose this, even when the rewrite fixes the same error. In practice, when a strong model fixes a response, ask it to make the smallest edit that removes the error, not to rewrite.

Weighting the segments that changed

RLHF-V goes one step further with Dense DPO. It splits a response into unchanged tokens yu and corrected tokens yc, and scores the response with a weighted average:

log π(y | x) = (1/N) [ ∑yi∈yu log p(yi | x, y<i) + γ ∑yi∈yc log p(yi | x, y<i) ],   N = |yu| + γ |yc|

With γ = 5, the corrected tokens count five times as much. Dividing by N keeps longer responses from scoring higher just for being long. With 1.4k annotated samples, RLHF-V reported a 34.8% reduction in the base model’s hallucination rate, and outperformed LLaVA-RLHF, which used about 10k comparisons.

A spectrum of granularity

Other methods place the signal at different granularities.

Segment or sentence level. FDPO, from the M-HalDetect paper, trains on 16k fine-grained human annotations of object, description and relation errors in InstructBLIP outputs. FGAIF gets segment labels from AI tools instead. ChatGPT extracts atomic facts, LLaVA-1.5 checks each fact against the image, and three reward models, one each for existence, attribute and relation, provide dense rewards to PPO. HSA-DPO detects hallucinated sentences, rewrites them, and weights each rejected response by the severity of its errors on a scale from 1 to 3.

Phrase level. HALVA’s DPA loss lowers the probability of the hallucinated phrase relative to the correct phrase. It touches only those tokens. A token-wise KL term to the reference model keeps the rest of the distribution where it was.

Token level. TPO computes a reward for every token without any span labels. For each token, it compares the logit under the clean image with the logit under a noised image. Tokens whose probability depends on the image get a larger weight. CHiP combines response, segment and token levels in one objective.

Fine-grained signals are not free of problems. Span labels cost more to collect than a single choice. The label-free token rewards measure something slightly different from correctness. A token can depend strongly on the image and still be wrong, for example when the model misreads a sign. Image sensitivity is a proxy for grounding, not a guarantee of it.

AI feedback and its failure modes

Human checking is slow. So most recent pipelines replace the human with a model. The model might be a stronger proprietary system, an open model, a contrastive embedding model, or the policy itself. The general case for AI feedback is covered in the RLHF article. Here we look at what is specific to images.

Four kinds of AI judge

A stronger proprietary model. Silkie built the VLFeedback dataset by sampling responses from 12 vision-language models and asking GPT-4V to rate each one for helpfulness, visual faithfulness and ethical considerations. DPO on this data was distilled into Qwen-VL-Chat. HA-DPO and POVID also rely on GPT-4-class models, to correct responses and to inject errors.

An open model, used carefully. RLAIF-V showed how to get reliable labels from weaker open models. Its key move is divide and conquer. A language model splits each response into atomic claims. Each claim becomes a yes-or-no question. An open vision-language model answers each question, and a claim is rejected when the model’s probability of “no” exceeds its probability of “yes”. A response’s score is minus the number of rejected claims. Judging one small claim is much easier than judging a whole paragraph. In the 7B setting, LLaVA-NeXT 34B labelled responses from LLaVA-1.5 7B. RLAIF-V ran four rounds, each sampling fresh responses from the current model.

The policy itself. In RLAIF-V’s 12B setting, the model labelled its own responses. CSR also uses self-rewarding. It builds responses sentence by sentence and scores each sentence with a weighted sum: 0.9 times a CLIP image-text similarity and 0.1 times the model’s own likelihood. The CLIP term is the visual constraint. It ties the self-reward to the image.

A contrastive embedding model. CLIP-DPO ranks the model’s own candidate responses by CLIP similarity to the image, filters them with rules, and uses the result as DPO pairs. No generative judge is involved.

How AI feedback goes wrong

An AI judge has all the problems of a reward model, plus some of its own.

The judge hallucinates. A judge that misreads the image will reward responses that share its misreading. The MLLM-as-a-Judge benchmark found that multimodal judges agree reasonably with humans on pairwise comparison, but diverge on scoring and batch ranking, and show hallucination and inconsistency in their judgements.

The judge has biases. Text judges prefer the first position, longer answers and their own outputs. Zheng and colleagues documented these position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases. Multimodal judges inherit them. A preference for detail is especially harmful here, because detail is where hallucination lives.

The judge shares the policy’s blind spots. Many judges and policies use similar CLIP-style encoders. The Eyes Wide Shut study found that the visual patterns that defeat CLIP are the same ones that defeat the multimodal models built on it. If neither the judge nor the policy can see a difference, the judge cannot teach it.

Loops amplify errors. Iterative pipelines sample from the current model, label with the judge, train, and repeat. An error type the judge detects reliably shrinks each round. An error type the judge misses gets no corrective signal. If the judge also prefers responses that happen to contain that error, for example because detailed answers are more likely to include a wrong count, the error grows.

Nuance

Self-feedback can work, with a caveat. RLAIF-V’s 12B model improved by labelling its own responses, and CSR improved for several rounds with self-rewards. Both constrain the judgement: RLAIF-V asks small yes-or-no questions about single claims, and CSR adds a CLIP image score. Neither result shows that an unconstrained self-judge is safe. In practice, if the policy judges itself, break the judgement into checks that its perception can actually answer.

Making AI feedback safer

The fixes follow from the failure modes.

  • Make each judgement small. Claim-level questions, as in RLAIF-V, are easier to answer correctly than whole-response comparisons.
  • Use specialised checkers where they exist. Detectors for existence, OCR for text, counters for counts. This moves feedback from a learned opinion toward a check. Rewarding What the Model Sees takes this to its conclusion with verifiable rewards.
  • Remove style from the comparison. Sample both responses from the same model with the same settings, as RLAIF-V does, so the judge’s style preferences have nothing to grab.
  • Audit the judge. Run the image-ablation test from the reward model section. Keep a small human-labelled set to estimate the judge’s error rate per hallucination type.
  • Refresh on-policy. Sampling new responses each round keeps the data matched to the current model. It also means the judge’s blind spots are tested against the model’s current errors, not old ones.

Multimodal safety

Everything so far has been about truth. Alignment also covers harm. Images make safety harder in a specific way. They add a second input channel, and the safety training of the language model was done almost entirely on text.

Why text safety does not carry over

Two findings explain most of the problem.

First, visual instruction tuning can erase safety. The VLGuard study found that vision-language fine-tuning can cause the language model to forget its safety alignment, partly because some instruction data contains harmful content. A model whose language backbone refused a request may comply after multimodal training.

Second, safety behaviour is not triggered by visual tokens in the same way as by text tokens. The FigStep authors analysed embeddings and attributed their attack’s success to missing safety alignment for visual embeddings. ECSO, a defence, gives supporting evidence from the other direction. It converts an unsafe image into a text description and lets the language model answer from the text. That alone restored much of the safety behaviour. The language model’s safety still existed. The image route bypassed it.

Insight

Images bypass the language model’s safety training rather than destroying it. ECSO restored much of the safe behaviour just by turning the image into a text description first. FigStep’s analysis pointed to missing safety alignment for visual embeddings. In practice, safety data must include images, and text that arrives through an image should pass through the same policy as typed text.

The threats

Harmful requests about harmful images. The simplest case is a request about an image of something dangerous. MM-SafetyBench found that query-relevant images can compromise models as if the text itself were malicious. It covers 13 scenarios with 5,040 text-image pairs.

Text hidden in images. FigStep writes a prohibited request into an image as typography, for example as the title of a numbered list with empty items. The text prompt asks only to fill in the list. A text safety filter sees nothing wrong. FigStep reported an average attack success rate of 82.50% across six open models. HADES hides and amplifies harmful intent through crafted images.

Typographic attacks. Text written in an image can override what the image shows. This was first observed in CLIP: an apple with a handwritten label reading “iPod” was classified as an iPod. Large vision-language models are also vulnerable. Cheng and colleagues verified the attack on commercial and open models. Qraitem and colleagues had models generate their own misleading labels and reported classification drops of up to 60%.

Instructions inside screenshots and documents. A screenshot, a scanned page or a web page can contain text addressed to the model. This is indirect prompt injection, described for text applications by Greshake and colleagues, arriving through the visual channel. Bagdasaryan and colleagues showed the instruction can be hidden in an adversarial perturbation that a person cannot read. For agents that act on screens, the stakes are higher. Adversarial pop-ups in OSWorld and VisualWebArena were clicked in 86% of cases on average and cut task success by 47%. Simply telling the agent to ignore pop-ups did not help. Agent safety is covered in VLMs That Act.

Adversarial images. Images are continuous and high-dimensional, which makes them easy to optimise against. Qi and colleagues found a single adversarial image that universally jailbroke an aligned model. Image hijacks controlled model outputs with small perturbations and success rates above 80% on LLaVA. Jailbreak in Pieces needs access only to the vision encoder, not the language model.

Safe parts, unsafe whole. A benign question and a benign image can combine into an unsafe request. SIUO (“safe inputs but unsafe output”) measures this across nine domains. MSSBench measures the mirror case, situational safety. It has 1,820 query-image pairs in which the same kind of request is safe in one visual context and unsafe in another.

Privacy. Images contain faces, documents, licence plates and backgrounds. A model can identify, read or infer. Private attribute inference from images reached up to 77.6% accuracy on attributes of the person who posted the image, using images that do not directly show that person. Accuracy scaled with general model capability.

Mitigations

Safety data in multimodal training. VLGuard showed that mixing a vision-language safety dataset into fine-tuning, or applying it afterwards, restored safe behaviour with minimal loss of helpfulness. SPA-VL provides safety preference data: 100,788 records across 6 harm domains, 13 categories and 53 subcategories, with responses from 12 models. MM-RLHF reported a 60% safety improvement for LLaVA-OneVision 7B.

Guard models. Llama Guard 3 Vision classifies multimodal prompts and responses against a hazard taxonomy. ShieldGemma 2 moderates image content. These sit outside the model and can be updated separately.

Inference-time defences. ECSO routes unsafe images through text. AdaShield prepends adaptive defence prompts against structure-based attacks such as FigStep.

Provenance. The most general rule for injection is to treat text that came from an image as data, not as instructions. A model can be trained to quote, summarise or flag such text, and not to obey it. This is the same principle as for retrieved documents, applied to pixels.

Measuring over-refusal. A safety signal that ignores the image produces a different failure: refusing benign requests because a word looks risky. A kitchen knife in a cooking photo is not a threat. Safety, like truth, must be image-conditioned. MSSBench measures both sides by pairing safe and unsafe contexts.

Knowing when not to answer

The last kind of alignment is the quietest. Sometimes the right answer is “I can’t determine that from the image.” The photo is blurred. The object is cut off. The text is too small to read. The question asks about something that is not there.

This is not a corner case. VizWiz collected over 31,000 visual questions from blind people who took photos with their phones and asked spoken questions about them. Many of the images are poor quality, and many of the questions cannot be answered from the image. A model that always answers will be confidently wrong on exactly those questions, for exactly the users who cannot check.

When should a model abstain?

We can derive the rule. Suppose a correct answer is worth +1, a wrong answer costs c, and abstaining is worth 0. If the model believes its answer is correct with probability p, the expected value of answering is

E[answer] = p · 1 − (1 − p) · c

Answering beats abstaining when this is positive:

p > c / (1 + c)

When a wrong answer costs the same as a right answer gains (c = 1), the threshold is 0.5. When a wrong answer is four times as costly (c = 4), the threshold is 0.8. A medical or accessibility setting has a high c. A casual chat has a low one.

Whitehead and colleagues formalised this for visual question answering. They measure coverage, the fraction of questions answered, and risk, the error rate on the answered ones. Their Effective Reliability score gives a positive score for correct answers, a penalty of c for wrong ones, and zero for abstentions. They also found the main obstacle. Using the model’s raw softmax confidence to abstain, the best models could answer fewer than 7.5% of VQA v2 questions while keeping risk at 1%. A learned selection function that looks at the image, the question and the answer raised coverage at 1% risk from 6.8% to 15.6%.

Nuance

The threshold c / (1 + c) is only correct if the confidence is calibrated. Whitehead and colleagues’ result shows that raw softmax confidence is not: it allowed fewer than 7.5% of questions to be answered at 1% risk. In practice, train a separate selector, or train the model on unanswerable examples, before setting a threshold from the cost.

Abstention must also be image-conditioned

Calibration for a vision-language model has a specific requirement. Confidence should drop when the visual evidence is missing, not when the question is merely unusual. A model that hedges on rare words but answers confidently about a blurred photo is calibrated to the wrong thing.

Several datasets train or test this. UNK-VQA perturbs either the image or the question so that the pair becomes unanswerable while staying close to the original. MM-UPD tests three kinds of unsolvable multiple-choice problems: the correct answer is absent, the answer set does not fit the question, and the image does not fit the question. Most models that score well on standard benchmarks struggled on it. LRV-Instruction’s negative instructions, which ask about objects that are not present, teach the model that “no” and “not visible” are valid answers.

The image-side negatives from the section on negatives fit this purpose directly. Take an answerable pair, crop away the region that contains the answer, and make “I can’t tell from this image” the preferred response. The model then learns that its confidence should follow the evidence.

There is a balance to keep. A model rewarded only for avoiding errors learns to abstain from everything, in the same way that CHAIR rewards a one-word caption. Abstention has to be paid for in coverage, and the cost c has to be stated explicitly.

Making answers checkable

Abstention handles the case where the model cannot answer. A related tool handles the case where the user cannot verify. If the model points to the region that supports each claim, a person can check it at a glance. That turns grounding from a hidden property of the model into a visible property of the answer. When Language Must Point covers how models output boxes, points and masks. Building Grounded Document Intelligence shows the same idea applied to documents, with masks that mark the evidence for each answer.

What to take away

We started with one question: how to teach a model to prefer answers the image supports. The answer has several parts, and they fit together.

The reward must depend on the image, and we can test whether it does by changing the image. Grounding failure has a clean definition: the answer is as likely without the image as with it. Hallucination comes in at least seven kinds, and benchmarks cover them unevenly. Preference data is only as good as the contrast inside each pair. If the pair differs in style, the model learns style. If it differs only in the image, the model must use the image.

A practical recipe follows from this:

  1. Measure image dependence first. Run blind and corrupted-image baselines on your evaluation set.
  2. Prefer on-policy pairs with minimal edits. Correct the model’s own outputs rather than writing new ones.
  3. Add image-side pairs, so that some of the margin can only come from the image.
  4. Push the signal to the span that is wrong, through minimal pairs, segment weights or token weights.
  5. Audit every judge and reward model with the image-ablation test before optimising against it.
  6. Report hallucination together with coverage, and abstention together with its cost.
  7. Put safety data into every multimodal training stage, and treat text inside images as data.

Several problems remain open. Reward models still fail on basic perception. Judges and policies share encoders and blind spots. Alignment methods concentrate on object errors and say little about counts, reading and actions. Multi-image and video settings add new ways to be wrong; MIA-DPO is an early step for multiple images, and Seeing in Time covers video. Agents that act on screens turn a hallucination into a wrong click.

The next article, When Language Must Point, takes the most direct route to grounding: making the model say where each claim comes from. After that, Rewarding What the Model Sees replaces learned preferences with rewards that can be checked.

Key insights and nuances

  • Removing the images barely changed standard multimodal DPO. In mDPO’s test, training on the same pairs with no images matched or slightly beat training with them on MMHal-Bench. Always run that ablation on your own pipeline. (Multimodal DPO)
  • Image-side pairs cancel the language prior exactly. With the response fixed and only the image changed, every text-only term drops out of the DPO margin. (Multimodal DPO)
  • A minimal edit concentrates the gradient on the error. Identical prefix tokens cancel in the DPO gradient, which helps explain why 1.4k corrections in RLHF-V beat about 10k comparisons in LLaVA-RLHF. (Fine-grained feedback)
  • On-policy data beat larger off-policy data. OPA-DPO with 4.8k on-policy examples beat the previous best method, which used 16k samples. The source of the responses matters as much as the label. (Preference data)
  • Negatives have a best difficulty. Medium noise beat weak and strong noise in SeVa. A small crop beat a large crop and a random image in mDPO. Tune negative difficulty like a hyperparameter. (Negatives)
  • Reward models fail at seeing, not at reasoning. VL-RewardBench found that judges fail mostly on basic perception. Audit a judge with the image-ablation test before trusting it. (Reward models)
  • Image reliance decays along a response. M3ID and LURE found hallucination concentrated later in long outputs. Short yes-or-no benchmarks such as POPE do not test that regime. (Language priors)
  • CHAIR rewards silence. A shorter caption cannot score worse on CHAIR. Report hallucination together with coverage. (Taxonomy)
  • Language-model safety is bypassed, not destroyed, by images. ECSO restored much of the safety behaviour simply by converting the image to text. Route image content through the same safety policy as text. (Safety)
  • Raw softmax confidence is a poor abstention signal. In Whitehead and colleagues’ study, softmax thresholds answered fewer than 7.5% of questions at 1% risk. A learned, image-aware selector roughly doubled coverage. (Abstention)
