The premise: arithmetic is free

An H100 can do roughly 990 trillion bfloat16 multiply-accumulates per second. It can read about 3.35 trillion bytes per second from its own memory. Every other number in distributed training falls out of that gap.

Divide the two and you get the only constant that matters: 295. For every byte an H100 pulls out of HBM, it must perform about 295 floating-point operations or the tensor cores sit idle. That number is not a tuning knob. It is the shape of the machine.

Now move the byte further away. Pull it across NVLink from a neighbouring GPU and the break-even ratio jumps to about 2,200 FLOP per byte. Pull it across the network from another node and it is closer to 20,000. The arithmetic did not get cheaper — the byte got more expensive, and it got more expensive in a very particular, very structured way that we can write down exactly.

Nuance 01 Arithmetic intensity is per level, not per chip

Often saidAn H100 needs 295 FLOPs per byte to break even.

ActuallyOnly against its own HBM. The break-even ratio is a property of the level, not the chip: roughly 33 FLOP/byte against shared memory, 295 against HBM, 2,200 against NVLink and about 19,800 against the fabric. A kernel that is comfortably compute-bound on HBM can be catastrophically communication-bound the moment its operand has to cross a node boundary. Whenever anyone quotes an arithmetic intensity, ask which level it is measured against.

Notice how uneven the ladder is. The first three rungs span a factor of nine. The step from HBM to NVLink is another 7×. The step from NVLink to the network is another 9×. There is no smooth gradient here — there are cliffs, and the entire discipline of parallelism strategy is the art of arranging your computation so that the heavy traffic stays above the cliffs and only the light traffic goes over them.

This article walks down the whole ladder from first principles. We will build a cost model that predicts collective runtimes to within a factor of two, derive the ring AllReduce and prove it is bandwidth-optimal, work out exactly which collective fires where in data, tensor, pipeline, context and expert parallelism, and finish with the failure modes that make real clusters slower than the arithmetic says they should be.

The latency ladder

Bandwidth is what you feel on big transfers. Latency is what you feel on small ones, and small ones are far more common than people expect — every synchronisation point, every gradient bucket flush, every pipeline bubble is a latency-dominated event.

What sets the floor

Before we reach for models, it is worth knowing which parts of that ladder are negotiable and which are physics. Any transfer decomposes into three additive terms.

Propagation delay
Distance divided by signal velocity. In optical fibre light travels at roughly c/1.47 ≈ 2.04×108 m/s, which works out to a convenient 4.9 nanoseconds per metre. A 100 m cable across a datacentre hall costs about 0.5 µs one way, 1 µs round trip. This term is irreducible. No protocol, no firmware, no amount of money changes it.
Serialisation delay
Message size divided by link rate — the time to clock the bits onto the wire one after another. A 1 MiB message on a 400 Gb/s link takes 21 µs to emit. This is the term bandwidth buys down.
Processing and queueing
Everything else: doorbell writes, DMA setup, switch store-and-forward, protocol headers, adaptive-routing decisions, kernel launch, and — usually the largest single contributor on small messages — waiting behind someone else's packets.

One consequence deserves stating on its own line, because it catches people out constantly. Bandwidth-delay product — link rate times round-trip time — is the number of bytes that must be in flight simultaneously to keep a link busy. On a 400 Gb/s link with a 10 µs round trip that is 500 KB. If your messages are smaller than that and you wait for each one, you will use a fraction of the link no matter what the sticker says. This is why RDMA fabrics lean so hard on deep queues and many outstanding operations, and why a collective that issues one small message at a time is a catastrophe.

The α–β cost model

Collapse the previous section into two parameters and you get the model that the entire MPI and NCCL literature is built on. Sending n bytes between two endpoints costs

T(n) = α + β·n
α = per-message latency (seconds) · β = inverse bandwidth (seconds per byte)

α absorbs propagation, protocol overhead, kernel launch and queueing. β is one over the achievable link bandwidth. The half-power message size we plotted above is simply n½ = α/β: the size at which the fixed cost equals the variable cost, and therefore the size at which you are getting half the link's peak. Below it you are buying latency; above it you are buying bandwidth.

Reductions do arithmetic on the way, so for reducing collectives we add a third term:

T(n) = α + β·n + γ·n
γ = seconds per byte of reduction arithmetic

On GPUs γ is almost always negligible against β — summing bf16 at 3.35 TB/s of HBM bandwidth is far faster than moving the same bytes over a 50 GB/s NIC — so I will carry the γ term through the derivations for correctness and then drop it in the numerics. On CPU-based clusters, or when the reduction is something expensive like a sorted merge, it stops being negligible.

Nuance 02 Latency is a software property, not a distance

Often saidInter-GPU latency is dominated by how far the signal has to travel.

ActuallyLight in fibre covers a metre in about 5 ns, so the wire accounts for single-digit nanoseconds inside a rack — yet NVLink's α is around 2 µs, three orders of magnitude more. Almost all of it is software: the collective library's kernel launch, the proxy thread posting a work request, the doorbell MMIO write, the NIC's DMA read of the descriptor. That is why α differs by 6× between fabrics whose cables are comparable, why NCCL_PROTO=LL exists, why CUDA graphs help small collectives so much, and why a busy CPU can silently double your latency.

Inside the die

The hierarchy does not start at the package boundary. A single H100 is already a distributed system with 132 independent processors, a shared interconnect, a contended memory, and a NUMA problem — and every lesson from the cluster scale has a miniature version here.

Working outward: each streaming multiprocessor has a 256 KB register file, which is the only storage in the machine fast enough that data movement is genuinely free. Below it sits 256 KB of combined L1 and shared memory per SM, where shared memory is the part you address explicitly. Below that is a 50 MB L2, physically split into two partitions joined by an on-die link — crossing that link costs roughly twice a local L2 hit, which is a NUMA effect inside a single chip. Below that, five HBM3 stacks delivering 3.35 TB/s in aggregate.

Two structural facts here reappear at every larger scale. First, the fast memory is private and the slow memory is shared. 132 SMs each own a register file, and all 132 contend for one HBM budget: if every SM misses to memory simultaneously, each gets about 25 GB/s. Second, there is a topology, and it is not uniform. Crossing between L2 partitions costs roughly double a local hit. Substitute "GPU" for "SM" and "NVLink" for "L2 crossbar" and you have described a DGX node.

NVLink and NVSwitch

Once you leave the die, the first question is how eight GPUs in a chassis talk to each other. The answer has changed four times, and the shape of that change is instructive: it is a steady march from topology you must reason about toward topology you can ignore.

The link itself

An NVLink is a bundle of differential pairs carrying a lightweight, memory-semantic protocol — loads, stores and atomics rather than packets with big headers. NVLink 4, on Hopper, uses 18 links per GPU. Each link carries 25 GB/s in each direction, giving 450 GB/s per direction per GPU, or 900 GB/s counting both directions at once.

Nuance 03 The NVLink-to-network gap is 9×, not 18×

Often saidNVLink is roughly 18× faster than the network on a p5 node.

ActuallyThat figure mixes two conventions. NVIDIA quotes NVLink 4 as 900 GB/s by summing both directions; network vendors quote a 400 Gb/s port as 400 Gb/s in each direction. Compared like with like — 450 GB/s per direction against 50 GB/s per direction — the honest ratio is 9×. That is still an enormous cliff and still the single most important number when choosing a parallelism strategy. It just is not eighteen, and the factor of two matters when you are deciding whether a strategy fits.

The reason NVSwitch matters is not that it is faster than a mesh in the best case — a direct mesh link is as fast as a switch port. It is that it makes every case the best case. On a Volta mesh, a ring collective had to be laid out along physical links or it would traverse two hops and halve its bandwidth; NCCL contained substantial machinery to discover the topology and construct rings that respected it. With a full crossbar, any permutation of GPUs achieves full bandwidth, and the collective library's job collapses from "find a good embedding" to "pick any ring."

The current extreme of this idea is rack-scale NVLink: GB200 NVL72 puts 72 GPUs into one NVLink domain with a switch fabric between racks, which turns what used to be a network problem into an intra-node problem for models that fit inside 72 GPUs. That is a topology change with real consequences for parallelism strategy, and we will come back to it.

PCIe, and why it hurts

PCIe is the connective tissue of the node: GPU to CPU, CPU to NIC, and on machines without NVSwitch, GPU to GPU. It is also, by a wide margin, the narrowest pipe that carries training traffic, and understanding exactly why requires going down to the lane.

A PCIe lane is one differential pair in each direction. Gen5 clocks it at 32 GT/s — 32 billion symbols per second. But not every symbol is payload. Through Gen5, PCIe uses 128b/130b encoding: every 130 bits on the wire carry 128 bits of data plus a 2-bit sync header. So one Gen5 lane delivers

32 × 10⁹ × (128/130) / 8 = 3.94 GB/s per lane per direction

Multiply by 16 lanes and you get 63 GB/s each way for a x16 slot. Gen6 changes the recipe — PAM4 signalling puts two bits per symbol so 64 GT/s comes from the same 32 GBd baud rate, and the 128b/130b scheme is replaced by fixed 256-byte FLITs with built-in forward error correction — landing at roughly 121 GB/s.

The practical consequence is that on any node where the NIC hangs off the same PCIe root complex as the GPU, the network's ceiling is PCIe's ceiling. A p5 instance pairs each pair of GPUs with enough EFA capacity that this is balanced, but on ad-hoc clusters it is extremely common to find a 400 Gb/s NIC (50 GB/s) sitting behind a Gen4 x16 link (31.5 GB/s), which quietly caps the network at 63% of its rating. Check lspci -vv before you trust a spec sheet.

Nuance 04 PCIe headline rates are pre-encoding, and the encoding changed

Often saidGen5 ×16 gives you 64 GB/s.

Actually32 GT/s across 16 lanes is 64 GB/s of symbols. Through Gen5 every 130 bits on the wire carry 128 bits of payload, so the usable figure is 63.0 GB/s — and that is before TLP headers, which cost a further few percent on small transfers. Gen6 drops 128b/130b for PAM4 with FLIT-mode framing and its own forward error correction, so the familiar 1.5% rule of thumb does not carry forward. Quote the post-encoding number, or say which one you mean.

NIC, RDMA, InfiniBand, EFA

Leaving the node means going through a network interface card, and the interesting question is how much of the host has to be involved. The answer, in a well-built cluster, is: almost none of it.

Three ideas stacked together

Kernel bypass
The application maps the NIC's registers and queues into its own address space. Posting a send is a write to a memory-mapped doorbell, not a system call. This removes context switches and interrupt latency from the critical path — worth several microseconds.
Zero copy
The NIC DMAs directly out of the buffer you registered, and DMAs directly into the receiver's registered buffer. No copy into a socket buffer, no copy out.
One-sided operations
An RDMA WRITE names a remote address and a remote key. The receiving NIC performs the write; the receiving CPU is never notified and never scheduled. This is what makes RDMA fundamentally different from a fast socket: the remote side's software is not on the critical path at all.

Add GPUDirect RDMA and the picture gets better still. The GPU exposes a window of its HBM through a PCIe BAR; the NIC is given a memory key for that window and DMAs straight in and out of GPU memory. The host DRAM never sees the gradient.

InfiniBand versus EFA

Both are RDMA fabrics, but they make opposite bets about ordering, and that single difference explains most of their behavioural differences.

InfiniBand's Reliable Connection transport delivers packets in order. That is a lovely property for software, and it means a connection follows one path through the fabric, chosen by a deterministic hash of the packet headers. It also means that if two heavy flows hash to the same link, they collide and stay collided for the life of the flow.

AWS's Scalable Reliable Datagram transport takes the other side. SRD sprays packets across every available path and delivers them out of order, pushing reassembly up to the library. This trades ordering guarantees for near-immunity to hash collisions, and it makes tail latency dramatically more predictable at scale. The cost shows up in α: EFA's per-message latency is meaningfully higher than InfiniBand's, in the region of 10–15 µs versus 2–3 µs.

The practical reading for training workloads is that EFA punishes small messages and rewards large, well-pipelined transfers. Which is another way of saying: on EFA, keep your collectives above n½, and prefer strategies whose inter-node traffic arrives in large contiguous chunks.

Nuance 05 EFA's higher α is a design choice, not weaker silicon

Often saidEFA is simply slower than InfiniBand.

ActuallyEFA's α is roughly four times InfiniBand's, but that is the price of SRD's packet spraying rather than a worse NIC. InfiniBand's reliable connection preserves ordering by pinning a flow to one path, which is exactly what exposes it to the hash collisions of §23. SRD sprays across every path and pushes reordering and retransmission up into the transport, where it costs microseconds. You are buying immunity to ECMP collisions with latency — a good trade on large pipelined collectives, a bad one on small ones.

Inter-node topology

A cluster is not a bag of nodes. It is a graph, and the shape of that graph determines which communication patterns are cheap and which are catastrophic.

The dominant design for GPU clusters is a folded Clos network, usually called a fat tree. Endpoints attach to leaf switches; leaf switches attach to spine switches; every leaf connects to every spine. The defining property of a non-blocking Clos is that the aggregate uplink capacity of each leaf equals its downlink capacity, so any permutation of endpoints can communicate at full rate simultaneously. Building this with radix-k switches gives you k³/4 endpoints in three levels — with 64-port switches, 65,536 GPUs.

Rails

The refinement that matters for GPU training is rail-optimised wiring. Instead of connecting all eight NICs of a node to the nearest leaf switch, you connect GPU j of every node to leaf switch j. Leaf switch 3 therefore sees GPU 3 of every node in the cluster and nothing else.

Why bother? Because the collectives that dominate training are symmetric across nodes. When 64 GPUs perform an AllReduce, the traffic pattern is not random: rank 3 on node A talks to rank 3 on node B. Under rail-optimised wiring that entire conversation lives inside one leaf switch — one hop, no spine, no contention with the other seven rails. The spine only carries traffic that genuinely needs to change rail.

Oversubscription and bisection

Bisection bandwidth is the aggregate capacity crossing the worst-case cut that splits the cluster in half. It is the hard ceiling on any all-to-all pattern, because in the worst case half the GPUs are sending to the other half. A fully non-blocking fat tree has bisection equal to half the endpoint bandwidth; real clusters are frequently oversubscribed at the leaf by 2:1 or 4:1 because non-blocking spine capacity is expensive and most workloads do not need it.

Most workloads. Mixture-of-experts training does need it, because expert routing produces genuine all-to-all traffic with no locality to exploit. If you are planning an MoE run on a cluster someone else specified, the oversubscription ratio is the first number to ask for.

The composition trick. The final stage of the figure is the single most useful structural idea in this article. Cross-rail traffic is expensive; NVLink is cheap; therefore rearrange data within the node over NVLink so that all inter-node traffic is same-rail. Every good hierarchical collective is an instance of this pattern, and so is Ulysses context parallelism, and so is the intra-node sharding dimension of HSDP.

The collective alphabet

A collective is an operation that every rank in a communicator participates in. It is not a library convenience; it is a different primitive from point-to-point messaging, because the implementation is free to choose any pattern of point-to-point transfers that produces the required result. That freedom is where all the performance is.

There are eight that matter, plus a barrier. Before we look at algorithms, look at semantics — what each one promises, independent of how it is achieved. In the figure, colour indicates which rank a piece of data originated on, and Σ indicates a reduction.

Two identities worth memorising

The eight are not independent. Two decompositions do almost all the work in real implementations:

AllReduce = ReduceScatter ∘ AllGather
AllGather = Gather ∘ Broadcast   (but never implement it that way)

The first is genuinely how every bandwidth-optimal AllReduce is built. Reduce the vector so that each rank owns the finished sum of one slice, then let everyone gather all the slices. Each half costs (p−1)/p · n bytes of traffic per rank, so the whole thing costs 2(p−1)/p · n. We will prove in a few sections that you cannot do better.

The second identity is true and useless: routing everything through one rank makes that rank's link the bottleneck and gives you a cost proportional to p·n instead of n. It is a good reminder that the semantics of a collective say nothing about its cost.

Broadcast and Reduce

Broadcast is the simplest collective and it already contains every idea we need. Root has n bytes; all p ranks must end up with them. There are three qualitatively different ways to do it, and their costs differ by orders of magnitude.

Flat

The root sends to each of the other p−1 ranks in turn. Every byte leaves the root p−1 times, so the root's outbound link carries (p−1)·n bytes:

Tflat = (p−1)(α + nβ)

Linear in p on both terms. At p = 1024 this is unusable.

Binomial tree

At step 1 the root sends to one rank. Now two ranks have the data, and at step 2 both send, so four ranks have it. The number of ranks holding the data doubles every step, so it takes ⌈log₂p⌉ steps:

Ttree = ⌈log₂p⌉ · (α + nβ)

Logarithmic in the latency term, which is the best possible — information cannot reach p ranks in fewer than log₂p rounds if each round doubles the informed set. But the bandwidth term is also multiplied by log₂p, and that is wasteful: the root still sends n bytes log₂p times, and every intermediate node forwards the whole vector before its children can start.

Pipelined ring

Split n into k chunks and stream them around a ring. Rank i forwards chunk c to rank i+1 as soon as it has received it, so all the links are busy at once. A chunk takes p−1 hops to reach the last rank, and the k chunks are pipelined behind each other:

Tring = (p − 2 + k)(α + (n/k)β)

Expand it: (p−2)α + (p−2)nβ/k + kα + nβ. As k grows the middle term vanishes and the last term — a single nβ, independent of p — is all that remains of the bandwidth cost. Differentiating with respect to k gives the optimum

k* = √( (p−2) · nβ / α )  ⟹  T* = nβ + (p−2)α + 2√( (p−2) α n β )

For large n this converges to nβ: broadcasting to a thousand ranks costs the same bandwidth as sending to one. That is the payoff for pipelining, and the same trick reappears in every bandwidth-optimal collective in this article.

Reduce is broadcast run backwards with arithmetic at the merge points, and every one of these three schemes has an exact mirror image. The costs are identical with a γn term added per level. That symmetry is worth internalising, because it means anything you learn about one applies to the other.

AllReduce, four ways

AllReduce is the collective that dominates deep learning. In plain data-parallel training it is the only collective, it runs once per step on the full gradient vector, and it sits squarely on the critical path. Getting it right is worth more than almost any other systems optimisation.

Here are the four algorithms you will actually meet, in increasing order of sophistication.

1 · Flat

Everyone reduces to rank 0; rank 0 broadcasts back. Cost 2(p−1)(α + nβ). Present in every naive implementation and in exactly zero good ones.

2 · Binomial tree

Reduce up a tree, broadcast down it. Cost 2⌈log₂p⌉(α + nβ). Excellent latency, mediocre bandwidth: the vector crosses log₂p levels, so the total bytes moved scale with log₂p rather than staying constant.

3 · Recursive halving and doubling

This is Rabenseifner's algorithm and it is cleverer than it first looks. In round s, rank i pairs with rank i XOR 2s. They exchange half of their current working vector — i sends the half that j will own, j sends the half that i will own — and each reduces the half it received. After log₂p rounds each rank owns 1/p of the vector, fully reduced: that is a ReduceScatter in log₂p rounds. Then run the same butterfly in reverse, doubling the vector size each round, to AllGather. Total:

Trhd = 2 log₂p · α + 2(p−1)/p · nβ + (p−1)/p · nγ

Because each round halves the payload, the bandwidth term telescopes: n/2 + n/4 + … + n/p = n(p−1)/p. Logarithmic latency and optimal bandwidth. The catch is that the textbook version needs p to be a power of two, and the pairing pattern is a hypercube — which is a poor match for a physical ring of NVLinks or a rail-optimised fabric.

4 · Ring

Arrange the ranks in a cycle. Do p−1 rounds of ReduceScatter passing one chunk to your neighbour each round, then p−1 rounds of AllGather doing the same. Cost:

Tring = 2(p−1) α + 2(p−1)/p · nβ + (p−1)/p · nγ

Same bandwidth term as Rabenseifner, worse latency term — linear in p rather than logarithmic. And yet the ring is what NCCL reaches for on large messages, because a ring only ever uses nearest-neighbour links, which means it can be embedded into almost any physical topology at full bandwidth, and because it can be chunked and pipelined so deeply that the α term is paid once rather than p−1 times in wall-clock terms.

What NCCL actually does. It implements ring, tree, CollNet (switch-offloaded) and NVLS (NVLink SHARP) algorithms, and three wire protocols — Simple, LL and LL128. LL trades bandwidth for latency by embedding a 4-byte flag alongside every 4 bytes of payload, so a receiver can poll for arrival without a separate synchronisation; that is 50% efficiency, acceptable when you are latency-bound anyway. LL128 uses 128-byte lines carrying 120 bytes of data — 93.75% efficient — and is the default on NVLink. The selection is a tuned lookup over message size, rank count and topology, and you can override it with NCCL_ALGO and NCCL_PROTO when the heuristic guesses wrong.

The ring, derived

The ring AllReduce is the single most important algorithm in distributed deep learning, and it is worth deriving rather than quoting.

Setup: p ranks in a cycle, each holding a gradient vector of n bytes. Split each rank's vector into p chunks of n/p bytes each. Index them 0 through p−1, the same way on every rank.

Phase one: reduce-scatter

Run p−1 rounds. In round s, rank i sends chunk (i − s) mod p to rank i+1, which adds it into its own copy of that chunk. Follow one chunk: chunk 0 starts on rank 0, moves to rank 1 in round 0 picking up rank 1's contribution, to rank 2 in round 1, and so on. After p−1 rounds it has visited every rank and accumulated every contribution — and it now sits on rank p−1.

Every rank is doing this simultaneously for a different chunk, so at the end of phase one, rank i owns the fully-reduced chunk (i+1) mod p and nothing else is finished. That is exactly a ReduceScatter. Each rank sent p−1 chunks of n/p bytes:

volumeRS = (p−1) · n/p bytes sent per rank

Phase two: all-gather

Now run p−1 more rounds of the same shape, except the receiver overwrites instead of adding, because the arriving chunk is already final. In round s, rank i sends chunk (i − s + 1) mod p to rank i+1. After p−1 rounds every rank has every finished chunk.

volumeAG = (p−1) · n/p bytes sent per rank

Add them up and apply the α–β model, counting 2(p−1) rounds each of which is one message of n/p bytes:

Tring = 2(p−1)·α + 2·(p−1)/p·n·β + (p−1)/p·n·γ

Two properties are worth pausing on. First, the bandwidth term is independent of p in the limit: as p → ∞, 2(p−1)/p → 2, so a ring AllReduce across ten thousand GPUs moves the same number of bytes per GPU as one across ten. Second, every link is busy in every round. There is no idle capacity anywhere in the schedule. Those two facts together are why the ring survived.

A lower bound

Is 2(p−1)/p · n the best possible? Yes, and the proof is short enough to do here. Assume each rank has one full-duplex link and can send and receive simultaneously — the standard model.

AllGather first

In an AllGather, each rank starts with n/p bytes and must end with all n bytes. The (p−1)/p of the result it does not already hold cannot be manufactured locally; it must arrive over the wire. So every rank must receive at least

n − n/p = (p−1)/p · n bytes

and by symmetry (the union of all ranks' data is the result, and every rank needs it) every rank must send at least as much. A ring AllGather transmits exactly (p−1) chunks of n/p, which is (p−1)/p · n. Optimal, with nothing left on the table.

ReduceScatter by duality

ReduceScatter is the transpose of AllGather: reverse every message and replace concatenation with addition. Any ReduceScatter schedule run backwards is a valid AllGather schedule with the same volume, so the same bound applies. Rank i's output slice depends on all p ranks' contributions to that slice, and p−1 of those live elsewhere, so at least (p−1)/p · n bytes must be received per rank.

Composition

An AllReduce implies a ReduceScatter (just throw away the slices you do not want) and implies an AllGather (start from a state where each rank owns one finished slice). Both are subproblems, so:

volumeAllReduce ≥ 2·(p−1)/p·n bytes per rank

The ring meets this exactly. Recursive halving-doubling also meets it exactly. Any algorithm claiming to beat it is either changing the problem — compressing, quantising, sparsifying, or reducing inside the network so that a switch does work a rank would otherwise do — or measuring wrong.

Nuance 07 There are two optimality floors, not one

Often saidRing AllReduce is optimal.

ActuallyRing is optimal in bandwidth: it attains the 2(p−1)/p·n bound exactly. There is a second, independent floor — a result depending on all p ranks cannot be produced in fewer than ⌈log₂p⌉ rounds, since the set of ranks that can have influenced any given rank at most doubles per round — and ring misses it badly, paying 2(p−1)·α. The tree hits the latency floor and misses the bandwidth one. Recursive halving-doubling hits both, but only for p a power of two. “Optimal” means nothing here without naming which term.

ReduceScatter and AllGather

These two deserve their own section not because they are hard — you have already seen both, as the halves of the ring — but because modern training uses them separately, and that changes the accounting in a way people routinely get wrong.

The factors here are worth stating slowly, because they are where reasoning usually goes wrong. For a model with Ψ parameters in bf16, the gradient is 2Ψ bytes. An AllReduce moves 2(p−1)/p times its input per rank, which for any realistic p is very close to 2. So a data-parallel step moves roughly 4Ψ bytes per rank, not 2Ψ — the parameter count is doubled once by bf16 and again by the collective.

Nuance 08 A data-parallel step moves 4Ψ, not 2Ψ

Often saidData parallelism costs one gradient's worth of traffic — 2Ψ bytes for a bf16 model.

ActuallyTwo doublings get conflated. The gradient is 2Ψ bytes because bf16 is two bytes per parameter; then AllReduce moves 2(p−1)/p ≈ 2 times its input per rank. The product is ≈ 4Ψ bytes per rank per step. A 2× error here is enough to make a strategy look feasible when it is not, which is precisely what the table below exists to prevent.

It is usually worth it, because the memory saved buys you a larger micro-batch, and a larger micro-batch improves the compute-to-communication ratio — which partly pays back the 50%.

Where hybrid sharding comes from. HSDP shards parameters within a group of size g and replicates across p/g groups. The AllGathers then only span g ranks, and if you set g = 8 they never leave the node — 450 GB/s NVLink instead of 50 GB/s network. Only the gradient ReduceScatter crosses the fabric. On a cluster where the NVLink-to-network ratio is 9×, this is the difference between a working ZeRO-3 run and an unusable one, and it is why the intra-node sharding dimension is the first thing to set on an EFA cluster.

AllToAll

AllToAll is the collective with no shortcuts. Every rank sends a distinct message to every other rank — it is a distributed transpose — and unlike a reduction there is nothing to combine on the way, so no clever tree can reduce the volume.

Per-rank volume is (p−1)/p · n, which looks cheap next to AllReduce's 2(p−1)/p · n. The problem is not volume, it is structure. An AllReduce has locality you can exploit: reduce within the node first, then across. An AllToAll has none — every pair genuinely must exchange data, so the traffic is spread uniformly across the fabric and the limiting resource is bisection bandwidth, not per-link bandwidth. On an oversubscribed fat tree, AllToAll is the pattern that finds the oversubscription.

Nuance 10 AllToAll moves half what AllReduce moves, and is still harder

Often saidAllToAll is expensive because it moves so much data.

ActuallyIt moves (p−1)/p·n per rank — half of AllReduce's 2(p−1)/p·n. The difficulty is structural, not volumetric. A reduction has locality you can exploit, so you reduce inside the node and then across; an AllToAll genuinely requires every pair to exchange, so traffic spreads uniformly and the binding constraint becomes bisection bandwidth rather than per-link bandwidth. No hierarchical decomposition rescues it.

Ulysses is the friendlier case. Latency-optimal vs bandwidth-optimal

Figure 10 showed the crossover empirically. It is worth deriving, because the answer is a single closed-form expression and it explains every algorithm-selection heuristic inside NCCL.

Take the two extremes. A binomial-tree AllReduce does a reduce up the tree and a broadcast down, ⌈log₂p⌉ rounds each, moving the whole vector every round:

Ttree = 2⌈log₂p⌉ · (α + nβ)

A ring pays 2(p−1) rounds but only ever moves a 1/p slice:

Tring = 2(p−1)·α + 2·(p−1)/p·nβ

The tree is cheaper exactly when Ttree < Tring. Collecting the α terms on one side and the β terms on the other:

n · [ log₂p − (p−1)/p ] · β  <  [ (p−1) − log₂p ] · α
n  <  n× = (α/β) · ( (p−1) − log₂p ) / ( log₂p − (p−1)/p )

The crossover is proportional to α/β — the same n½ that characterises the link — scaled by a factor that depends only on p. That factor grows roughly like p / log₂p, so the crossover moves right as the job gets bigger: at large scale the ring's latency term becomes so punishing that trees win for increasingly large messages.

Derive the crossover for the intuition, not for the decision.

Hierarchical collectives

Everything so far has assumed one flat network with one β. Real clusters have at least two, differing by roughly 9×, and a collective that ignores that is leaving most of the machine unused.

Consider a flat ring AllReduce across 16 GPUs on 4 nodes. Order the ring so that consecutive GPUs are neighbours where possible, and you still cross a node boundary four times. Now here is the problem: a ring runs at the speed of its slowest link. Every round, every link must carry one chunk, so the twelve NVLink hops sit idle at 11% utilisation waiting for the four network hops. The entire collective proceeds at network speed even though three quarters of it is on NVLink.

The fix is to decompose. Reduce inside each node over NVLink, exchange only the reduced slices over the network, then broadcast back inside the node.

Written out, the hierarchical cost for G GPUs per node and N nodes is

T = 2·(G−1)/G·n·βnv  +  2·(N−1)/N·(n/G)·βnet  +  (latency terms)

The network term shrinks by a factor of G·(P−1)/P·N/(N−1), which for realistic sizes is just about exactly G — the number of GPUs in the fast domain. On an 8-GPU node that is an 8× reduction in fabric traffic before you account for the fact that the NVLink phases also run 9× faster.

NCCL does this automatically when it can see the topology, which is one reason its tree algorithm has a two-level structure: an intra-node phase and an inter-node phase with one representative GPU per node per rail. It is also, structurally, exactly what HSDP does with its sharding and replication dimensions.

Nuance 14 Hierarchy buys you G, not the bandwidth ratio

Often saidDoing the collective hierarchically speeds it up by the NVLink-to-network ratio, 9×.

ActuallyTwo distinct factors are at work, and they compose. The traffic reduction is G·(P−1)/P·N/(N−1) ≈ G, the number of GPUs in the fast domain — 8 on a standard node — and it has nothing to do with how fast NVLink is; it would hold even if NVLink and the fabric ran at identical speed. On top of that, the phases that moved onto NVLink run at 9× the bandwidth. Quoting either number alone understates what decomposition does.

Reduction inside the switch

There is one more move available: do the arithmetic in the network. InfiniBand switches with SHARP, and NVSwitch 3 with NVLink SHARP (marketed as NVLS), can perform the reduction on the aggregation tree itself rather than shuttling partial sums between endpoints.

It is worth being precise about what this buys, because the marketing tends to overstate it. Under in-network reduction each rank sends its full vector up once and receives the result down once, so per-rank volume is 2n. The ring's volume is 2(p−1)/p·n, which for p = 64 is 1.97n. So the bandwidth advantage is essentially zero — in fact the ring moves marginally fewer bytes.

Nuance 15 In-network reduction is not a bandwidth optimisation

Often saidSHARP halves AllReduce traffic by removing the (p−1)/p factor.

ActuallyPer-rank volume under in-network reduction is 2n: the vector goes up once and comes back once. A ring moves 2(p−1)/p·n, which at p = 64 is 1.97n — marginally fewer bytes per endpoint. The wins are real but they sit elsewhere: the 2(p−1)·α latency term collapses to roughly 2α, the SMs a ring burns on copy-and-add kernels come back, and aggregate bytes on the wire drop sharply because the switch consumes p inputs and emits one. Sell it on latency and SM occupancy, never on bandwidth.

The real wins are elsewhere, and they are substantial:

  • Latency. The 2(p−1)·α term collapses to something like 2·α plus switch processing. At p = 512 and α = 3 µs that is 3 milliseconds of pure latency removed from every step.
  • GPU cycles. A ring AllReduce burns SMs on copy and add kernels for its entire duration, competing with the compute you are trying to overlap it with. In-network reduction gives those back.
  • Fabric traffic. Volume per rank is unchanged, but the traffic no longer traverses p−1 hops of the tree — the switch consumes p inputs and emits one, so aggregate bytes on the wire drop sharply even though per-endpoint bytes do not.

The constraints are real: the reduction operator must be one the switch implements, the datatype must be supported, and the aggregation tree must be configured by the fabric manager. It is not something you get by accident, and on clusters without it, none of this applies.

Nuance 16 Sequence parallelism is the same bytes and twice the α

Often saidSequence parallelism reduces tensor-parallel communication.

ActuallyIt moves exactly the same number of bytes. The rule that falls out

Line the strategies up by how much traffic they generate per unit of compute and a clear ordering appears:

  1. Pipeline parallel is the cheapest — one activation tensor per stage boundary, and stage boundaries are rare. Its cost is bubbles, not bytes.
  2. Data parallel is next — one gradient AllReduce per step, amortised over the whole batch, and it overlaps beautifully with the backward pass.
  3. ZeRO-3 is 1.5× data parallel in volume but the collectives are on the critical path of every layer, so it needs aggressive prefetch to hide.
  4. Tensor and sequence parallel move activation-sized data every layer, which is why the received wisdom is to keep the tensor-parallel degree inside a single NVLink domain and never let it cross the network.
  5. Expert parallel is the most demanding, because AllToAll has no locality to exploit and its message sizes are data-dependent.

That ordering, plus the 9× cliff between NVLink and the fabric, generates the standard recipe almost mechanically: tensor parallelism inside the node, pipeline and data parallelism across nodes, sharding wherever memory demands it, and context or expert parallelism only when the model shape forces your hand.

Overlap, bucketing, and the exposed tail

Every cost model so far has quietly assumed communication is time you spend. It need not be. Gradients become available in reverse layer order during the backward pass, and the reduction of layer 31's gradient can proceed while layer 30 is still computing. If communication is fully hidden behind compute, its cost is zero — and the α–β model stops mattering entirely.

The reason this does not simply solve the problem is that hiding is not free, and the residue that fails to hide — the exposed tail — is what actually shows up in your step time.

Why bucket at all

The naive approach launches one AllReduce per parameter tensor. A 7B model has thousands of tensors, many of them small: layer-norm gains, biases, embeddings split across ranks. Each collective costs 2(p−1)α in latency regardless of size, so at p = 64 and α = 12 µs, every message costs 1.5 ms before a single byte moves. A thousand tensors is a second and a half of pure latency.

So gradients are accumulated into buckets and reduced in batches. This is the same trade-off as the pipelined broadcast in §11: more, smaller messages give better overlap but pay more α; fewer, larger messages amortise α but delay the first launch and leave a longer tail. There is an optimum, and it moves with p.

Congestion, collisions, and incast

Every bandwidth figure quoted so far is a capacity. What you get is capacity divided by however many flows happen to be sharing the link, and in a datacentre fabric that divisor is decided by a hash function you do not control.

ECMP and the birthday problem

A leaf switch with several uplinks to the spine layer must choose one for each packet. Choosing per-packet would reorder the flow, which a reliable-connection transport cannot tolerate, so the standard answer is ECMP: hash the five-tuple — source and destination IP, source and destination port, protocol — and send the whole flow down whichever uplink the hash selects. Order is preserved because every packet of a flow follows one path.

The cost is that path selection is random and uncoordinated. Two flows can hash to the same uplink while a neighbouring uplink sits idle, and no mechanism notices. This is the balls-in-bins problem: throw k flows into k uplinks and the expected occupancy of the busiest is not 1 but roughly ln k / ln ln k. At k = 64 that is about 2.9 — the unluckiest link carries nearly three times its share, and since a collective runs at the speed of its slowest participant, that link sets the pace for everyone.

Nuance 19 ECMP collisions are the normal case

Often saidTwo flows landing on the same uplink is bad luck.

ActuallyIt is the expected outcome. Throwing k flows into k uplinks is balls in bins: the busiest bin holds roughly ln k / ln ln k, about 2.9 at k = 64, not 1. Because a collective advances at the pace of its slowest participant, the unluckiest link sets the step time for everyone. The diagnostic tell is run-to-run variance at fixed configuration — a fabric that is fine on one job and slow on the next with nothing changed is usually just hashing differently.

Two answers, and what each costs

Spread the packets. AWS's SRD, used by EFA, abandons the idea that a flow owns a path. It sprays packets of a single message across every available route and reassembles at the receiver. Load is even by construction and hash collisions cannot happen. The cost is that the transport must handle arbitrary reordering, which is why EFA exposes an unordered delivery model and why its α is roughly four times InfiniBand's — the reordering and retransmission logic lives above the wire. NVIDIA's newer InfiniBand generations offer adaptive routing that achieves something similar by moving flows off congested paths, and the RoCE world has been converging on spraying too.

Never leave the rail. If GPU j on every node communicates only with GPU j on other nodes, the traffic is leaf-local or single-path by construction and there is nothing to collide with. This costs nothing at all, which is why rail-aligned collectives are so much more predictable than their raw bandwidth suggests, and why NCCL works hard to keep inter-node phases on-rail.

Incast

The other failure mode is not a routing problem but a buffering one. When many ranks send to one — the gather step of a naive AllGather, or any AllToAll where the schedule accidentally synchronises — the last-hop switch port is oversubscribed by the fan-in ratio, and switch buffers are shallow, measured in tens of megabytes shared across all ports. At 400 Gb/s a port drains a megabyte in 20 microseconds, so a 32-way incast fills the buffer faster than any end-to-end congestion signal can react.

Lossless fabrics respond with backpressure: ECN marks to slow the senders, and if that is too slow, PFC pause frames to stop the upstream port outright. PFC works, but it pauses everything on that priority class, including traffic that was not part of the problem, and pauses propagate upstream. This is how a single hot receiver turns into a fabric-wide stall, and in the worst case a deadlock. It is the reason schedules matter: a ring AllReduce, where every rank sends to exactly one peer at a time, is nearly immune to incast, while a flat gather is a machine for producing it.

Nuance 20 PFC pauses traffic unrelated to the congestion

Often saidBackpressure protects the fabric from a hot receiver.

ActuallyECN marking does — it signals the specific senders to slow down. PFC is blunter: it pauses an entire priority class on an upstream port, including flows unrelated to the congestion, and the pause propagates further upstream. One overloaded receiver can therefore stall a region of the fabric and, in the worst case, deadlock it. This is why the schedule matters and not only the volume.

Measuring it

There are two bandwidth numbers you can compute from a collective benchmark, and confusing them is the most common mistake in this area.

Algorithm bandwidth is the honest, naive one: the size of the buffer you handed the library divided by the time it took.

algbw = n / T

It is trivially correct and almost useless for comparison, because it does not account for the fact that different collectives inherently move different amounts of data for the same buffer size. An AllReduce of 1 GB moves roughly 2 GB per rank; a Broadcast of 1 GB moves 1 GB. Comparing their algbw tells you nothing about whether the hardware is being used well.

Bus bandwidth corrects for this by multiplying by the collective's intrinsic volume factor, so that a well-implemented collective of any kind reports a number close to the hardware's peak.

The practical consequence: busbw is the number you compare against the wire. If a node has 8 GPUs each with 50 GB/s of network per direction and your 64-rank AllReduce reports 42 GB/s busbw, you are at 84% of line rate and there is little left to win. If it reports 12 GB/s, something is wrong — and the ratio tells you roughly what.

A diagnostic reading of the curve

Run a size sweep, plot busbw against message size on a log axis, and the shape tells you which term dominates.

  • Rises steeply then plateaus, plateau near line rate. Healthy. The knee sits near n½ = α/β, which is where you should expect it from §4.
  • Plateau well below line rate, flat everywhere. A β problem. Suspect the wrong path — traffic going over PCIe instead of NVLink, GPUDirect RDMA not engaged, or a rail misconfiguration sending everything through the spine. Check NCCL_DEBUG=INFO output for the topology NCCL detected and confirm the channels use the links you think they do.
  • Knee far to the right of α/β. An α problem, and usually a software one: too many small messages, a protocol fallback, or an algorithm choice that pays more latency rounds than necessary. Try pinning NCCL_ALGO and NCCL_PROTO and see which combination moves the knee.
  • Plateau that degrades as p grows, with high variance run to run. Congestion. This is the ECMP signature from §23 — the variance comes from different hash outcomes on different runs.
  • Plateau at exactly half or a quarter of expectation. Almost always a unit or convention error rather than a real problem. Check whether the number you are comparing against is unidirectional or bidirectional, and whether it is per GPU or per node.

Nuance 21 An exact factor of two is almost never a bug

Often saidThe measurement is half what it should be, so something is broken.

ActuallySuspiciously round ratios are usually a convention mismatch rather than a fault. Check in order: unidirectional versus bidirectional; per GPU versus per node; algorithm bandwidth versus bus bandwidth; GB/s versus Gb/s, a factor of 8; and 109 versus 230 bytes, a factor of 1.074 that shows up as a stubborn 7% rather than a 2×. Only when all five come back clean is it worth looking for a real problem.

Nuance 22 Benchmark bus bandwidth is an upper bound

Often saidnccl-tests reports 42 GB/s, so the training step gets 42 GB/s.

ActuallyA standalone all_reduce_perf measures the fabric at its most idle: nothing competing for SMs or HBM bandwidth, no other collectives in flight. Training does not run under those conditions. Treat the benchmark as a ceiling, and treat the gap between it and in-training bus bandwidth as its own diagnostic — a large gap is an overlap-tuning problem, not a fabric problem.

What to carry away

The whole article compresses to a handful of statements. If you keep nothing else, keep these.

Arithmetic is free; movement is not.
An H100 must do about 295 floating-point operations per byte it reads from its own memory just to break even, and roughly 20,000 per byte it reads from another node. Every design decision in distributed training is a consequence of that spread.
Two numbers describe any link: α and β.
T = α + βn. Their ratio n½ = α/β is the message size at which you reach half the link's asymptotic bandwidth, and it is the single most useful number to know about a fabric. Below it you are buying latency; above it you are buying bandwidth.
Latency is a software property more than a physical one.
Two microseconds of NVLink α is not the speed of light across a metre of copper — that is five nanoseconds. It is queue-pair processing, doorbell writes, completion polling, and kernel launch. Which is why α varies by 6× between fabrics whose wires are comparable.
Every collective decomposes, and the decomposition is the algorithm.
AllReduce = ReduceScatter + AllGather. That identity generates the ring algorithm, explains why ZeRO-1 costs the same as DDP, explains sequence parallelism, and gives you the bandwidth-optimal schedule for free.
There is a floor, and good implementations reach it.
Every rank must receive at least (p−1)/p·n bytes to complete an AllGather, so AllReduce cannot cost less than 2(p−1)/p·n. Ring hits that bound exactly. No algorithm will beat it on bandwidth; algorithms only differ in how much α they pay to get there.
A ring runs at the speed of its slowest link.
Which is why flat collectives across a hierarchical machine waste most of the machine, and why decomposing by level — reduce inside the node, exchange across it, broadcast back — cuts fabric traffic by roughly the number of GPUs in the fast domain.
Match the parallelism to the level of the hierarchy.
Tensor and sequence parallelism move activation-sized data every layer; keep them inside NVLink. Data and pipeline parallelism move less and tolerate the fabric. Expert parallelism needs genuine bisection bandwidth. The standard recipe is not a convention, it is this ordering plus a 9× bandwidth cliff.
Hidden communication is nearly free, and the tail is what remains.
Bucket size trades α against overlap, the optimum moves with rank count, and the last bucket can never hide because its gradients do not exist until the backward pass ends.
Compare bus bandwidth, never algorithm bandwidth.
And when a number looks exactly 2× off, check whether someone is quoting bidirectional against unidirectional before looking for a real bug.

The thread running through all of it is that communication cost is not a tax levied on an otherwise clean computation. It is a structural property of how you chose to split the problem, and it is predictable from first principles with about six formulas. Once the cost model is in your head, most parallelism decisions stop being matters of taste.

The nuances, in one place

Twenty-two claims in this article contradict something that is widely repeated. They are marked inline as they arise; this is the index, so you can check a belief without rereading the argument around it. Each entry states the correction, not the common version — follow the link for the reasoning.

  1. 01Arithmetic intensity is per level, not per chip — 295 FLOP/byte is the HBM number; the fabric number is nearer 19,800.
  2. 02Latency is a software property, not a distance — the wire is nanoseconds; α is microseconds, and the gap is the stack.
  3. 03The NVLink-to-network gap is 9×, not 18× — 18× compares bidirectional against unidirectional.
  4. 04PCIe headline rates are pre-encoding, and the encoding changed — Gen5 ×16 is 63.0 GB/s, and Gen6 changes the encoding entirely.
  5. 05EFA's higher α is a design choice, not weaker silicon — you are buying immunity to hash collisions with latency.
  6. 06A plateau at half line rate may be the protocol — LL is 50% efficient by construction, on purpose.
  7. 07There are two optimality floors, not one — ring hits the bandwidth bound and misses the latency bound.
  8. 08A data-parallel step moves 4Ψ, not 2Ψ — bf16 doubles once and the collective doubles again.
  9. 09ZeRO-1 and ZeRO-2 buy memory, not bandwidth — identical volume to DDP; only ZeRO-3 changes it, upward.
  10. 10AllToAll moves half what AllReduce moves, and is still harder — and is still harder, because the constraint is bisection.
  11. 11MoE breaks the equal-message-size assumption — cost follows the worst imbalance, not the mean.
  12. 12Ulysses is capped by KV heads, not attention heads — under GQA the ceiling is often 8, not 32.
  13. 13The ring-versus-tree crossover is moot twice over — Rabenseifner wins both terms; hierarchy outranks both.
  14. 14Hierarchy buys you G, not the bandwidth ratio — traffic falls by the fast-domain size; speed is a separate factor.
  15. 15In-network reduction is not a bandwidth optimisation — 2n versus 1.97n; sell it on latency and SM occupancy.
  16. 16Sequence parallelism is the same bytes and twice the α — it buys activation memory, not communication volume.
  17. 17The bucket optimum tracks p, not bandwidth — 25 MB is far too small at four-digit rank counts.
  18. 18Hidden communication still costs a few percent — overlapped means 3% instead of 30%, not zero.
  19. 19ECMP collisions are the normal case — the busiest of k links carries about 2.9× its share at k = 64.
  20. 20PFC pauses traffic unrelated to the congestion — which is how one hot receiver stalls a region of the fabric.
  21. 21An exact factor of two is almost never a bug — check five conventions before looking for a fault.
  22. 22Benchmark bus bandwidth is an upper bound — the gap to in-training bus bandwidth is its own diagnostic.

If there is a pattern across the twenty-two, it is that most of them come from one of three sources: a unit or direction convention left unstated (03, 04, 08, 21), a quantity that is optimal in one term being described as optimal outright (07, 13, 15), or a cost model being applied outside the assumptions it was derived under (05, 11, 17, 22). Those three habits account for most of the confusion in this area, and all three are cheap to guard against once you know to look.
