The question this article answers

Suppose you have a fixed amount of compute and you want to build a vision-language model (VLM). You must pick a vision encoder and a language model. You must pick an image resolution. You must decide how much caption data, interleaved web data and plain text to train on. Every one of these choices spends the same budget. Which split buys the most capability?

For a text-only language model, this question has a well-studied answer. A scaling law describes how loss falls as you add parameters and training tokens. From that law you can work out how to split a compute budget between model size and data. The site's article Scaling Laws from First Principles derives that answer in detail.

A VLM breaks the clean picture in three places. First, it has two towers joined by a connector, so "model size" is really three numbers. Second, a training sample is not a fixed number of tokens. An image can become 64 tokens or 7,000 tokens, depending on resolution and on how the patches are merged. Third, most VLMs are assembled from parts that were already trained elsewhere. Their cost was paid before the VLM run started.

This article asks what a scaling law means under those conditions, and what the published evidence says. We start with a short recap of the language-model case. Then we define the compute budget of a VLM carefully, because every later result depends on it. After that we go through each axis that people have scaled: the vision tower, the data, the fusion style, the split of parameters, the number of visual tokens, and post-training. We close with what open VLMs have actually spent, and with what nobody knows yet.

This is post 05 of the series. The previous post, Teaching Language Models to See, covers how VLMs are pretrained. Here we ask how that recipe should change as the budget grows.

Scaling laws in one page

A scaling law is a formula that predicts a model's loss from the resources spent on it. The most used form, from the Chinchilla study, writes the loss L as a function of the parameter count N and the number of training tokens D:

L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ E is the irreducible loss. The two other terms shrink as the model and the data grow.

Training compute ties the two resources together. For a dense transformer, each parameter costs about six floating-point operations per token: two in the forward pass and four in the backward pass. So the compute budget is

C ≈ 6 · N · D

Fix C, substitute D = C / 6N into the loss, and minimise over N. The answer is a compute-optimal model size. Hoffmann et al. found that model size and token count should grow at roughly the same rate, about 20 training tokens per parameter in their fit. Kaplan et al. had earlier recommended much larger models trained on fewer tokens. The difference came mostly from how parameters and compute were counted, a story the scaling-laws article tells in full.

Two further points from that article matter here. The first is that train-optimal is not deploy-optimal. If a model will serve billions of requests, a smaller model trained on more tokens can be cheaper over its lifetime, even if it wasted training compute (Sardana et al., 2023). The second is that loss is not capability. A smooth fall in loss can still show up as sudden jumps on a benchmark, and the link between them has to be measured.

Does the same law hold beyond text? Henighan et al. (2020) trained autoregressive transformers on images, video, mathematics, and image–caption pairs in both directions. Every domain followed a power law plus a constant. The compute-optimal model size grew with compute along nearly the same power law in every domain, roughly Nopt ∝ C0.7 in their setup. So the basic shape survives contact with pixels. The rest of this article is about what that simple picture leaves out.

What changes when the model has two towers

Take a typical modern VLM. A vision encoder turns the image into patch features. A small connector maps those features into the language model's embedding space, often after merging neighbouring patches. A language model (the LLM) reads the visual tokens together with the text and predicts the next text token. The architectures are surveyed in The VLM Architecture Gallery, and the encoder side in Inside a Modern Vision Encoder.

Parameters split into three

The single number N becomes a sum:

N = NV + NC + NL vision encoder + connector + language model

These three parts are not equal in cost or in effect. The connector is usually tiny. In LLaVA-OneVision, the trainable projector for the 72B model has 72M parameters. The encoder is usually a few hundred million parameters. The LLM is billions. So the parameter count is dominated by the LLM, but, as we will see, the compute is not always.

Data splits into kinds

A text model trains on one kind of token. A VLM trains on a mixture. Typical pretraining mixes image–caption pairs, interleaved web documents with several images, and text-only data. MM1 used 45% captions, 45% interleaved documents and 10% text in its ablation setup. DeepSeek-VL reported that keeping at least 70% language data during pretraining was needed to preserve language ability. The data also arrives in stages: alignment, large-scale pretraining, instruction tuning, and often reinforcement learning. So D is a vector of token counts, one per kind and per stage.

An image does not have a fixed size in tokens

A ViT cuts an image of H × W pixels into square patches of side p. The number of patches is

P = (H / p) · (W / p) a 448 × 448 image with 14-pixel patches gives 32 × 32 = 1,024 patches

Many VLMs then merge each m × m block of patch features into one token before the LLM sees it. This is called pixel shuffle or patch merging. InternVL, Qwen2-VL and NVLM use a 2 × 2 merge. So the LLM receives

V = P / m2 1,024 patches with a 2 × 2 merge give 256 visual tokens

Both numbers grow with the square of the resolution. Doubling the side of the image multiplies the work of both towers by four. Tiling schemes multiply it again by the number of tiles. This is why resolution is a scaling axis of its own, and why a VLM's "tokens per sample" is a design choice rather than a property of the data.

The compute budget of one training sample

Now apply the six-FLOPs rule to each tower. The encoder runs on every patch. The LLM runs on every visual token and every text token. If both towers are trained, one sample with T text tokens costs about

Csample ≈ 6 · NV · P + 6 · NL · (V + T) the connector term is small and is left out; attention scores are also left out

Shukor et al. use the same accounting for late-fusion models, written as C = 6(NvDv + N·D), with Dv the number of vision-only tokens.

The factor of six assumes the tower is trained. Frozen towers change it, and the change is not symmetric. A frozen encoder at the start of the network only needs a forward pass: 2 FLOPs per parameter per patch. A frozen LLM is different. If the connector below it is being trained, the gradient must still travel back through the LLM to reach the connector. That backward pass computes gradients with respect to activations, which costs another 2 FLOPs per parameter. Only the weight-gradient half is skipped. So a frozen LLM still costs about 4 FLOPs per parameter per token.

k = 6 (trained) · 4 (frozen, but something below it trains) · 2 (frozen, nothing below it trains) multiply each tower's parameter count by its k

Figure 1 walks through one sample with a 0.4B encoder, a 448-pixel image, a 2 × 2 merge, 200 text tokens and a 7B LLM.

Two consequences follow from this arithmetic, and both run against intuition.

Insight

The encoder's share of compute is NV·m² / (NV·m² + NL·(1 + T/V)). The merge factor multiplies the encoder's weight, because the encoder sees every patch while the LLM sees only the merged tokens. With a 2 × 2 merge, a 0.675B encoder (Qwen2-VL) costs as much per image as a 2.7B LLM would. Paired with Qwen2-VL-2B's 1.5B LLM, the encoder is about 64% of the visual compute. Paired with the 72B LLM, it is under 4%. In practice, the encoder is a rounding error for large VLMs and the main cost for small ones. SmolVLM's finding that small models want small encoders, and Qwen3-VL's switch to a 300M encoder for its 2B and 4B models, both fit this arithmetic.

Nuance

Freezing the LLM during the connector-alignment stage saves only a third of the LLM's training compute, not two thirds, because gradients still have to flow back through it. The real savings of a frozen LLM are in memory: no optimizer state and no weight gradients for billions of parameters. So when a recipe says "stage 1 is cheap", it is cheap because it is short, not because each step is.

The calculator also shows why high resolution is expensive for the encoder in a way this formula hides. The six-FLOPs rule counts only matrix products against weights. Self-attention adds a cost that grows with the square of the number of patches. At a few thousand patches per image this term is no longer small. Qwen2.5-VL's encoder therefore uses windowed attention of 112 × 112 pixels in most layers and full attention in only four of its 32 layers.

Sunk cost: the compute you did not pay

One more accounting choice changes every comparison. Almost every open VLM starts from a pretrained LLM and a pretrained encoder. The LLM may have seen 18 trillion text tokens before the VLM run begins. Should its cost count?

For the question "how should I spend my budget", the answer is no. That compute is sunk. But it means that two VLM papers reporting their training tokens can be describing very different total investments. InternVL 2.5 reports that its 78B model used about 120B tokens of multimodal training, against a cumulative 1.4T for Qwen2-VL-72B, and credits the reuse of its large pretrained InternViT encoder. The encoder's own training is not in the 120B figure. A scaling law fitted on VLM-stage tokens alone measures the cost of adaptation, not the cost of the model.

Nuance

"Trained on 10× fewer tokens" is only meaningful if both models start from equally expensive parts. InternVL 2.5's comparison with Qwen2-VL is honest about this: it attributes the saving to a 6B encoder trained earlier. When you compare recipes, add up the pretraining of every reused tower, or at least name which parts were free.

Scaling the vision tower on its own

The first scaling studies in vision did not involve a language model at all. They asked how a vision encoder improves with model size, data and compute. Their answers set the encoders that VLMs still use.

Supervised ViTs: a saturating frontier

Zhai et al. (2021) trained Vision Transformers from 5M to 2B parameters on up to 3 billion labelled images (JFT-3B). They plotted error against training compute. The best models at each compute level lay on a frontier that a power law with a saturation term fits well. Three findings came out of the plot. Small models saturate: a ViT-Ti stops improving even with more data. Large models need more data: even 30–300M images were not enough to saturate the biggest ones. And with enough data, training a larger model for fewer steps beat training a smaller one for longer. Their 2B-parameter ViT-G reached 90.45% top-1 on ImageNet after fine-tuning.

Two later results refined this. Alabdulmohsin et al. (2023) fitted scaling laws for the shape of the network, not just its size. They found that compute-optimal ViTs should grow the MLP width fastest, then depth, then width. Their shape-optimised SoViT-400m/14 matched the much larger ViT-g/14 at equal pretraining compute, with less than half the inference cost. That 400M shape became SigLIP-So400m, the encoder inside PaliGemma, Idefics2, LLaVA-OneVision and many others. Dehghani et al. (2023) went the other way and trained a 22B-parameter ViT. It kept improving with scale, and it later became the encoder of PaLI-X.

Contrastive encoders: the data distribution sets the slope

Most VLM encoders are trained contrastively on image–text pairs, as in CLIP and ALIGN. Cherti et al. (2023) studied CLIP scaling with fully open data and code. They trained models from ViT-B/32 to ViT-g/14 on LAION subsets of 80M, 400M and 2B pairs, for up to 34B samples seen. Zero-shot classification and retrieval errors followed power laws in total compute.

The surprise was in the comparison with OpenAI's CLIP. The architectures and recipes were nearly the same. Yet OpenCLIP models on LAION scaled better on retrieval, and OpenAI's models on their private data scaled better on ImageNet classification. For ImageNet zero-shot error, the fitted exponents were about 0.11 for OpenCLIP and 0.16 for OpenAI CLIP. The authors attribute the difference to the training distribution. So an encoder scaling law is a property of the model and its data together.

Other studies moved different knobs. BASIC scaled data to 6.6B pairs, the model to 3B weights and the batch to 65,536. Li et al. (2023) found an "inverse scaling law" for CLIP training: larger encoders tolerate shorter image and text token sequences during training, which made large CLIP models much cheaper to train. SigLIP replaced the softmax contrastive loss with a sigmoid loss that does not need a global normalisation over the batch. SigLIP 2 added captioning-style and self-supervised losses to the same recipe. EVA-CLIP-18B pushed a CLIP encoder to 18B parameters. Meta CLIP 2 scaled CLIP to worldwide multilingual data and found that the English-only drop seen in earlier multilingual CLIPs (a "curse of multilinguality") could be avoided with a larger model (ViT-H/14) and more seen pairs. Going in the other direction, Li et al. (2024) studied CLIP under small budgets and found that smaller ViTs suit smaller datasets, while larger ViTs win only once the data is large.

Beyond ten billion pairs

Wang et al. (2025) built WebLI-100B, 100 billion image–text pairs, and trained SigLIP models on 1B, 10B and 100B unique examples with the same number of examples seen. On common Western-centric benchmarks such as ImageNet zero-shot and COCO retrieval, the gain from 10B to 100B was small. On tasks that need long-tail concepts, it was large. A ViT-L/16 trained for one epoch on 100B examples reached 41.7% on Dollar Street geolocalisation. The same model trained for ten epochs on 10B examples reached 35.9%, at the same compute. Low-resource languages such as Telugu also improved.

Nuance

A flat curve on standard benchmarks is not the same as a flat curve in capability. WebLI-100B's data scaling looked saturated on ImageNet and COCO and was clearly not saturated on cultural and multilingual tasks. The same paper found that quality filters such as CLIP-score filtering, which help standard benchmarks, can reduce cultural diversity. If your users are not the benchmark's users, measure on their distribution before concluding that more data no longer helps.

Does the encoder need language at all?

Fan et al. (2025) asked whether self-supervised encoders trail CLIP in VLMs because they lack language supervision or because they were trained on different data. They trained DINOv2-style self-supervised models and CLIP models on the same 2 billion MetaCLIP images, from 1B to 7B parameters, and evaluated each as the encoder of a VLM on VQA. In this controlled setup the self-supervised models scaled better with model size and data than CLIP, and did not saturate at 7B. At scale they matched CLIP on VQA, including OCR and chart tasks once the data contained enough text-rich images.

Insight

Encoder quality inside a VLM is not a function of parameter count. Idefics2 found that EVA-CLIP-5B, ten times larger than SigLIP-So400m, gave about the same VLM performance and suggested it was under-trained. SoViT-400m matched a model 2.5× its size at equal compute. Web-SSL showed the training objective and data decide which encoders keep improving. So when a VLM needs a better encoder, the first lever is a better-trained encoder of a known shape, not a bigger one.

Quality, quantity and repetition

Web image–text data is noisy. A common response is to filter it hard. LAION kept about 10% of the pairs it crawled. DataComp turned this into a benchmark: a fixed pool of 12.8 billion pairs, fixed training code and a fixed compute budget, so that only the filter changes. Its best baseline subset, DataComp-1B, trained a CLIP ViT-L/14 to 79.2% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy, 3.7 points above OpenAI's CLIP ViT-L/14. Data Filtering Networks (DFN) then trained a small model only to score pairs, and the resulting DFN-5B set trained a ViT-H to 84.4%.

These results seem to say "filter harder". Goyal et al. (2024) showed that the right amount of filtering depends on how much compute you have.

Utility decays with repetition

The argument starts from a simple observation. High-quality data is scarce. If you filter a pool down to its best 10%, a long training run will see that subset many times. Each repeat teaches less than the first view. Goyal et al. model this with a utility that halves every τ repeats:

bk+1 = b · (1/2)k / τ b is the utility of a sample on its first view; k counts repeats; τ is the half-life in repeats

A small, clean pool starts with high utility per sample and runs out of fresh samples quickly. A large, noisy pool starts lower but stays fresh for longer. Plot accuracy against compute and the curves cross. In their experiment on a 128M-sample pool with ViT-B/32 models, LAION-style filtering to 10% gave the best accuracy early, then plateaued. Given enough training, even the unfiltered pool caught up and passed it.

Insight

Data curation is not compute-agnostic. A filter tuned on a short run picks too small a subset for a long run, because the long run repeats that subset until it stops teaching. In practice, choose the filtering threshold together with the number of samples you will see, and loosen it as the planned run grows. The same logic explains why repeated text data loses value for language models (Muennighoff et al., 2023).

Two more results complete the picture. DFN found that a filter model's own ImageNet accuracy does not predict how good a dataset it produces. A model trained on a small amount of high-quality data can be a better filter than a stronger classifier. And data quality can be raised by rewriting, not only by removing. Recaptioning web images with a strong captioner, as in Recap-DataComp-1B and in MM1's VeCap data, adds information per image without adding images. MM1 found that VeCap captions, only about 7% of its caption data, still gave a 2–4 point boost in few-shot performance.

Removing redundancy is a third lever. SemDeDup removed semantically near-duplicate pairs from LAION and trained to similar quality with less data. Sorscher et al. (2022) showed in theory and experiment that a good pruning metric can beat power-law data scaling. They also found that the best pruning rule depends on how much data you start with: keep easy examples when data is scarce and hard ones when it is abundant. Like Goyal et al., they find no filter that is right at every scale.

Scaling laws for mixed-modal and native models

The encoder studies scale one tower in isolation. A different line of work trains one generative model over several modalities and asks how the modalities interact as the model grows. This is where the first true multimodal scaling laws come from.

Competition and synergy between modalities

Aghajanyan et al. (2023) trained over 250 decoder-only models on seven "modalities": text, images, image–text, speech, speech–text, code and molecules. Images were turned into discrete tokens by a VQ tokenizer, 1,024 tokens per image. Models ranged from 8M to 30B parameters, trained on 5 to 100 billion tokens.

Their question was simple. If you train one model on two modalities, is it better or worse than two separate models given the same per-modality data? They wrote the loss of a model trained on modalities i and j as the average of the two single-modality laws, plus an interaction term:

L(N, Di, Dj) = ½ [L(N, Di) + L(N, Dj)] − Ci,j + Ai,j / Nαi,j + Bi,j / (|Di| + |Dj|)βi,j C is the maximum benefit of sharing; the last two terms are the cost of sharing one set of weights and one optimisation

The shared model wins when the benefit term Ci,j is larger than the two competition terms. Both competition terms shrink as the model and the data grow. So a pair of modalities can compete in a small model and help each other in a large one. The authors used the law to predict the scale at which competition between speech and text would vanish, around a 30B model trained on 45B tokens, and confirmed it with a 30B speech–text model.

Chameleon took this approach to production scale. It is an early-fusion model: images become 1,024 discrete tokens per 512 × 512 image and are mixed with text in one transformer. The 34B model saw 9.2 trillion tokens over 2.1 epochs and used about 4.3 million GPU-hours. There is no pretrained tower to reuse, so the whole cost is paid in the run.

Early fusion versus late fusion, at equal compute

Most VLMs are late-fusion models: a separate image encoder feeds an LLM. Early-fusion models feed image patches almost directly into one shared transformer. Is late fusion better, or just convenient? Shukor et al. (2025) trained 457 native multimodal models from scratch, from 0.3B to 3B parameters and 250M to 400B tokens, on a mix of image captions, interleaved documents and text. They fitted Chinchilla-style laws to the average validation loss.

The loss fell with compute at almost exactly the same rate in both designs: L ∝ C−0.0492 for early fusion and L ∝ C−0.0494 for late fusion. The compute-optimal model size for early fusion grew as Nopt ∝ C0.526, close to Chinchilla's balance. The designs differed in how they wanted the budget split. Late-fusion models wanted more parameters for a given budget. Early-fusion models wanted more training tokens. Early fusion was slightly better at small budgets and cheaper to train.

The same study added mixture-of-experts layers to the early-fusion model. The experts learned to specialise by modality, and the sparse model reached a lower loss at the same compute. Earlier work points the same way: LIMoE trained one sparse contrastive model for both images and text, and Mixture-of-Transformers gave each modality its own non-embedding weights while sharing attention, cutting the compute needed to reach a given loss. Its compute-optimal ratio of parameters to tokens fell as compute grew (the ratio N/D scaled as C−0.312), so sparse models wanted to spend extra budget on data.

Nuance

"No inherent advantage to late fusion" is a statement about models trained from scratch. In practice late fusion's advantage is the free pretrained LLM. Shukor et al. measured it: a native model needed fewer than 100B multimodal tokens to match an LLM-initialised one on captions, but up to 1T tokens on interleaved and text data. NaViL (Tian et al., 2025), studying native models under a data limit, likewise found that initialising from a pretrained LLM helped convergence. So under a fixed budget that does not include the LLM's pretraining, late fusion and LLM initialisation remain the cheap path to strong text ability.

How the data mixture moves the optimum

The mixture of data types changes the law, not just the loss. In Shukor et al., interleaved data benefited more from larger models than caption data did. Wu et al. (2026) trained native mixture-of-experts models from scratch, from 71M to 3B active parameters, on mixtures with different shares of multimodal tokens. They fitted separate allocation laws for the language loss and the multimodal loss. The language law was stable across mixtures, with the compute-optimal model size growing roughly as C0.65. The multimodal law was not. As the multimodal share grew from 10% to 30%, its model-size exponent fell from 0.686 to 0.622, shifting the optimal budget toward more tokens. Shukor et al. (2025, a second paper) went one step further and fitted laws that include the domain weights themselves, so that small runs can predict the best mixture for an 8B model.

The practical rules in production recipes are cruder but point the same way. MM1 found a 5:5:1 ratio of captions, interleaved documents and text kept both few-shot and text performance. DeepSeek-VL found it needed at least 70% language data to protect the LLM. The site's article on mixing data without losing capabilities covers why mixtures interfere.

Scaling the encoder inside a native model

NaViL (Tian et al., 2025) asked the encoder question inside an end-to-end trained model. With the LLM fixed, growing the visual encoder from 75M to 2.4B parameters gave diminishing returns: past some size, the loss barely moved. The LLM's capacity capped the benefit. Growing the LLM, by contrast, kept lowering the loss in the usual log-linear way. They defined the optimal encoder size for each LLM as the point where doubling the encoder stopped paying, and found that its logarithm grew linearly with the logarithm of the LLM size.

Insight

The vision encoder should grow with the LLM, but much more slowly. NaViL's log-linear relation, SmolVLM's preference for a 93M encoder with a 135M LLM, Qwen3-VL's 300M encoder for its 2B and 4B models, and InternVL3.5's use of a 300M encoder up to 30B and a 6B encoder only for its 38B and 241B models all point the same way. A family that pairs one encoder with every LLM size is over-spending at the small end or under-spending at the large end.

How to split parameters between the towers

Native-model studies are clean but small. Production VLMs are late-fusion models built from pretrained parts. For them, the scaling question becomes: given a family of encoders and a family of LLMs, which pair should I buy? There is no fitted law for this yet, but many ablations bear on it.

The case for a big encoder

PaLI (Chen et al., 2022) argued that vision had been under-scaled. Language models were already tens of billions of parameters, while the largest ViTs were a few billion. PaLI trained a 4B-parameter ViT-e and paired it with the 13B mT5-XXL. The authors reported that scaling the vision component gave a better return per parameter than scaling the language component, so PaLI-17B split its capacity fairly evenly between the modalities. PaLI-X continued the trend with the 22B ViT and a 32B UL2 backbone, and reported that scaling both components kept helping.

InternVL (Chen et al., 2023) made the same bet in open models: a 6B InternViT aligned progressively with LLMs. InternVL 2.5 later argued that a large encoder reduces how much multimodal data the model needs, and NVLM used the same InternViT-6B, frozen, for its 72B models.

The case for a strong LLM

Other studies found the LLM matters more. Idefics2 (Laurençon et al., 2024) fixed the backbone sizes, the data and the number of updates, then swapped parts. Replacing LLaMA-1-7B with Mistral-7B improved the average benchmark score by 5.1 points. Replacing CLIP-ViT-H with SigLIP-So400m improved it by 3.3. Their first finding reads: for a fixed number of parameters, the quality of the language model has a higher impact than the quality of the vision backbone. MM1 found that moving from ViT-L to ViT-H, a doubling of encoder parameters, added less than 1%, while raising resolution from 224 to 336 pixels added about 3%. Li et al. (2024), in a study titled "Are bigger encoders always better", found that enlarging the encoder alone did not reliably improve the VLM.

The "what transfers" study of Li et al. (2026) gives the most direct evidence for the LLM side. They trained over 150 VLMs on 34 LLMs from 7 families with one controlled recipe. A low-dimensional capability score extracted from the LLM's text benchmarks predicted the VLM's multimodal accuracy, and the fit extrapolated from backbones up to 8B to 72B. Base LLMs made better VLM backbones than their instruction-tuned versions.

Nuance

The two camps are measuring different things. PaLI trained its own large encoders well and measured return per added parameter at a time when LLMs were already large. Idefics2 compared existing encoders, one of which (EVA-CLIP-5B) the authors suspected was under-trained, and measured at a fixed total size. Both can be right: a well-trained larger encoder helps, a large but poorly trained one does not, and once the encoder is decent the LLM is where extra parameters pay most. Read every "encoder versus LLM" ablation together with how each encoder was trained.

What the open families actually chose

Figure 7 shows the split for fifteen published models. Three views tell three stories. As a share of parameters, the vision encoder is usually a few percent of a large model. On a log scale, encoders stay in a narrow band from 0.1B to 6B while LLMs span three orders of magnitude. As a share of training compute per image, the picture changes again: small models and models with large encoders or aggressive merging spend a large fraction of their compute on vision.

Insight

Encoders have barely scaled while LLMs grew a hundredfold. Across Table 1, the encoder stays between 0.1B and 6B parameters; the LLM ranges from 0.135B to 235B. The capacity that went into vision instead went into tokens: higher resolution, tiling, native aspect ratios. For a planner this means the main vision scaling knob in a late-fusion VLM is not NV but V, and its cost is paid in the LLM.

Visual tokens are a scaling axis

If the encoder barely grows, what does scale the vision side? The number of visual tokens. More tokens means more pixels reach the LLM, which helps reading, counting and small objects. It also means a longer sequence through every LLM layer, in training and in every request. This section asks how to trade tokens against model size.

Resolution beats encoder size, until it does not

MM1's encoder ablations put image resolution first: going from 224 to 336 pixels added about 3% across metrics, more than doubling the encoder. Its connector ablations reached the same conclusion from the other side: the number of visual tokens and the resolution mattered most, and the connector type had little effect. In its fine-tuning experiments with sub-image splitting, MM1 saw roughly a 15% relative gain from 336 to 1,344 pixels, and a slight drop at 1,792 pixels, which the authors attribute to evaluation images being smaller than that.

Idefics2 found the opposite for its resampler: no gain beyond 64 visual tokens per image in its setup. The authors suggested that more tokens might pay off only with much longer training. Its image-splitting option, which raises the count to 320, did help text-heavy benchmarks such as TextVQA and DocVQA. So the value of a token depends on the task and on what the token carries.

Model size versus resolution at equal cost

PaliGemma 2 gives the cleanest controlled comparison. The same recipe was run with three LLM sizes (3B, 10B, 28B) and three resolutions (224, 448, 896 pixels, giving 256, 1,024 and 4,096 tokens). The paper reports the relative training cost per example. The 3B model at 896 pixels costs 23.5 units; the 28B model at 224 pixels costs 18.9. A small model at high resolution is more expensive to train than a large model at low resolution. The authors note that the encoder is small in parameters but the vision tokens dominate the LLM's compute.

The tasks split cleanly. Text, document, screen and chart understanding gained mainly from resolution. Multilingual captioning and tasks that need reasoning or world knowledge, such as AI2D, CountBenchQA and NLVR2, gained mainly from LLM size. Going from 10B to 28B often gave only small gains.

An inference-optimal law

Li et al. (2024) made the trade-off quantitative for deployment. Inference cost is roughly proportional to N · (Q + V), where Q is the number of text tokens in the prompt and V the number of visual tokens after compression. They trained LLaVA-style models on Qwen-1.5 LLMs from 0.5B to 14B parameters, compressed the visual tokens to between 1 and 576, and fitted the average error on nine benchmarks:

Y(N, V) = A / Nα + B / Vβ + D fitted α = 0.077 for LLM size and β = 0.015 for visual tokens, on visual reasoning benchmarks

Because α is about five times β, error grows much faster when you shrink the LLM than when you shrink the visual tokens. So for a fixed inference budget on visual reasoning tasks, the best choice was the largest LLM that fits, with very few visual tokens, often just one. The trade-off reversed for OCR and document tasks, where compression destroys the information and the optimum keeps as many tokens as possible. Long text prompts also shifted the optimum toward more visual tokens, because the text already dominates the sequence cost.

Nuance

"One visual token" is an average over a benchmark mix that is mostly coarse visual reasoning, measured with one LLM family and one compression method. The same paper shows the opposite optimum for OCR. Zhuang et al. (2026) stress-tested how much text a vision token can carry and found a stable regime, an unstable regime and a collapse as the characters per token grow. The practical rule is to set the visual token budget per task type, not per model, and to test it on the densest inputs you expect.

Train-optimal and deploy-optimal VLMs

The language-model lesson carries over with a twist. A deploy-optimal LLM is smaller and trained longer than a train-optimal one. A deploy-optimal VLM has a second lever: it can spend training compute on a model that works well with fewer visual tokens. Token compression, dynamic routing between resolutions and efficient encoders all trade a little training effort for a lot of serving cost. InternVL3.5's visual resolution router and FastVLM's hybrid encoder are examples. Post 16, The Cost of a Picture, covers these methods in detail. For scaling, the point is that the visual token count belongs in the scaling law, next to N and D.

Scaling post-training data and compute

Pretraining is where scaling laws were born. Post-training is where most VLM recipes now spend their design effort. The data volumes vary over four orders of magnitude between open recipes, and there is only one fitted law so far.

How much instruction data

LLaVA-1.5 used 558K samples to align the connector and 665K for instruction tuning. LLaVA-OneVision grew this to four stages: 558K for alignment, 4M of high-quality knowledge data, 3.2M single-image instructions and 1.6M mixed single-image, multi-image and video samples. Cambrian-1 collected Cambrian-10M and curated it down to 7M. Qwen2.5-VL's SFT set has about 2 million entries, half of them text-only. InternVL3.5 reports about 130B tokens of SFT. Dataset efforts kept pushing the open supply: MAmmoTH-VL built 12M instruction pairs with rationales, Infinity-MM collected over 40M samples, and FineVision unified 24M samples from more than 200 sources with decontamination against 66 benchmarks.

More is not automatically better. Cambrian-1 capped the number of samples taken from any one source and varied the cap. A cap between 250K and 350K samples gave the best average; both lower and higher caps did worse. The authors describe this as an elbow in the data-balancing curve. The site's article on instruction tuning covers the language-side mechanics, and post 06, One Model, Many Visual Skills, covers visual instruction tuning.

Insight

Instruction data scale interacts with the choice of encoder. Cambrian-1 found that a self-supervised DINOv2 encoder, weak with 0.7M instruction samples, improved markedly with 5M samples and an unfrozen encoder, narrowing the gap to CLIP. An encoder ranking made with a small instruction set can therefore be wrong for the final recipe. Compare encoders at the instruction-data scale you will actually use.

A scaling law for supervised fine-tuning

Zhang et al. (2026) fitted the first scaling law for VLM supervised fine-tuning. They trained 60 models from 50M to 8B parameters, saved 1,560 checkpoints and evaluated each on more than ten benchmarks. They argue that the SFT frontier is harder to compute than an LLM's, because model size, the LLM's pretraining tokens and the SFT tokens all interact. They proposed one law for teams that control their data and one for open models whose training data is unknown. They also report that training loss correlated strongly with downstream performance (R² = 0.98).

Nuance

MM1 reported the opposite for pretraining: validation loss was not strongly correlated with downstream performance, so they fitted their learning-rate law to 8-shot benchmark scores instead. The two findings are compatible. In SFT the training data and the benchmarks are close in format; in pretraining on web captions they are not. Use loss as a proxy only after checking the correlation for your own stage and data.

Reinforcement learning and test-time compute

RL stages are now standard, and some reports give their cost. InternVL3.5 compared RL methods on its 8B model. Offline preference optimisation (MPO) took about 0.3K GPU-hours. Online GSPO took about 5.5K GPU-hours for one episode and 11K for two. Their cascade of the two took about 5.8K and gave the best reasoning score. These are small numbers next to pretraining, but they buy most of the reasoning gains reported. The mechanics are in post 12, Rewarding What the Model Sees.

The last axis is compute at inference. InternVL 2.5 reported that chain-of-thought raised its 78B model's MMMU score from 66.4% to 70.1%. Thinking longer, cropping and zooming are a separate way to scale, with their own trade-offs. Post 13, Thinking With Images, covers multimodal test-time scaling, and How Models Improve Without Changing Their Weights covers the language-model case.

What open VLMs actually spend

Scaling laws are about allocation. To reason about allocation, it helps to know the actual numbers. Few open VLM papers report compute, and they report it in different units: chip-days, GPU-hours, tokens or steps. Table 2 collects what is reported and converts it into an approximate FLOP count with a stated method.

Two conversion methods are used. When the token count is reported, the estimate is 6 · N · D with N the total parameters, which overstates frozen stages. When only hardware time is reported, the estimate is chip-hours × peak bf16 throughput × 40% utilisation. Peak dense bf16 throughput is 312 TFLOP/s for an A100, 989 for an H100, 275 for a TPUv4 chip and 197 for a TPUv5e chip. These are order-of-magnitude estimates, not measurements.

Insight

For late-fusion VLMs, the largest compute decision is made before the VLM run starts: the choice of LLM. InternVL2.5-78B's multimodal training is under 1% of the FLOPs that went into its Qwen2.5-72B backbone; Qwen2.5-VL-72B's pretraining is roughly a quarter. A scaling law fitted only to the VLM stage therefore describes a small, cheap adjustment to a fixed, expensive prior. In practice, pick the strongest LLM you can serve, then spend the VLM budget on tokens per image and data quality, which are the knobs the VLM stage controls.

Nuance

Low multimodal compute is not evidence that more would not help. Qwen2.5-VL raised its pretraining from 1.2T to 4.1T tokens between versions, and Qwen3-VL reports about 2.2T tokens of multimodal training while matching or surpassing comparable text-only backbones on language benchmarks in several cases. No published study has yet fitted how VLM-stage tokens trade against LLM size at fixed total cost.

What is still unknown

No Chinchilla for late-fusion VLMs

Nobody has published the late-fusion equivalent of Chinchilla: for a fixed total budget, how to choose the encoder size, the LLM size, the visual token count and the multimodal token count together. The native-model laws are the closest, but they start from random weights and stay below a few billion parameters. Production VLMs start from pretrained towers whose cost is sunk and whose quality varies by family. Li et al. (2026) suggest the right variable for the LLM may be a capability score, not a parameter count.

From loss to capability

A scaling law predicts loss. VLM users care about reading a chart or finding an object. MM1 found pretraining loss a poor guide to downstream scores, while the SFT study found a tight relation. Benchmarks also saturate unevenly: WebLI-100B's data scaling looked flat on ImageNet and steep on cultural tasks. Post 07, Measuring What a VLM Can Do, covers which benchmarks measure what, and why a single average hides these effects.

Repeating images

For text, Muennighoff et al. (2023) measured how the value of repeated tokens decays: up to about four epochs repeated data was almost as good as new data. For images, the data-filtering work measures a similar decay in contrastive training. For generative VLM pretraining, where the same image can appear with many captions, rewritten captions and generated questions, the effective number of unique examples is not well defined. High-quality caption sets such as PixMo-Cap, with 712K images, are small enough that long runs must repeat them, and nobody has fitted how their value decays.

Tokens per image as a variable

Every law above treats the number of visual tokens as fixed per experiment, or varies it only at inference. Native-resolution models such as Qwen2-VL see a different token count for every image. How a training run should allocate tokens across images of different difficulty, and how that interacts with model size, is open. So is the scaling of video, where the token count multiplies again by the number of frames; post 11, Seeing in Time, covers that case.

What the encoder should learn at scale

Web-SSL showed self-supervised encoders keep improving to 7B on matched data. Perception Encoder argued that the best features for a VLM are not at the encoder's output layer. AIMv2 trained encoders with an autoregressive multimodal objective and reported better multimodal understanding than CLIP and SigLIP. Which objective scales best as the encoder of a VLM, rather than as a stand-alone model, is not settled.

A planning procedure

Putting the evidence together gives a procedure for a late-fusion VLM under a fixed budget. Each step names the result it rests on.

  1. Fix the deployment constraints first. The serving budget per request bounds NL · (Q + V). Decide it before choosing parts, because it rules out combinations (Li et al., 2024).
  2. Choose the LLM by capability, not size alone. Within the serving bound, the strongest base LLM is the largest lever (Idefics2; Li et al., 2026). Prefer a base checkpoint to an instruction-tuned one.
  3. Choose a well-trained encoder of modest size, matched to the LLM. Use the compute-share formula to check that the encoder does not dominate a small model (Figure 2; SmolVLM; NaViL).
  4. Set the visual token budget per task family. Document and OCR workloads need many tokens; coarse visual reasoning needs few (PaliGemma 2; Li et al., 2024). Budget the cost in the LLM, not in the encoder.
  5. Size the data filter to the run. A longer run needs a larger, less aggressively filtered pool, or rewritten captions (Goyal et al., 2024).
  6. Keep enough text in the mixture. Protect language ability with a substantial text share (MM1; DeepSeek-VL), and measure it.
  7. Fit hyperparameters on small runs. MM1 fitted its peak learning rate as a function of size on runs from 9M to 1.2B parameters and extrapolated to 30B: η = exp(−0.4214 ln N − 0.5535).
  8. Check that your proxy tracks your goal. Verify the loss–benchmark correlation at your stage before trusting loss curves (MM1; Zhang et al., 2026).

Key insights and nuances

  • The encoder's compute share scales with the merge factor. Its share is NV·m² / (NV·m² + NL·(1 + T/V)): about 64% for Qwen2-VL-2B and under 4% for Qwen2-VL-72B. Small VLMs are vision-bound; large ones are sequence-bound. → budget
  • A frozen LLM is not free. When the connector below it trains, it still costs about 4 of 6 FLOPs per parameter per token. Its savings are mostly memory. → budget
  • Reported VLM tokens hide sunk cost. InternVL2.5-78B's ≈120B tokens and Qwen2-VL's 1.4T are not comparable without counting the encoders and LLMs they reuse. → budget
  • Encoder quality is not encoder size. A shape-optimised 400M ViT matches a 2.5× larger one, and a 5B CLIP encoder matched a 400M SigLIP inside Idefics2. → vision tower
  • Saturated benchmarks can hide unsaturated capability. Going from 10B to 100B pairs barely moved ImageNet but raised Dollar Street geolocalisation from 35.9% to 41.7% at equal compute. → vision tower
  • The best data filter depends on the compute budget. Repeated high-quality data loses utility, so long runs need larger, less filtered pools. → data
  • Early and late fusion scale at the same rate but want different splits. Late fusion wants more parameters, early fusion more tokens; late fusion's practical edge is the free pretrained LLM. → fusion
  • The encoder should grow with the LLM, slowly. NaViL's log-linear optimum and the size-dependent encoder choices of Qwen3-VL and InternVL3.5 agree. → fusion
  • "Encoder versus LLM" ablations disagree because encoders were trained differently. A well-trained larger encoder helps; a large under-trained one does not. → split
  • Vision capacity moved from encoder weights into tokens. Encoders stayed between 0.1B and 6B while token counts per image rose into the thousands. → split
  • At equal inference cost, LLM size usually beats visual tokens, except for reading. In Li et al., α ≈ 5β for visual reasoning; OCR reverses the optimum. → tokens
  • A small model at high resolution can cost more than a large model at low resolution. PaliGemma 2's 3B at 896 px costs 23.5 units per example; its 28B at 224 px costs 18.9. → tokens
  • Loss is a good proxy in SFT and a weak one in pretraining. R² = 0.98 for SFT versus MM1's weak pretraining correlation. → post-training
  • For late-fusion VLMs the biggest compute decision is the LLM. The multimodal stage costs from under 1% to about a quarter of the backbone's pretraining. → compute
