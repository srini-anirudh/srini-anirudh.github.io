From a click to a motor command

The previous post ended with agents that use a computer. They look at a screenshot and emit an action such as click(412, 230). This post asks what changes when the thing being controlled is a robot arm.

Take one instruction: put the cup in the bowl. A GUI agent would need a handful of discrete actions. Each one is a symbol with a few arguments. The screen waits patiently while the model thinks. If the agent clicks the wrong button, it can often press undo.

A robot gets none of this. It must send a vector of real numbers to its motors many times per second. A typical command says how far to move the hand along three axes, how much to rotate it, and whether to close the gripper. If the commands arrive late, the arm stops or jerks. If one command is slightly wrong, the next camera frame shows a world the robot has never seen. There is no undo for a dropped cup.

So the core question of this post is simple to state. A vision-language model produces words. How do we turn it into a model that produces a fast stream of continuous motor commands, and what does the language model actually contribute?

The answer is the vision-language-action model, or VLA. A VLA takes camera images, a language instruction and usually the robot's own sensor readings. It outputs robot actions. It is built by starting from a pretrained vision-language model and training it further on robot demonstrations. The name comes from RT-2, published by Google DeepMind in 2023. Since then the idea has grown into a family of open and closed models: OpenVLA, Octo, π0 and its successors, GR00T N1, Gemini Robotics, SmolVLA and others.

Figure 1 shows the loop we are trying to learn. Keep it in mind. Every design choice later in the post is about one part of it: what the policy sees, what it outputs, how often it runs, and what happens between calls.

The rest of the post builds the idea from the ground up. We start with the control problem and why it is hard even without language. Then we look at the two lines of work that existed before VLAs, the step RT-2 took, and the many ways to represent an action. After that we cover the systems questions: speed, data, training stages, reinforcement learning, video pretraining, evaluation and safety. Along the way, two tables compare action representations and models along the aspects the post introduces.

The control problem

Strip away language for a moment. A robot policy is a function π(at | ot) that maps an observation at time t to an action. Four words in that sentence need care: observation, action, time and loop.

What the robot observes

The observation usually has two parts. The first is one or more camera images. A fixed camera looks at the whole scene. A wrist camera, mounted next to the gripper, gives a close view of the object being grasped. Many VLAs use two or three cameras at once.

The second part is proprioception: the robot's readings of its own body. These are the joint angles, the gripper opening and sometimes joint velocities or forces. Proprioception is cheap and precise. It tells the policy where the hand is, which the camera can only estimate. Web images and text contain nothing like it, which is one reason a VLM cannot simply be prompted to control a robot.

What an action is

An action is a vector of numbers sent to a low-level controller. Its meaning depends on the robot and on the chosen control mode. Three common choices:

  • End-effector deltas. Move the hand by (Δx, Δy, Δz), rotate it by three small angles, and set the gripper. That is seven numbers for one arm. RT-1, RT-2 and OpenVLA use this kind of space.
  • Joint positions. Set a target angle for every joint. The bimanual ALOHA system records the target joint positions of two arms at 50 Hz.
  • Whole-body commands. Humanoids and mobile manipulators add a base, a torso and dexterous hands. Figure's Helix controls 35 degrees of freedom of a humanoid upper body.

Different robots therefore produce actions of different sizes and meanings. A model trained on many robots has to reconcile them. We return to this in the section on cross-embodiment training.

How often the policy must act

The control frequency is how many commands per second the robot receives. RT-1 ran at 3 Hz. Many public datasets record between 2 and 10 Hz. Fine bimanual tasks such as threading a cable tie use 50 Hz. Helix's fast controller runs at 200 Hz.

Frequency matters because contact is fast. When a gripper touches a cup, the useful correction happens within tens of milliseconds. ACT's authors ran a small user study on their ALOHA system: lowering teleoperation from 50 Hz to 5 Hz made precise tasks clearly harder for the human operators. A policy that imitates those operators inherits the same need.

Frequency also sets a time budget. At 50 Hz there are 20 milliseconds between commands. A 7-billion-parameter model cannot run a full forward pass in that time on a robot's computer. Much of the VLA literature is about escaping this budget.

Imitation and the closed loop

Most VLAs are trained by behaviour cloning. A human operates the robot, usually by teleoperation, and the system records pairs of observations and actions. The policy is then trained like any supervised model. It maximises the likelihood of the demonstrated action:

L(θ) = − ∑(o, a) ∈ D log πθ(a | o)D is the set of demonstrated observation-action pairs

This looks like next-token prediction for language. The difference shows up at test time. The robot runs in a closed loop: act, observe the result, act again. Each action changes the next observation. The model's own mistakes decide what it sees next.

Why errors compound

Here is the classic argument, due to Ross and Bagnell. Suppose the learned policy makes a mistake with probability ε at each step, as long as it stays on states the demonstrations covered. A task lasts T steps.

Now suppose the first mistake happens at step t. The robot is now in a state the demonstrator never visited. Nothing in the training data says how to recover, so in the worst case the policy keeps erring for the remaining T − t steps. Add this up over all possible first-mistake times:

expected mistakes ≤ ∑t=1T ε (T − t + 1) ≈ εT2 / 2compared with εT if every step were judged on the expert's own states

A supervised classifier makes about εT mistakes on T examples. An imitating policy can make on the order of εT2. The bound is a worst case, but the effect is real and visible in every robot learning paper. Figure 2 animates it.

Two remedies follow directly from the derivation. The first is to collect data on the learner's own states. DAgger does this by asking the expert to label what the learner should have done. In robotics this becomes human interventions: an operator takes over when the robot drifts, and the correction is added to the data. BC-Z, HIL-SERL, ConRFT and π*0.6 all use some form of it.

The second remedy is to make fewer decisions. The bound grows with the number of decisions T, not with seconds. If the policy predicts a chunk of H future actions at once, a task of T steps needs only T/H decisions.

Insight

Compounding error scales with the number of decisions, not with wall-clock time. A ten-second task at 50 Hz is 500 decisions if the policy predicts one action at a time, but only 10 if it predicts one-second chunks. ACT's ablation shows the size of the effect: on simulated bimanual tasks, success rose from 1% without chunking to 44% with long chunks. So before making a policy bigger, check how many decisions it is asked to make.

Demonstrations disagree with each other

One more property of robot data shapes everything that follows. Human demonstrations are multimodal. Faced with an obstacle, one operator goes left and another goes right. Both are correct.

A policy trained with mean squared error predicts the average of the two. The average goes straight into the obstacle. So the policy needs an output that can represent several distinct good actions. Categorical tokens can do this. So can diffusion and flow models. A plain regression head cannot, unless the data happen to be unimodal. Keep this in mind for the section on action representations.

Before VLAs: two separate lines of work

Before 2023, two lines of research approached language-conditioned robots from opposite ends. One trained policies that could move but knew little. The other used language models that knew a lot but could not move.

Policies that learned to move

The first line trained end-to-end visuomotor policies with language conditioning. CLIPort (2021) combined a "what" pathway from CLIP, which understands concepts, with a "where" pathway from Transporter networks, which is spatially precise. BC-Z (2022) scaled real-robot imitation to more than 100 tasks, using demonstrations and human interventions. It could perform 24 unseen tasks with an average success of 44%, conditioned on language or on a video of a human.

RT-1 (December 2022) is the direct ancestor of the VLA. It used an EfficientNet conditioned on the instruction through FiLM layers, a TokenLearner to compress image tokens, and a Transformer to output actions. It had 35 million parameters and ran at 3 Hz. Google collected its data over 17 months with 13 robots: 130,000 episodes covering more than 700 tasks. RT-1 performed its training instructions at 97% success.

RT-1 also fixed an action format that later models inherited. Each action had seven arm dimensions (position, rotation, gripper), three base dimensions and a mode switch. Each continuous dimension was mapped to one of 256 uniform bins, and the model predicted bins with a cross-entropy loss. Actions had become tokens, even though no language model was involved yet.

Language models that planned

The second line used large language models as planners. SayCan (2022) let a language model propose the next skill in words, such as "pick up the sponge". A value function for each pretrained skill then scored how feasible that skill was in the current scene. The robot executed the skill with the best combined score. Inner Monologue fed success detections and scene descriptions back to the language model as text. Code as Policies had the model write Python that called perception and control functions. VoxPoser had it compose 3D value maps for a motion planner.

PaLM-E (2023) went further. It inserted images and continuous state estimates directly into the token sequence of a language model and trained the result end to end on embodied tasks, visual question answering and captioning. Its largest version had 562 billion parameters. It showed positive transfer: robot planning improved when the model also trained on internet-scale vision-language data. But PaLM-E still produced plans in words. Separate low-level policies turned those words into motion.

A third, smaller thread tried to put everything into one token stream. Gato (2022) trained a single network to output text, button presses or joint torques depending on context. VIMA showed that many manipulation tasks can be specified with prompts that mix words and images.

Nuance

In SayCan the language model only chooses among skills that already exist. Its knowledge decides which skill to run, never how to move. The same limit returns in end-to-end VLAs. RT-2's authors report that web pretraining improved which objects and instructions the robot could handle, but gave it no new motions. When a VLA fails, it is worth asking which of the two kinds of knowledge was missing.

So by early 2023 there was a gap. Planners understood the world but needed a library of skills. Policies had skills but understood only what was in their training set. The VLA closes the gap by asking one model to do both.

RT-2: actions as text tokens

RT-2 (July 2023) made a deliberately simple move. Take an existing vision-language model. Write the robot action as a string of numbers. Fine-tune the model to output that string. No new layers, no new heads.

The recipe

An RT-2 action has eight parts: a flag that ends the episode, six numbers for the change in hand position and rotation, and the gripper extension. Each continuous part is mapped to one of 256 bins, as in RT-1. The action becomes eight integers.

The integers must become tokens. RT-2 was built on two VLM families. In PaLI-X, every integer up to 1000 already has its own token, so bin 128 is simply the token for "128". PaLM-E has no such tokens, so the authors overwrote the 256 least frequently used tokens in its vocabulary with action bins. The training example then looks like a visual question:

image + "Q: what action should the robot take to pick up the apple? A:" → "1 128 91 241 5 101 127"illustrative action string: termination flag, six motion bins, gripper bin

The most important detail is co-fine-tuning. RT-2 did not fine-tune on robot data alone. It mixed robot trajectories with the original web vision-language data, so the model kept answering visual questions while it learned to act. At inference time the output is restricted to valid action tokens when the robot asks for an action.

What it gained, and what it cost

The largest RT-2 model had 55 billion parameters. It could not run on the robot. The team served it from a multi-TPU cloud service, queried over the network. The 55B model ran at 1 to 3 Hz. A 5B version ran at about 5 Hz. That is slow enough to limit the tasks to fairly simple tabletop motions.

In return, RT-2 showed what its authors called emergent capabilities. Across about 6,000 evaluation trials it generalised better to new objects, backgrounds and environments than RT-1. It followed commands that never appeared in robot data, such as moving an object onto a particular number or icon. It could pick the smallest object, or the one closest to another. With chain-of-thought prompting it chose a rock as an improvised hammer.

Insight

The same 5B architecture trained on the same robot data scored 9% from scratch and 42% when initialised from a web-pretrained VLM. So the first lever for generalisation is the pretrained initialisation, not the robot architecture.

The paper is just as clear about the limit. Web pretraining improved generalisation over semantic and visual concepts. But in the authors' words, the robot did not acquire any ability to perform new motions. Its physical skills stayed within the distribution of skills in the robot data. It learned to deploy those skills in new ways. That sentence frames much of the work that followed: semantic knowledge comes from the VLM, motor knowledge must come from somewhere else.

One policy for many robots

If motor knowledge must come from robot data, the obvious step is to pool robot data from everywhere. That is harder than it sounds. Labs use different arms, cameras, grippers, control modes and frequencies. Each dataset is an island.

Open X-Embodiment

The Open X-Embodiment collaboration (October 2023) pooled 60 existing datasets from 21 institutions into one format. The result has more than one million real robot trajectories from 22 robot embodiments, covering 527 skills. The authors trained two models on it. RT-1-X uses the RT-1 architecture. RT-2-X uses the 55B RT-2 model.

The results were mixed in an informative way. On small datasets, RT-1-X beat the methods originally trained on each dataset in four of five cases: other robots' data helped where data was scarce. On the two large datasets, RT-1-X did worse than RT-1 trained only on the target robot. The authors read this as underfitting. RT-2-X, with far more capacity, did well on both.

RT-2-X also gained skills. On the Google robot it learned tasks that appeared only in the Bridge dataset, which was collected on a different arm. But on the standard generalisation tests (new objects, backgrounds, environments), RT-2-X and RT-2 performed roughly the same. RT-2 already generalised well there thanks to its VLM backbone.

Insight

The Open X-Embodiment results separate two sources of improvement. Data from other robots added new skills but not new visual generalisation. The VLM backbone added visual and semantic generalisation but not new skills. And a 35M-parameter model underfit the pooled data where a 55B model did not. So mixing many robots needs a high-capacity model, and it pays off mainly as broader motor coverage.

Making different robots fit one model

Pooling data forces a decision about mismatched action spaces. Four approaches appear in the literature.

  • Pad to the largest robot. π0 uses an 18-dimensional action vector: enough for two 6-DoF arms, two grippers, a mobile base and a vertical torso. Smaller robots fill the unused slots with zeros.
  • Use a fixed physical layout. RDT-1B defines a 128-dimensional unified action space. Each slot has a physical meaning, such as the right arm's joint positions or the end-effector velocity. Every robot writes its values into the matching slots.
  • Give each robot its own adapters. GR00T N1 uses a small MLP per embodiment to encode states and actions into a shared width. HPT pretrains a shared trunk with robot-specific input stems. CrossFormer trains one transformer on 900,000 trajectories from 20 embodiments, including arms, quadrupeds, navigation robots and drones.
  • Learn a shared notion of motion. Gemini Robotics 1.5 reports a mechanism it calls Motion Transfer that lets one model control an ALOHA, a bi-arm Franka and an Apollo humanoid without robot-specific post-training. The report does not give enough detail to describe it further.

Octo and OpenVLA: the open baselines

Octo (May 2024) was trained on 800,000 Open X-Embodiment trajectories. It is a transformer trained from scratch, with 27M and 93M parameter versions. It takes language or goal images as the task, and a diffusion head produces the actions. Octo was designed to be fine-tuned to new sensors and action spaces in a few hours on consumer GPUs. Strictly, it is a generalist robot policy rather than a VLA, because it does not start from a VLM.

OpenVLA (June 2024) is the open counterpart of RT-2. It starts from the Prismatic VLM, which fuses two vision encoders (SigLIP and DINOv2) with a Llama 2 7B language model. Post 02, Inside a Modern Vision Encoder, explains why fusing a language-aligned encoder with a self-supervised one helps spatial tasks. OpenVLA was trained on 970,000 demonstrations. Actions use 256 bins per dimension, with bin edges set from the 1st to 99th percentile of the data so that rare outliers do not stretch the range. The bins replace the 256 least used tokens of the Llama tokenizer.

Training used 64 A100 GPUs for 14 days, about 21,500 A100-hours. The 7B model runs at about 6 Hz on one RTX 4090. It outperformed the 55B RT-2-X by 16.5 percentage points of absolute success across 29 tasks. It can be fine-tuned with LoRA on consumer GPUs, as described in How LoRA Changes a Model Without Rewriting It.

The OpenVLA paper also lists design findings that run against VLM habits:

  • Train the vision encoder. Freezing the vision encoder usually helps when building a VLM. For OpenVLA, fine-tuning it was crucial. The authors suspect the pretrained features miss fine spatial detail that control needs.
  • Resolution did not help yet. Inputs of 224 and 384 pixels performed the same, while 384 pixels took three times longer to train.
  • Many epochs. LLM and VLM runs usually see their data once or twice. OpenVLA trained for 27 epochs. Real-robot success kept rising until action-token accuracy on the training set passed 95%.

Nuance

"Freeze the vision encoder" is good advice for building a VLM and bad advice for OpenVLA. The models in this post disagree, though. RDT-1B keeps SigLIP frozen to save memory. GR00T N1 trains its vision encoder but freezes the language model. The safe reading is narrower: control needs spatial detail that web pretraining may not provide, so test an unfrozen vision encoder before assuming the VLM recipe transfers.

How to represent an action

A language model ends in a softmax over a vocabulary. A robot needs real numbers. Between those two ends sits the most consequential design choice in a VLA. This section walks through the options in the order the field discovered them.

Bins as tokens

The RT-1, RT-2 and OpenVLA approach maps each action dimension to one of 256 bins. With actions normalised to [−1, 1], a bin is 2/256 wide, so the rounding error is at most half a bin, or 1/256 in absolute units. That is 1/512 of the range. That is fine for coarse tabletop moves.

Two problems appear as tasks get faster. The first is speed. Decoding is sequential, one token per forward pass of the whole model. A seven-dimensional action costs seven passes.

The second problem is subtler, and the FAST paper explains it well. Suppose the policy predicts a one-second chunk at 50 Hz, one token per dimension per step. That is 50 × 7 = 350 tokens. A smooth arm motion changes very little in 20 milliseconds. So given the previous token, the next one is nearly determined. The model can reach a low loss by copying. The training signal carried by each token, its marginal information, goes toward zero as the control frequency rises. The FAST authors found that autoregressive VLAs with per-step binning failed completely on their dexterous high-frequency tasks.

Predicting chunks

Action chunking comes from ACT (2023), the method trained on the low-cost ALOHA bimanual system. The policy predicts the next H actions at once instead of one. ACT trained a conditional VAE over chunks, so it could represent different styles of demonstration. The paper reports that ACT learned six difficult real-world tasks from about ten minutes of demonstrations, with 80 to 90% success on tasks such as slotting a battery.

Chunking helps for two reasons. It divides the number of decisions by H, which is the compounding-error argument from the control section. It also lets the model represent pauses and other patterns that span several steps, which a policy that sees only the current frame cannot represent.

But chunking has a cost: while a chunk executes, the robot is blind to new observations. If someone moves the cup halfway through, the robot keeps reaching for the old position. ACT softened this with temporal ensembling: it predicts a new chunk every step and averages the overlapping predictions. The Bidirectional Decoding paper analysed the trade-off directly. Chunking captures the temporal structure of demonstrations, but at the cost of reactivity to unexpected states. Figure 3 lets you feel the trade-off.

Two newer methods attack the trade-off at inference time. Real-time chunking (RTC, 2025) starts computing the next chunk while the current one executes. Actions that will run before the new chunk is ready are frozen. The rest of the new chunk is generated by inpainting, so it joins smoothly. It works with any diffusion or flow policy without retraining. SmolVLA uses a similar asynchronous scheme to keep a small model responsive.

Compressing chunks: FAST

FAST (January 2025) keeps tokens but fixes what they encode. The idea comes from JPEG. A smooth signal can be described by a few low-frequency cosine components. FAST applies the discrete cosine transform (DCT) to each action dimension of a chunk:

Ck = ∑n=0H−1 an cos[ π k (2n + 1) / 2H ],  k = 0, …, H−1an is one action dimension at step n of the chunk; Ck is the weight of the k-th cosine

The full tokenizer has five steps.

  1. Normalise each dimension so that its 1st and 99th percentiles map to −1 and 1.
  2. Apply the DCT to each dimension separately.
  3. Scale and round the coefficients. Most become zero. The scale factor trades precision against length.
  4. Flatten the sparse matrix, putting all dimensions' low-frequency coefficients first.
  5. Compress the integer sequence with byte-pair encoding, the same algorithm used for text. This gives a fixed vocabulary that slots into the VLM's tokenizer.

The authors also released FAST+, a universal tokenizer trained on one million real robot trajectories. Combined with π0, FAST matched the performance of the diffusion-based π0 while training up to five times faster. It worked on dexterous tasks where per-step binning had failed.

Nuance

FAST makes autoregressive VLAs train fast, not run fast. The FAST paper reports that π0-FAST needs about 750 ms to produce a one-second chunk on an RTX 4090, because it decodes 30 to 60 tokens through the full 2B language backbone. The flow-matching π0 produces a chunk within about 100 ms. The slow inference was acceptable on static tasks and not on dynamic ones. So choose FAST when training compute is the bottleneck, and pair it with a faster decoder when the task moves.

Generating chunks: diffusion and flow matching

Diffusion Policy (2023) treated action generation like image generation. Start from random noise in the shape of an action chunk, and remove the noise step by step, conditioned on the observation. Because the model learns a whole distribution, it can represent the left-or-right ambiguity from the control section. Across 12 tasks from four benchmarks, it improved on the previous best methods by 46.9% on average. Octo, RDT-1B and CogACT all use diffusion heads.

π0 (October 2024) uses flow matching, a close relative of diffusion with straighter paths. Let A be the true chunk and ε a sample of Gaussian noise of the same shape. Pick a time τ between 0 and 1 and mix them:

Aτ = τ A + (1 − τ) ε,    L(θ) = ‖ vθ(Aτ, o) − (A − ε) ‖2the network learns the velocity that points from noise toward the data

Why this shape? Along the straight line from ε to A, the derivative of Aτ with respect to τ is exactly A − ε. So the network is trained to predict that direction from any point on the line. At inference, π0 starts from pure noise at τ = 0 and takes ten Euler steps of size 0.1:

Aτ + 0.1 = Aτ + 0.1 · vθ(Aτ, o)ten steps from τ = 0 (noise) to τ = 1 (an action chunk)

π0 predicts chunks of H = 50 actions. During training it samples τ from a shifted beta distribution that puts more weight on noisy inputs. The authors argue that predicting actions from heavy noise is the hard part, because the observation strongly constrains the final answer. The action expert

π0 also changed where the action computation happens. It starts from PaliGemma, a 3B VLM. It adds an action expert: a second set of transformer weights, about 300M parameters, initialised from scratch. Image and text tokens go through the VLM weights. Robot state and noisy action tokens go through the expert weights. The two share self-attention at every layer, so action tokens can attend to the image and text. The design follows Transfusion, and the authors describe it as a mixture of experts with two elements.

A block-wise causal mask organises the sequence into three blocks: images and text, then robot state, then the noisy actions. Earlier blocks cannot attend to later ones. This lets the model cache the image and text computation once per observation and rerun only the small expert for each of the ten flow steps. The full model has 3.3B parameters.

Plain regression, revisited

If multimodality is the reason to avoid regression, does regression ever win? OpenVLA-OFT (2025) studied how to fine-tune OpenVLA for a specific robot. Its recipe combined parallel decoding of the whole chunk in one pass, action chunking, continuous outputs and a simple L1 loss. On the LIBERO simulation benchmark, average success went from 76.5% to 97.1%, and action throughput rose 26 times. On a real bimanual ALOHA robot it also beat π0 and RDT-1B fine-tuned with their default recipes.

The likely reason is that fine-tuning data for one robot and one task are much less multimodal than a pretraining mix. The trade-offs are summarised below.

So when an autoregressive VLA will not learn a fast task, look at what each output token carries before scaling the model.

Fast and slow: splitting the policy in two

The action representation decides how much work one chunk costs. The next question is where that work runs, and how often. A large VLM is too slow for a 50 Hz loop. A small controller is fast but knows little. Most recent systems split the policy into two parts that run at different rates.

The latency budget

π0's paper gives a useful breakdown on one RTX 4090. Encoding the images takes 14 ms. The forward pass over the observation takes 32 ms. Ten flow steps of the action expert take 27 ms. That is 73 ms on the robot, or 86 ms with the network hop to an off-board GPU. For its 50 Hz robots, π0 runs inference every 0.5 s and executes 25 actions from each chunk.

Compare that with RT-2's 55B model at 1 to 3 Hz in the cloud. The difference comes from three choices together: a 3B backbone instead of 55B, a small expert for the repeated computation, and chunks long enough to cover the inference time.

Dual-system architectures

Several groups borrowed Kahneman's language of a slow, deliberate "System 2" and a fast, automatic "System 1". The slow part is a VLM that understands the scene and the instruction. The fast part is a small policy that turns that understanding into motor commands at a high rate, using fresh observations.

  • Helix (Figure AI, February 2025). System 2 is a 7B open-weight VLM running at 7 to 9 Hz. System 1 is an 80M-parameter transformer running at 200 Hz. System 2 compresses what matters into one continuous latent vector, which System 1 reads alongside its own visual features. The two are trained end to end, on about 500 hours of teleoperation, to control 35 degrees of freedom.
  • GR00T N1 (NVIDIA, March 2025). System 2 is the Eagle-2 VLM (a SigLIP-2 encoder and a SmolLM2 language model) running at 10 Hz on an L40 GPU. System 1 is a diffusion transformer trained with flow matching that cross-attends to the VLM's features and produces actions at 120 Hz. The released model has 2.2B parameters, 1.34B of them in the VLM. Sampling a chunk of 16 actions takes 63.9 ms.
  • Gemini Robotics (Google DeepMind, March 2025). The backbone is a distilled version of Gemini Robotics-ER hosted in the cloud, with latency cut from seconds to under 160 ms. A local action decoder on the robot compensates for that delay. End to end, from raw observation to action chunk, takes about 250 ms. With multiple actions per chunk the effective control rate is 50 Hz.

A different split keeps both levels inside language. Hi Robot (2025) puts a high-level VLM on top of a π0 low-level policy. The high level reads complex prompts and spoken feedback, such as "that's not trash", and emits simple commands like "pick up the cup". π0.5 uses one model for both: it first predicts the next subtask as text, then predicts the actions for that subtask. RT-H inserts "language motions" such as "move arm forward" between the task and the action. Gemini Robotics 1.5 lets an embodied reasoning model orchestrate a VLA that itself "thinks before acting" in natural language.

Nuance

"Dual system" names several different designs. Helix and GR00T N1 train both parts end to end, so gradients from the fast policy reach the VLM through a latent vector or middle-layer features. Hi Robot passes plain language between levels. Gemini Robotics splits mainly for latency, between a cloud backbone and an on-robot decoder. The interface decides what you can do later: a language interface can be read, corrected by a person and supervised with web data, while a latent interface carries more detail but cannot be inspected. Choose the interface for the kind of correction you expect to need.

What the VLM brings, and what it does not

We now have the pieces: a pretrained VLM, an action output and a way to run it fast enough. It is time to ask the second half of the core question directly. What does the VLM actually contribute to a robot policy?

What it brings

  • Semantic generalisation. RT-2 handled new objects and instructions that need reasoning, such as "move X near the sum of two plus one" or a command given in Spanish. OpenVLA and π0 both report better language following than policies trained without a VLM.
  • Knowledge of unseen object categories. In π0.5's ablations, removing web data made the robot significantly worse at following commands about out-of-distribution objects. The authors attribute this to the broad object knowledge in web data.
  • Spatial and embodied reasoning. Gemini Robotics-ER can point at objects, predict boxes and trajectories, and estimate grasps. Post 09, When Language Must Point, explains how pointing and boxes are trained. Gemini Robotics introduced ERQA, a question-answering benchmark for this kind of embodied reasoning.
  • Task decomposition. A VLM can turn "clean the kitchen" into subtasks. Hi Robot, π0.5 and Gemini Robotics 1.5 all exploit this.

What it does not bring

  • New motions. As RT-2's authors put it, physical skills stay within the distribution of skills in the robot data.
  • Proprioception, force and timing. Web data has no joint angles, no contact forces and no notion of 20 ms. All of this is learned from robot data.
  • Fine spatial precision for free. OpenVLA had to fine-tune its vision encoder. Many systems add wrist cameras for the last centimetres of a grasp.
  • Speed. A bigger VLM is slower. Every architecture in the previous section exists to work around that.

Which layer to read from

A surprising detail concerns where the action module reads the VLM. GR00T N1's authors found that features from a middle layer of the language model gave both faster inference and higher policy success than final-layer features. They use layer 12. SmolVLA makes the same choice for speed and quality: its action expert sees features only up to half the layers of its VLM, and the remaining layers are skipped.

Insight

The last layer of a VLM is shaped by next-token prediction over text. Control seems to want earlier, more visual features. GR00T N1 reports higher success and faster inference from layer 12 than from the final layer, and SmolVLA drops the upper half of its VLM. So treat the read-out layer as a hyperparameter, and remember that truncating the VLM can buy speed and accuracy together. Post 18, What Happens Inside a VLM, looks at why visual information is concentrated in middle layers.

Pretrained knowledge can be damaged

The VLM's knowledge is not guaranteed to survive robot training. Physical Intelligence studied this in a 2025 paper on knowledge insulation. Their observation was blunt. Adding a randomly initialised action expert and training everything together harmed the model's ability to follow language. Their example: told to put a spoon in the bin, a model grasped a piece of trash instead. They attribute this to gradient interference: large, noisy gradients from the new expert flow into the pretrained backbone. A small companion lab reproduced the damage to a pretrained skill but not the loss of instruction following: in its toy, joint training raised the backbone's pointing error from 0.014 to 0.80, while the four variants could not be ranked on unseen instructions.

The fix has three parts. The backbone is trained on discrete FAST action tokens, which fit its next-token objective, and on web data. The action expert is trained with flow matching. A stop-gradient keeps the expert's gradients out of the backbone. Information still flows forward from the VLM to the expert through attention. The authors report that this trains faster, runs fast, and keeps language following. Mixing Data Without Losing Capabilities covers gradient interference in general.

Nuance

Adding an action expert makes a VLA faster and more precise, and can also make it understand language worse. The knowledge-insulation paper shows that the damage comes from training the new, randomly initialised weights jointly with the pretrained backbone. So "initialised from a VLM" does not mean "keeps the VLM's knowledge". Check language following after robot training, not only task success.

Reasoning before acting

If the VLM can reason, should the policy reason before it acts? Several papers say yes, with grounded reasoning rather than free text. Embodied chain-of-thought (ECoT, 2024) trained OpenVLA to write a plan, the current subtask, the intended motion, object bounding boxes and the gripper position before each action. The training text was generated automatically for existing robot datasets. ECoT raised OpenVLA's absolute success on hard generalisation tasks by 28%, with no extra robot data. The cost is slower inference, since the model writes many tokens per step.

MolmoAct (2025) turns reasoning into geometry. It first predicts depth-aware perception tokens, then a trajectory trace drawn in the image, then the actions. A person can edit the trace to steer the robot. Gemini Robotics 1.5 interleaves natural-language thoughts with actions and reports that this helps on multi-step tasks. Post 13, Thinking With Images, covers multimodal reasoning in general.

Where robot data comes from

Every section so far ends in the same place: motor skill comes from robot data. And robot data is scarce. Text models train on trillions of tokens. The largest open robot collection, Open X-Embodiment, has about a million trajectories. Each was produced by a person operating a physical machine in real time.

Teleoperation and its cousins

Most VLA data is collected by teleoperation. In ALOHA, an operator moves two small "leader" arms, and two "follower" arms copy them. The whole bimanual system cost under 20,000 US dollars. Mobile ALOHA adds a wheeled base for whole-body tasks. The Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) removes the robot entirely: people collect demonstrations with handheld grippers carrying cameras, and the policy is later deployed on real robots.

The scale of the largest collections shows how expensive this is. RT-1's 130,000 episodes took 17 months with 13 robots. DROID (2024) has 76,000 trajectories, about 350 hours, across 564 scenes and 84 tasks, collected by 50 people on three continents over 12 months. π0 was trained on about 10,000 hours of Physical Intelligence's own data plus Open X-Embodiment. Gemini Robotics reports thousands of hours collected on a fleet of ALOHA 2 robots over 12 months.

Cheaper sources

  • Simulation. Physics simulators produce unlimited, labelled trajectories. MimicGen generated more than 50,000 demonstrations across 18 tasks from about 200 human ones, by adapting them to new scenes. RoboCasa provides thousands of 3D assets for simulated kitchens. The cost is the sim-to-real gap in visuals and physics.
  • Human video. The web has enormous amounts of people using their hands. But video has no robot action labels. Latent action methods learn a small discrete code that explains the change between two frames, then train the VLA to predict those codes. LAPA (2024) pretrains this way and then maps latent actions to real actions with a small amount of robot data. EgoMimic co-trains on egocentric human video with 3D hand tracking and on robot data.
  • Generated trajectories. GR00T N1 used video generation models to create "neural trajectories" of its robot doing new things. This expanded its in-house teleoperation data from 88 hours to 827 hours of training trajectories. Latent actions labelled these clips.
  • Web vision-language data. Captioning, question answering and object localisation keep the VLM's knowledge alive during robot training, as in RT-2's co-fine-tuning.

Diversity beats volume

How should a fixed collection budget be spent? A 2024 study on data scaling laws for imitation learning collected more than 40,000 demonstrations and ran more than 15,000 real-robot rollouts. Generalisation followed a rough power law in the number of training environments and objects. The number of demonstrations per environment saturated quickly. With four people collecting for one afternoon in many places, their policies reached about 90% success on two tasks in new environments with new objects.

π0.5 found the same pattern at a larger scale. Its mobile-manipulation data covers about 400 hours in about 100 homes. When the authors varied the number of training locations while holding the number of training samples fixed, performance kept rising with locations. With 104 locations, the model performed about as well as a baseline that had been trained in the test homes themselves.

Insight

For generalising to new places, the number of distinct environments matters far more than the number of demonstrations. The scaling-law study found that demonstrations per environment saturate quickly, and π0.5 matched an in-home baseline with data from 104 other locations, at a fixed sample budget. So spend a data budget on new places and objects before spending it on more repetitions in the same lab.

Most of the data is not about the task

π0.5 also shows how lopsided a good mixture can be. In its first training phase, 97.6% of training examples did not come from mobile manipulators doing household tasks. They came from other robots, from web data and from high-level subtask annotations. Yet the model ran on mobile manipulators in new homes.

Its ablations separate the sources. Removing data from other robots, either from other environments or from lab tasks, significantly hurt performance. Removing web data did not significantly change success on the full household tasks. It did significantly hurt language following with unseen object categories.

Nuance

Whether web data "matters" depends on the metric. In π0.5, dropping web data made no statistically significant difference to end-to-end household success, but caused a significant drop in following commands about unseen object categories. A benchmark of familiar objects would conclude web data is useless. So evaluate each data source on the capability it is supposed to provide.

Labelling quality instead of filtering it

Demonstrations vary in quality. A natural response is to filter out the bad ones. π0 instead reweights: each task-robot combination gets a weight proportional to n0.43, where n is its number of samples, so very common tasks are down-weighted. π0.7 (April 2026) goes further and keeps imperfect data. It adds episode metadata to the prompt, such as the overall speed and a quality score from 1 to 5. Failures and autonomous rollouts can then be used as training data, because the model learns what a low-quality episode looks like. At test time, the prompt asks for high quality. This is the robot version of conditioning on a desired outcome.

The training recipe, stage by stage

The data sources above are used in stages. The stages mirror those of language models, which post 06, One Model, Many Visual Skills, describes for VLMs.

  1. VLM pretraining. The vision encoder and language model learn from images and text. Post 04, Teaching Language Models to See, covers this stage. Robot work usually starts from a released VLM: PaliGemma for π0, Prismatic for OpenVLA, Eagle-2 for GR00T N1, SmolVLM-2 for SmolVLA, Gemma 3 for π0.7.
  2. Robot pretraining. The model learns actions from a large, diverse, multi-robot mixture, usually co-trained with web data.
  3. Post-training. The model is specialised on curated, high-quality data for one robot or one task. π0's authors compare this to LLM post-training: pretraining gives breadth and recovery behaviour, and post-training gives fluent execution.
  4. Reinforcement learning. Optionally, the policy improves from its own experience.

What to freeze

Each stage must decide which weights to train. The published choices differ widely.

  • Train everything, co-train with web data: RT-2.
  • Train everything including the vision encoder: OpenVLA, which found this crucial.
  • Freeze the language model, train the rest: GR00T N1, in both pretraining and post-training.
  • Freeze both encoders: RDT-1B keeps SigLIP and the T5-XXL text encoder frozen and trains a 1.2B diffusion transformer.
  • Train the backbone and the expert with different losses, separated by a stop-gradient: knowledge insulation.
  • Low-rank adapters: OpenVLA's fine-tuning on consumer GPUs.

Reinforcement learning for VLAs

Imitation has a ceiling. A cloned policy is at best as good as its demonstrators, and it inherits the compounding errors of the control section. Reinforcement learning optimises task success directly, from the policy's own rollouts. Post 12, Rewarding What the Model Sees, covers RL for VLMs. Reinforcement Learning from First Principles derives policy gradients and PPO.

Robots make RL harder in specific ways. Real rollouts are slow and can break things. Someone must reset the scene. Success must be detected, by a person or a model. And the RL algorithm must work with the policy class: as the PA-RL paper points out, the reparameterisation trick of SAC suits Gaussian policies but is unstable for diffusion policies and intractable for autoregressive token policies.

The published approaches fall into a few groups.

  • Actor-critic for any policy class. PA-RL samples several actions from the base policy, re-ranks them with a learned critic and refines the best, then trains the policy on these improved actions with its ordinary supervised loss. It fine-tuned OpenVLA on a real robot from 40% to 70% success in 40 minutes.
  • Alternating RL and supervised learning. iRe-VLA found direct online RL on a large VLA unstable and alternates RL phases with supervised phases on successful data.
  • On-policy RL in simulation. A 2025 study, "What Can RL Bring to VLA Generalization?", found that PPO improved semantic and execution generalisation over supervised fine-tuning while keeping visual robustness similar. It found PPO more effective than the LLM-derived methods DPO and GRPO. SimpleVLA-RL scaled outcome-reward RL for OpenVLA-OFT in simulation, reached the state of the art on LIBERO, and observed the policy discovering new behaviour patterns, which its authors call "pushcut".
  • Human-in-the-loop real-world RL. HIL-SERL, a non-VLA system, learned precise dexterous tasks to near-perfect success within 1 to 2.5 hours of real training, using demonstrations and human corrections. ConRFT applied a similar offline-then-online scheme to a VLA and reported 96.3% average success on eight real tasks after 45 to 90 minutes of online fine-tuning.
  • Advantage-conditioned policies at scale. Physical Intelligence's RECAP method (π*0.6, November 2025) trains a value function and conditions the policy on whether an action was better or worse than expected. It mixes demonstrations, on-policy rollouts and expert interventions. The resulting model folds laundry in real homes, assembles boxes and makes espresso. On some of the hardest tasks, RECAP more than doubled throughput and roughly halved the failure rate.

Nuance

RL methods that work for reasoning LLMs do not transfer to VLAs unchanged. The RL-for-VLA generalisation study found PPO more effective than GRPO and DPO. GRPO compares a group of rollouts from the same starting point. That is cheap for a math problem and possible in simulation, which is where SimpleVLA-RL uses it. On a real robot every rollout costs minutes and a reset. So real-world VLA RL has gravitated to critics, advantage conditioning and human interventions rather than group-relative sampling.

Learning from video: world models and video pretraining

A VLM learns from images and text. It learns what things are. It does not learn how things move when pushed. Video does contain that information. So a second line of work pretrains on video and asks the model to predict the future.

Video as a plan

UniPi (2023) cast decision making as text-conditioned video generation. A model generates a video of the task being done, and an inverse dynamics model extracts the actions that would produce it. SuSIE used an image-editing diffusion model to produce a subgoal image, then a low-level policy to reach it. π0.7 revives the idea: a lightweight world model generates subgoal images at runtime, and the VLA is conditioned on them.

Learned simulators

UniSim (2023) learned a generative simulator of real-world interaction from carefully combined datasets. Policies trained purely inside it transferred to the real world zero-shot. Genie (2024) learned a controllable environment from unlabelled game videos, inferring a discrete latent action space without any action labels. NVIDIA's Cosmos platform provides pretrained world foundation models for physical AI, and its Cosmos-Reason1 model targets physical common sense and embodied reasoning.

Video pretraining for policies

GR-1 (2023) is a GPT-style model that predicts both robot actions and future images. After pretraining on a large video dataset, it raised success on the CALVIN benchmark from 88.9% to 94.9%, and in zero-shot unseen scenes from 53.3% to 85.4%. GR-2 (2024) pretrained on 38 million video clips, more than 50 billion tokens, and reported 97.7% average success over more than 100 tasks.

V-JEPA 2 (2025) takes a non-generative route. It learns to predict video in an abstract representation space from more than a million hours of internet video. A small action-conditioned version, V-JEPA 2-AC, is then post-trained on less than 62 hours of unlabelled robot video from DROID. It was deployed zero-shot on Franka arms in two labs, picking and placing objects by planning toward image goals.

World action models

The most recent step merges the world model and the policy. DreamZero (February 2026) starts from a 14B pretrained image-to-video diffusion model and adds state and action encoders and decoders. It jointly denoises future video and future actions. Its authors call it a world action model. They report more than twice the generalisation of state-of-the-art VLAs to new tasks and environments in real-robot tests. With system optimisations, the 14B model runs closed-loop at 7 Hz. Video-only demonstrations from other robots or from humans, 10 to 20 minutes of them, gave a relative improvement of more than 42% on unseen tasks.

Insight

VLAs and world action models fail in opposite places. RT-2's authors found that web pretraining brought semantic generalisation but no new motions. DreamZero's authors open by saying that VLAs excel at semantic generalisation but struggle with unseen physical motions, and they get motion generalisation from video. The two pretraining sources teach different things. So diagnose the failure first: if the robot misunderstands what to do, a stronger VLM helps; if it cannot do how, video pretraining is the better lever.

The cost is compute. A 14B video diffusion model at 7 Hz needs heavy engineering, and generated futures can contain physically wrong details. Post 19, Models That See and Draw, covers the generative side of these models.

Evaluating a robot policy

How do you know a VLA is better? For a GUI agent, the environment can check the final state automatically. For a robot, someone usually has to watch each trial, judge success and reset the scene. Trials are slow, so papers run few of them. That creates a statistics problem.

How many trials are enough

A trial is a coin flip with an unknown success rate p. After n trials, the standard error of the measured rate is the square root of p(1 − p)/n. A 95% confidence interval is about 1.96 standard errors on each side:

p̂ ± 1.96 √( p̂(1 − p̂) / n )normal approximation; for small n the Wilson interval used in Figure 9 is more accurate

With 20 trials at a true rate of 50%, the interval is about ±22 percentage points. That is wider than the gap between many published methods.

To detect a difference between two policies, the required number of trials per policy is roughly:

n ≈ (zα/2 + zβ)2 · [ pA(1 − pA) + pB(1 − pB) ] / (pA − pB)2zα/2 = 1.96 for 95% confidence; zβ = 0.84 for 80% power

For 50% against 60%, this gives about 385 trials per policy. Few real-robot papers run that many per comparison. Figure 9 lets you try other numbers.

Insight

With 20 real trials at 50% success, a 95% interval spans about ±22 points, and telling 50% from 60% reliably needs about 385 trials per policy. Many real-robot comparisons use tens of trials. So treat small differences in real-robot tables as unresolved unless the paper reports intervals or many trials, and prefer pairwise or partial-credit protocols that extract more information per trial.

Simulation benchmarks

Simulation makes trials cheap and repeatable. LIBERO provides procedurally generated household tasks; VLA papers usually report four suites of ten tasks that test spatial layouts, objects, goals and long tasks. CALVIN tests long-horizon language-conditioned manipulation: the policy must complete chains of five instructions, and the metric is the average number completed. SIMPLER builds simulated versions of two common real setups, the Google robot and the WidowX used for BridgeData. It reduces the control and visual gaps without building full digital twins, and shows strong correlation between simulated and real performance rankings.

Nuance

LIBERO is close to saturated. OpenVLA-OFT reports a 97.1% average over the four standard suites, and later methods report similar or higher numbers. At that level, differences reflect fine-tuning details more than general capability. So a LIBERO gain says little about generalisation; look for results on unseen scenes, objects and instructions, or on real robots.

Real-world evaluation at scale

Two recent systems make real evaluation more scalable. AutoEval runs policies around the clock on WidowX arms, with automatic scene resets and learned success detection. Its results closely match hand-run evaluations. RoboArena crowd-sources evaluation across a network of labs using the DROID platform. Evaluators choose their own tasks and scenes but must run double-blind comparisons between pairs of policies. Rankings come from the aggregated preferences. With more than 600 pairwise episodes over seven policies at seven institutions, it ranked policies more accurately than conventional centralised evaluation.

Papers also differ in what they count. Some report binary success. Others, including π0.5 and Gemini Robotics, report progress scores that give partial credit for completed subtasks. Progress scores extract more signal per trial but are not comparable across papers. Most strong results also test along named generalisation axes: unseen objects, backgrounds, environments, instructions and embodiments. The Agent Evaluation Playbook and post 07, Measuring What a VLM Can Do, discuss the same trade-offs for software agents and VLMs.

Safety when the output moves

A VLM that hallucinates writes a wrong sentence. A VLA that hallucinates moves a real arm. Safety for robots therefore has more layers than safety for chat models. Post 08, Aligning Vision-Language Models, covers the model-level side.

  • Physical safety. Classic robotics safety is about collisions, forces and speeds near people. In practice these limits are enforced by controllers and hardware below the learned policy, not by the VLA itself.
  • Semantic safety. A robot must also understand that some instructions are harmful in context. Google DeepMind's ASIMOV benchmark tests this, with scenarios generated from real scenes and hospital injury reports. One example asks whether a robot chef should pour boiling liquid into the trash. The same work generates robot constitutions, sets of natural-language rules that steer behaviour, from real-world data.
  • Adversarial attacks. RoboPAIR jailbroke LLM-controlled robots in three settings, from a white-box self-driving model to a black-box robot dog with a commercial LLM planner, and elicited harmful physical actions. BadRobot showed similar attacks on embodied LLM agents. Text in the scene can also act as an injected instruction, as with GUI agents.
  • Irreversibility. A GUI agent can often undo. A robot cannot un-break a glass. Human oversight, with an operator ready to intervene, remains standard during deployment. As the recipe section showed, those interventions also become training data.

Insight

Chat-model guardrails do not carry over to robots by default. RoboPAIR found jailbreaks quickly in all three of its settings, including a commercial robot using a production LLM. Once a model's output is a physical action, a jailbreak becomes a physical event. So place independent safety layers below the model, such as force limits, workspace bounds and emergency stops, and do not rely on the VLA to refuse.

Open problems

  • Data. Robot data is still orders of magnitude smaller than web data. Latent actions, generated video and human video are promising but unproven at the largest scales.
  • New motions. VLMs bring semantics, not motor skill. World action models such as DreamZero suggest that video can supply motion generalisation, at a high compute cost.
  • Touch and force. Almost all VLAs see and read but do not feel. Contact-rich tasks need tactile and force sensing that no web corpus contains.
  • Speed versus knowledge. Larger backbones know more and run slower. Middle-layer read-outs, dual systems and real-time chunking help, but the tension remains.
  • Memory and long tasks. Cleaning a kitchen takes minutes. Policies need memory of what they have done. π0.7 builds on a separate memory system for this, and post 11, Seeing in Time, covers long video context.
  • Real-world RL. RL works in simulation and in short real sessions with human help. Doing it safely and autonomously at fleet scale is unsolved.
  • Evaluation. Comparisons rest on few trials and lab-specific setups. Shared, statistically sound real-world evaluation is still early.
  • Safety. Semantic safety benchmarks test answers, not actions. Testing what a robot actually does under adversarial or ambiguous instructions remains open.

You should now be able to read a VLA paper and ask the right questions. What does the policy observe? How are actions represented, and what does one chunk cost? How fast does the loop close, and what happens between calls? Which knowledge comes from the VLM, which from robot data and which from video? How many trials support the claim? The next post, The Cost of a Picture, picks up one thread from here: how to make the visual part of a VLM cheaper, which matters doubly when it must run at control rates.

Key insights and nuances

  • Errors compound per decision. The imitation bound grows with the number of decisions, so chunking helps; ACT's success rose from 1% to 44% with long chunks. The control problem
  • Pretraining is the first lever. RT-2's 5B model scored 9% from scratch and 42% from a pretrained VLM on the same robot data. RT-2
  • The VLM brings semantics, robot data brings motion. RT-2 learned no new motions; RT-2-X gained skills from other robots but not visual generalisation. One policy for many robots
  • VLM habits do not always transfer. OpenVLA needed a trainable vision encoder and 27 epochs, the opposite of common VLM practice. One policy for many robots
  • The tokenizer, not the model, limited fast tasks. Per-step bins carry almost no information per token at high frequency; FAST fixed training. How to represent an action
  • FAST trains fast but runs slowly. About 750 ms per one-second chunk against about 100 ms for flow matching. How to represent an action
  • "Dual system" is several designs. Latent, feature and language interfaces trade bandwidth against inspectability. Fast and slow systems
  • Middle layers serve control better. GR00T N1 reads layer 12; SmolVLA drops the upper half of its VLM. What the VLM brings
  • New action experts can damage the VLM. Knowledge insulation keeps their gradients out of the backbone. What the VLM brings
  • Diversity beats volume. Environments and objects matter more than repeated demonstrations; π0.5 matched an in-home baseline from 104 other locations. Where robot data comes from
  • Whether web data matters depends on the metric. In π0.5 it mattered for unseen objects, not for overall task success. Where robot data comes from
  • LLM RL recipes need adapting. PPO beat GRPO and DPO in a VLA study; real-world RL leans on critics and interventions. The training recipe
  • Video teaches what VLMs miss. World action models target motion generalisation, the gap VLAs leave. Learning from video
  • Small trial counts hide differences. Twenty trials give about ±22 points; LIBERO is near saturation. Evaluating a robot policy
  • Jailbreaks become physical. Safety must sit in layers below the model. Safety when the output moves
