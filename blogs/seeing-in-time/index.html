What changes when the picture moves

Earlier posts in this series turned one image into a few hundred visual tokens and handed them to a language model. A video looks like the same problem repeated. It is a stack of images, one after another. So why not encode every frame and stack the tokens?

Start with a concrete clip. It is one minute long. A ball sits on a table next to a cup. The ball starts to roll, reaches the edge and drops to the floor. Later a person walks in, picks up the cup, drinks, and puts it down. Now ask five questions about it.

  1. What colour is the cup? Any single frame answers this.
  2. Which way did the ball roll? One frame cannot answer this. You need at least two frames from the roll, in the right order.
  3. Did the ball fall before or after the person picked up the cup? You need frames from both events and you need to know which came first.
  4. How long did the person hold the cup? You need to know when the hold started and when it ended, in seconds.
  5. When exactly did the ball hit the floor? The drop lasts about a second. If no sampled frame falls inside that second, the event does not exist for the model.

The first question is an image question. The other four are about time. They ask about order, motion, duration and a specific moment. A model that sees a bag of unordered frames can answer the first question and will guess at the rest. Figure 1 walks through the clip and marks which frames each question needs.

This post follows those four temporal demands through the whole stack. The thread is simple. Time costs tokens, and the token budget is fixed. Every design choice in a video model is a way of spending that budget: on more frames, on more detail per frame, on telling the model when each frame happened, or on finding the right moment before looking closely.

The post assumes the image pipeline from Inside a Modern Vision Encoder and the architectures from The VLM Architecture Gallery. Positional encoding is derived in How Models Know Where They Are. Here we only add what time changes.

The token arithmetic of video

Begin with the cost of one frame. A vision transformer cuts an image of height H and width W into square patches of side p. Each patch becomes one token. Most modern VLMs then merge each 2×2 block of neighbouring tokens into one, so a merge factor r = 2 divides the count by four. The tokens per frame are:

τ = (H / p) · (W / p) / r2 For a 448×448 frame with 14-pixel patches and a 2×2 merge: 32 · 32 / 4 = 256 tokens. This is exactly what InternVL 2.5 uses per video frame.

A video adds two numbers: its duration D in seconds and the sampling rate f in frames per second. The visual tokens for the whole video are then:

N = D · f · τ One hour at 2 frames per second with 256 tokens per frame: 3,600 · 2 · 256 = 1,843,200 tokens.

That number is the core difficulty. A one-hour video at a modest frame rate is almost two million tokens. That is larger than the context window of nearly every open model. Even where it fits, attention inside the language model compares every token with every other token. Its cost grows with N2. Doubling the duration doubles the feed-forward cost but quadruples the attention cost. At these lengths attention dominates.

Proprietary systems hit the same wall. Google reports that Gemini 1.5 samples video at one frame per second for its long-video tests. Gemini 2.5 was trained to work with 66 visual tokens per frame instead of 258. The report says this lets about three hours of video fit in a one-million-token context instead of about one hour. The arithmetic checks out: 3,600 × 258 is about 0.93 million, and 10,800 × 66 is about 0.71 million.

What time adds beyond more pixels

If video were only "more images", the problem would be pure compression. It is not. Time adds four kinds of information that no single frame contains.

  • Order. Which event came first. A model must know the sequence position of each frame.
  • Motion. Direction and speed. These live in the difference between nearby frames, so they need frames close together in time.
  • Duration. How long something lasted. This needs a mapping from frames to real seconds, not just frame indices.
  • Causality. Why something happened. The cause and the effect may be far apart, so the model must keep both in view.

Each one pulls the budget in a different direction. Motion wants dense frames over a short window. Causality wants sparse frames over a long window. Duration and moments want a clock. The rest of this post is about how models trade these against each other.

Insight

The visual token count of a video is the product of three knobs: duration, frame rate and tokens per frame. Gemini 2.5's move from 258 to 66 tokens per frame bought three times the duration at the same context size. So when a model claims a longer maximum video, first check which knob moved. It is often detail per frame, not a better memory.

Choosing which frames to look at

The first lossy step happens before any network runs. A 30-frames-per-second video has 1,800 frames per minute. No model encodes them all. Something decides which frames to keep. There are four common rules.

Uniform sampling

Pick a fixed number of frames, say 8 or 32, spaced evenly over the video. This was the default in the first generation of video LLMs. Video-LLaVA, for example, samples 8 frames from every video. Uniform sampling keeps the token count constant, which makes training simple. Its weakness is that the time between frames depends on the video's length. Eight frames from a 10-second clip are 1.25 seconds apart. Eight frames from a one-hour video are 7.5 minutes apart.

Fixed frame rate

Pick a rate, such as one or two frames per second. Qwen2-VL samples at 2 frames per second. VideoLLaMA 3 and LLaMA-VID sample at 1 frame per second. The gap between frames is now the same for every video, so "two frames apart" always means the same amount of real time. The cost is that the token count grows with the length of the video. Every fixed-rate model therefore has a cap. VideoLLaMA 3 caps at 180 frames. Qwen2-VL and Qwen2.5-VL evaluate with at most 768 frames. Past the cap, a fixed-rate model has to spread its frames more thinly, which is uniform sampling again. Apollo notes that fps-based methods commonly do exactly this once a video exceeds their frame capacity, usually around 64 frames.

Keyframes and scene changes

Videos are redundant. A static shot of a lecturer produces hundreds of nearly identical frames. Content-aware sampling keeps a frame only when it differs from the last kept frame. LongVU does this with DINOv2 features: frames whose features are too similar to their neighbours are dropped. VideoLLaMA 3 applies the same idea at the patch level with its Differential Frame Pruner, which removes patches whose pixels barely changed since the previous frame.

Query-aware selection

The most targeted rule looks at the question first. If the question is about the cup, keep the frames that show the cup. Adaptive Keyframe Sampling (AKS) scores each frame by its relevance to the prompt and also rewards coverage of the whole video, so the selection does not collapse into one scene. Frame-Voyager learns to choose frame combinations, using a video LLM's loss on each candidate combination as the training signal. The cost of query-aware selection is that it must run again for every new question. Apollo's authors note this explicitly and leave such methods out of their study for that reason.

Figure 3 lets you try the first two rules on the one-minute clip. The events have very different lengths. The roll lasts twelve seconds. The drop lasts one and a half. The cup is put down in one second.

Two failure modes appear quickly. With 8 uniform frames, the frames are about 7.5 seconds apart. The one-second events are usually missed. With a fixed rate high enough to catch them, the same rule applied to an hour of video produces hundreds of thousands of tokens. No setting is right for every question. Short events need density. Long stories need coverage.

Insight

Uniform sampling quietly changes the playback speed of every training example. Apollo is a large controlled study of video LMM design. It points out that N uniform frames from a short clip look like slow motion. The same N frames from a long video look like fast forward. In their experiments, training with frame-rate sampling consistently beat uniform sampling, and the gap came from training, not from the test-time frame count. In practice: train at a fixed frame rate, and when a video is too long, space out short clips rather than stretching the frame spacing.

Apollo's fix is worth spelling out. It samples short clips at a constant rate, and when the video is too long it spaces the clips out instead of the frames. Inside each clip, the time between frames never changes. The model can still learn what normal speed looks like. Apollo also varied tokens per frame and found a trade-off between frame rate and tokens per second, with 8 to 32 tokens per frame performing best at its scale. Its appendix adds a useful split. OCR and spatial questions lost accuracy sharply when tokens per frame dropped. Egocentric and reasoning questions cared more about frame rate. The right budget depends on what will be asked.

Nuance

"More frames help" is true only up to a point, and the point moves. LongVA's Video-MME score rose from 47.9 at 8 frames to 52.6 at 128 frames, then fell to 51.8 at 384. LLaVA-Video, trained on data selected for dynamic content, kept improving up to its 110-frame limit, and its authors argue that even 100 frames are not enough for a dynamic 30-second clip. InternVL 2.5 reports that many models peak at 16 or 32 frames and degrade beyond. The benefit of extra frames depends on whether the training data and the benchmark actually contain information between the frames you already have. Always sweep the frame count for your own task.

Encoding frames, clips and motion

Once frames are chosen, something must turn them into tokens. There are two families.

Image encoders, one frame at a time

The simplest option reuses the model's image encoder. Each frame passes through it independently. Frame 7 never sees frame 8 inside the encoder. Any temporal reasoning must happen later, in the connector or the language model. This is what LLaVA-OneVision, InternVL 2.5 and most current VLMs do. It has three practical advantages. Image and video share one encoder. Huge image datasets keep training that encoder. And image skills transfer to video for free. LLaVA-OneVision showed strong video understanding emerging from image training, which its authors call task transfer.

Video encoders, many frames at once

The alternative sees several frames together. Two ideas from the video-classification literature matter here.

The first is the tubelet. ViViT extended the image patch into a small cube that spans t frames as well as p×p pixels. One tubelet becomes one token. A tubelet of depth 2 halves the token count compared with per-frame patches, and the embedding can see motion inside the cube. The second idea is factorised attention. TimeSformer found that applying temporal attention and spatial attention separately within each block, which it calls divided attention, worked best among the schemes it compared. Both ideas avoid full attention over every patch of every frame.

Video encoders are usually pretrained without text. VideoMAE masks 90 to 95 percent of space-time tubes and reconstructs them. Video is so redundant that such extreme masking still works. V-JEPA predicts the features of masked regions instead of pixels. InternVideo2 combines masked reconstruction, contrastive alignment with text, audio and speech, and next-token prediction, and scales the encoder to 6 billion parameters.

Qwen2-VL sits between the two families. Its vision transformer is an image encoder, but its patch embedding is a 3D convolution with a depth of two frames. Two consecutive frames become one set of tokens. Images are fed as two identical frames so that one encoder serves both. This halves the video token count before anything else happens.

Insight

Dedicated video encoders lose to a good image encoder on most video questions. Apollo trained otherwise identical models with seven encoders. SigLIP, an image encoder, was the best single choice. Video encoders won only on temporal perception, such as speed and direction. Combining SigLIP with InternVideo2 beat both, by about 7 percent on their benchmark. So if motion matters to your task, add a video encoder beside the image encoder rather than replacing it.

Telling the model when

Suppose the frames are encoded. They enter the language model as one long sequence of tokens. How does the model know that a token came from second 20 rather than second 40? There are four answers, and the field has moved between them.

Order only

The plainest option adds nothing. Frame tokens are concatenated in order, and the language model's ordinary 1D position encoding counts upward through them. The model knows that frame 5 came after frame 4. It does not know how many seconds apart they were. Under uniform sampling that gap changes from video to video. Most early video LLMs worked this way.

Text tags between frames

The second option writes the time in words. InternVL 2.5 wraps each frame in image tags with a label such as "Frame-1". VideoLLaMA 3 writes "Time: xxs" before each frame. Apollo writes "clip from MM:SS-MM:SS" before each clip. Apollo tested this directly. Any separator between frames, learned or textual, improved its benchmark by 2 to 3 points. Timestamps did as well as anything else and needed no new embeddings.

Time inside the position encoding

The third option puts time into the rotary position embedding. Rotary embedding rotates each query and key by an angle proportional to its position, so attention scores depend on the distance between positions. Qwen2-VL's Multimodal RoPE (M-RoPE) splits the embedding into three parts: temporal, height and width. Each token gets three position numbers instead of one.

  • For a text token, all three numbers are equal, so M-RoPE reduces to ordinary 1D RoPE.
  • For an image token, the temporal number is constant and the height and width numbers follow the token's place in the grid.
  • For a video token, the temporal number goes up by one for each frame, and height and width work as for images.

This gives the model a real 3D sense of where each token sits. But "goes up by one per frame" is still a frame index. A model trained at 2 frames per second and tested at 1 frame per second sees the same position gaps for twice the real time. Qwen2.5-VL fixed this by aligning the temporal number to absolute time. With dynamic frame-rate sampling, the gap between temporal numbers now reflects the real seconds between frames. The report states the goal plainly: let the model learn the pace of events and localise moments at the level of seconds.

Back to text, for a reason

The fourth answer is the newest, and it is a return. Qwen3-VL replaced absolute-time M-RoPE with explicit text timestamps, such as <3.0 seconds>, placed before each group of frames. It trains with both seconds and hours:minutes:seconds formats. The report gives two reasons. Tying position numbers to absolute time makes them very large and sparse for long videos, which hurt long-range understanding. And learning time-aligned positions required training data spread evenly across many frame rates, which is expensive to build. Text timestamps cost a few extra tokens but avoid both problems.

Qwen3-VL also changed how M-RoPE allocates frequencies. The original split gave each of the temporal, height and width parts its own contiguous block of rotary frequencies. The report says this produced an imbalanced frequency spectrum that hurt long-video benchmarks. The fix interleaves the three axes across the embedding dimensions, so each axis gets both low and high frequencies. InternVL3 attacked a related problem from another side. Its Variable Visual Position Encoding (V2PE) advances the position index by 1 for each text token but only by a fraction δ < 1 for each visual token. Long visual inputs then use up less of the position range.

Nuance

The Qwen line went from order only (early models) to absolute time inside RoPE (Qwen2.5-VL) and then back to text timestamps (Qwen3-VL). Apollo's ablation and VideoLLaMA 3's design reach the same place from independent directions. The lesson is not that position-encoding approaches failed. Absolute time in RoPE worked for second-level grounding. It became costly at hour scale and in data construction. If you build a video model today, text timestamps are the cheaper default, and the extra tokens are a small fraction of the visual tokens.

Fitting a video into the budget

Sampling and tubelets reduce the token count before the language model. Most video models compress further. Compression is a broad topic covered for images in The Cost of a Picture. Video adds one new resource to compress: redundancy across frames. The methods fall into six groups.

Pooling

The cheapest compressor is an average. Video-ChatGPT averages each frame's CLIP features over space to get one token per frame. It also averages over time to get one token per spatial position. Both sets go to the language model. LLaVA-OneVision shrinks each frame's feature map with bilinear interpolation to 196 tokens and takes up to 32 frames. PLLaVA found something surprising about pooling. Fine-tuning an image model on many frames without pooling made performance saturate or drop. The authors traced this to a few very high-norm visual features that dominate. Pooling along time smoothed them out and fixed it.

Learned queries

A resampler replaces pooling with attention. A small set of learned query vectors attends to the frame features and returns a fixed number of tokens. Video-LLaMA uses a BLIP-2-style Q-Former per frame, adds temporal position embeddings, then runs a second "video Q-Former" across frames. LLaMA-VID goes to the extreme: two tokens per frame. One is a context token, produced by attending to the frame with the user's question. The other is a content token that pools the frame itself. At 1 frame per second that is 7,200 tokens for an hour. Apollo compared connectors directly. The Perceiver Resampler beat pooling-based connectors overall and on four of its benchmark's five categories.

Merging across frames

Consecutive frames repeat each other. Merging exploits that. Token Merging (ToMe) combines similar tokens inside a vision transformer and noticed that it merges object parts across frames of video. Chat-UniVi clusters tokens into dynamic visual tokens shared across space and time. MA-LMM keeps a memory bank of past frame features. When the bank is full, it finds the two most similar adjacent frames and averages them. The bank stays a constant size however long the video runs. VideoLLaMA 3's Differential Frame Pruner removes patches that barely changed. LongVU combines three steps: drop near-duplicate frames using DINOv2 similarity, keep full detail only on frames relevant to the question, and reduce spatial tokens in frames that depend on earlier ones.

Two speeds at once

The SlowFast idea comes from video recognition. SlowFast networks run a slow pathway at a low frame rate with rich features to capture appearance, and a lightweight fast pathway at a high frame rate to capture motion. Video LLMs borrowed it for token allocation. SlowFast-LLaVA, a training-free method, samples 50 frames from a video. Ten of them form the slow pathway at 12×24 tokens each. All 50 form the fast pathway at 4×4 tokens each. The total is 10 × 288 + 50 × 16 = 3,680 tokens. LLaVA-Video uses a variant that splits frames into interleaved slow and fast groups with different pooling strides, so no frame is encoded twice. The authors report that this lets them fit up to three times more frames than equal allocation.

Hierarchical compression

VideoChat-Flash compresses in two stages. It first compresses each clip with a video encoder, to an average of about 16 tokens per frame. It then drops more tokens inside the language model when they are irrelevant to the task. The authors report an overall ratio of about 1/50 with almost no loss, and 99.1 percent accuracy on a needle-in-a-haystack test over 10,000 frames. Video-XL compresses into the key-value cache instead. It inserts a special summary token for each interval of video and keeps only the summary tokens' cached keys and values.

Memory

The last group gives up on seeing everything at once. MovieChat keeps a short-term buffer of recent frames and consolidates it into a long-term memory by merging similar adjacent tokens. It reports handling more than 10,000 frames on a 24 GB graphics card. MA-LMM processes frames online and stores them in its memory bank. Memory is the natural design for streaming video, where the end of the video does not exist yet.

Insight

Equal tokens for every frame is rarely the best use of a budget. SlowFast-LLaVA, with no video training at all, matched or beat fine-tuned video models on some benchmarks. It did so by splitting 3,680 tokens into a few detailed frames and many coarse ones. Apollo's per-category results explain why this works: reading text needs tokens per frame, while following events needs frames per second. A split serves both. So when tuning a video model, treat the allocation across frames as a design variable, not a constant.

How video models developed

With the building blocks in place, the history of video LLMs reads as a sequence of answers to one question: where should the tokens go? Per-family detail lives in How the Open VLM Families Were Built. Here we follow the ideas.

2023: bolt a video front-end onto a chat model

The first wave appeared within weeks of each other in 2023. VideoChat connected video foundation models to a language model through a learnable interface. Video-LLaMA used a video Q-Former and added an audio branch built on ImageBind. Video-ChatGPT pooled CLIP features over space and time and released 100,000 video-instruction pairs. Video-LLaVA aligned image and video features into one space with LanguageBind encoders before projection and trained on both. These models saw 8 to a few dozen frames, mostly through heavy compression. LLaMA-VID pushed to hour-long input with only two tokens per frame. MovieChat did it with a memory that merges similar frames. Both paid in detail. VideoChat2, released with the MVBench benchmark, showed that progressive training on diverse instruction data helped a great deal on temporal tasks.

2024: images first, then long context

The second wave learned that strong image models make strong video models. LLaVA-OneVision trained one model on single images, multiple images and video with a similar maximum token count for each, and reported that video skills transfer from images. LongVA asked a different question: what if the language model can simply read more? It extended Qwen2-7B's context to 224K tokens by continued training on text, then aligned images only. The resulting model handled 2,000 frames, more than 200,000 visual tokens, without long-video training. The authors call this long context transfer. LongVILA built a multi-modal sequence-parallel training system to train on long videos directly, extending VILA from 8 to 2,048 frames.

Data caught up in the same year. LLaVA-Video built LLaVA-Video-178K, a synthetic instruction set of 178,510 videos with 1.3 million samples, captioned densely at one frame per second. Qwen2-VL brought native resolution, 3D patches and M-RoPE to video and claimed understanding of videos over 20 minutes. Apollo ran the controlled design study cited throughout this post. Its "scaling consistency" result is practical: design decisions tested on models of about 2 to 4 billion parameters transferred to larger ones with R2 above 0.9.

2025 onward: time as a first-class input

The third wave treats time explicitly. Qwen2.5-VL aligned M-RoPE to absolute time and evaluated on hour-long benchmarks. VideoLLaMA 3 kept image training central, pruned redundant patches, and wrote timestamps as text. InternVL3 added V2PE for longer contexts. Qwen3-VL moved to text timestamps and a native 256K-token window. On a video needle-in-a-haystack test it reported 100 percent accuracy up to 30 minutes, and 99.5 percent at about two hours with positional extension. SlowFast-LLaVA-1.5 showed that small models of 1 to 3 billion parameters can be strong on long video with token-efficient allocation. Reinforcement learning arrived too. Video-R1 adapted GRPO into T-GRPO, which we return to in the evaluation section and which Rewarding What the Model Sees covers in depth.

Nuance

The same model can have different scores in different papers. Apollo-7B reports 63.3 on Video-MME in its own paper. VideoLLaMA 3's comparison table lists it at 61.3. InternVL3 evaluates Video-MME at 16, 32, 48 and 64 frames and reports the best of the four. Qwen2.5-VL allows up to 768 frames. These are all reasonable choices, but they are different experiments. Before comparing two video models, check the frame count, the frames per second and whether subtitles were used.

Hour-long video

An hour of video breaks every short-video habit. At 1 frame per second it is 3,600 frames. A typical question concerns perhaps ten seconds of it. Three routes have emerged.

Route one: read everything

Extend the context and feed the frames in. LongVA, LongVILA, Gemini and Qwen3-VL take this route. It is conceptually clean. Nothing is thrown away by a heuristic. The costs are memory and latency, and the result depends on whether the model actually uses distant tokens.

Route two: compress or remember

Squeeze each frame hard or keep a running memory. LLaMA-VID, MovieChat, MA-LMM, VideoChat-Flash and Video-XL take this route. It scales to any length. The risk is that the compressor decides what is important before the question is known.

Route three: search, then look

Treat the video like a database. Find the relevant moments first, then look closely. VideoAgent uses a language model as an agent that iteratively decides which frames to inspect, with vision-language models as tools. It reported 54.1 percent on EgoSchema and 71.3 percent on NExT-QA while looking at only about 8 frames on average. VideoTree builds a tree over the video and expands only the branches relevant to the query. Video-RAG retrieves auxiliary text, such as speech transcripts, on-screen text and object detections, and passes it with the frames. These are the video versions of ideas covered in Thinking With Images and Searching With Pixels.

Nuance

A perfect needle-in-a-haystack score is a test of finding one frame, which is a single-frame skill. Gemini 1.5 Pro retrieved a hidden word from a single frame across 10.5 hours of video. Yet on HourVideo, which asks for summarisation, tracking and reasoning over 20 to 120 minutes of egocentric footage, Gemini 1.5 Pro scored 37.3 percent while human experts scored 85.0 percent. Retrieval over a long context and understanding of a long video are different abilities. Use needle tests to check that the context works, and use reasoning benchmarks to check that the model can use it.

Finding the moment: temporal grounding

Question five asked when the ball hit the floor. Answering with a time is a different output from answering with a word. The task is called temporal grounding, or moment retrieval. Given a video and a sentence, return the start and end of the segment the sentence describes. It is the time-axis version of the spatial grounding in When Language Must Point, where a model returns a box instead of a time span.

Benchmarks and the metric

Charades-STA added sentence-level time annotations to the Charades dataset of indoor activities. The original paper reports 13,898 clip-sentence pairs for training and 4,233 for testing. QVHighlights has over 10,000 YouTube videos, each with a free-form query, the relevant moments, and a five-point saliency score for every relevant clip. ActivityNet Captions provides 20,000 videos with 100,000 descriptions, each with its own start and end time, and is used for dense captioning and grounding.

The metric is the temporal intersection over union. Let the true segment be [sg, eg] and the prediction be [sp, ep]. The overlap is the length of their intersection. The union is the length they cover together.

tIoU = max(0, min(ep, eg) − max(sp, sg)) / (max(ep, eg) − min(sp, sg)) The denominator is the union when the segments overlap. Papers report R@1 at IoU 0.5 or 0.7, the share of queries whose top prediction clears the threshold, and mIoU, the mean tIoU.

There is a direct link between grounding and frame sampling. A model can only place a boundary where it has a frame. If frames are Δ seconds apart and the model picks the nearest frame, each boundary can be off by up to Δ/2. In the worst case both boundaries move inward, and an event of length L keeps a tIoU of only (L − Δ) / L. For a 4-second event sampled every 2 seconds, that floor is 0.5. At 2 frames per second it is 0.875. Figure 9 lets you try this.

Insight

Grounding accuracy is capped by frame spacing before the model does anything. Qwen3-VL's evaluation reflects this: it samples Charades-STA at 4 frames per second, against 2 frames per second for all its other video benchmarks. In practice, if you need second-level timestamps, sample densely around candidate moments, even if you sample the rest of the video sparsely.

How to output a time

A language model emits tokens, so a timestamp must become tokens somehow. Four representations are in use.

  • Plain numbers in seconds. The model writes "13.4 to 16.6". VideoLLaMA 3 prompts for start and end times in seconds and parses the numbers out of the answer. Qwen2.5-VL trains on grounding data written both in seconds and in hour-minute-second-frame format, so it can read and write either. It needs no new vocabulary. It works best when the input also carries real time, as with absolute-time positions or text timestamps.
  • Dedicated time tokens. VTG-LLM adds absolute-time tokens to the vocabulary. Grounded-VideoLLM adds discrete temporal tokens that carry time knowledge. These avoid splitting a number across several sub-word tokens.
  • Separate heads. TRACE treats timestamps, saliency scores and captions as distinct tasks, each with its own encoder and decoding head, interleaved as a sequence of events.
  • Time-aware input. TimeChat binds each frame's timestamp to its visual features inside the frame encoder, and uses a sliding Q-Former so longer videos produce more tokens.

Training data for grounding is mostly converted from existing annotations. TimeChat built a 125,000-instance instruction set across six time-sensitive tasks. VTG-LLM re-annotated public grounding datasets into VTG-IT-120K. Momentor generated Moment-10M with an automatic engine. Qwen2.5-VL reports a Charades-STA mIoU of 50.9 for its 72B model. The same report lists GPT-4o at 35.7, which shows how much explicit time helps on this task.

Video data and its problems

Every capability above depends on data with the right properties. Video data has three recurring problems: weak captions, static content and sparse annotation.

Web-scale video-text pairs

HowTo100M collected 136 million clips from 1.22 million narrated instructional videos. Its text is speech, transcribed automatically. People do not always say what they are doing at the moment they do it, and later work describes such data as noisy. WebVid-2M, released with the Frozen in Time model, scraped over two million videos with their short stock-footage captions. InternVid scaled up with language models: over 7 million videos, about 760,000 hours, 234 million clips and 4.1 billion words of generated descriptions. Panda-70M took 3.8 million high-resolution videos and split them into semantically consistent clips. Several cross-modal teacher models captioned each clip. A retrieval model, fine-tuned on human choices, then picked the best caption. The result is 70 million captioned clips.

Dense synthetic captions

Short captions say what is in a clip. They rarely say what changes. ShareGPT4Video found that giving GPT-4V many frames at once, or a grid of frames, produced captions that were less detailed and sometimes confused about time. It designed a differential captioning strategy that describes changes between frames, built 40,000 dense captions, and trained a captioner that labelled 4.8 million more videos. LLaVA-Video-178K went further. It selected dynamic, untrimmed videos and sampled them at one frame per second. It captioned them recurrently at three levels of time, from about ten seconds up to the whole video. Earlier descriptions served as context for later ones.

Egocentric and long-form sources

Ego4D provides 3,670 hours of first-person daily-life video from 931 camera wearers. It underlies EgoSchema and HourVideo. Long, continuous, unscripted footage is exactly where a model must remember and reason across minutes, so egocentric data matters for long-video skills. LLaVA-Video's 7B model was weaker on EgoSchema, and its authors attribute this to a smaller share of egocentric data in training.

Mixtures

Video models are trained on mixtures of text, images, multiple images and video. VideoLLaMA 3 is explicitly image-centric: it builds large, clean image-text data and adds video late. Apollo found that keeping about 10 to 14 percent text in the mixture was important, and among mixtures with that much text, video-heavy ones performed best. Video-R1 added image reasoning data to its video reinforcement-learning set because high-quality video reasoning data was scarce. The broader science of mixing is in Mixing Data Without Losing Capabilities.

Insight

More video captions can be worse than fewer, better ones. In LLaVA-Video's ablation, LLaVA-Hound had more captions than LLaVA-Video-178K, yet trained a weaker model. The authors name two causes: its source, WebVid, is relatively static, and it was annotated from 10 frames per video. Static clips teach a model that one frame is enough. So filter video data for motion and caption it densely, or the model will learn the single-frame shortcut that the next section describes.

Evaluation and the single-frame shortcut

Benchmarks are covered in general in Measuring What a VLM Can Do. Video adds one specific danger. A video question may be answerable without the video. It may be answerable from the text alone, from one frame, or from frames in any order. Then a high score says nothing about time.

The evidence

The warning came early. In 2022, Lei and colleagues showed that a model trained on single frames, with a frame ensemble at inference, could beat existing multi-frame methods on popular video-and-language tasks. They called this a strong static appearance bias in the datasets. The same year, Buch and colleagues proposed the atemporal probe, which selects frames without using their order. It reached strong or state-of-the-art results on standard video question answering, which showed that event order was often unnecessary.

Apollo repeated the test on modern benchmarks with ten open models. It ran each benchmark three ways: with the video, with only the centre frame, and with only the question text. A large part of many benchmarks was answerable from text alone or from the centre frame. NExT-QA and Perception Test were largely solved by one frame. For Video-MME, the reliance on the video decreased as videos got longer. Apollo then built ApolloBench by removing questions that more than half the models answered correctly from text or one image. It was 41 times faster to run and more dependent on perception.

Benchmarks built against the shortcut

Several benchmarks were designed with the shortcut in mind. MVBench converts static image tasks into 20 dynamic tasks that its authors state cannot be solved effectively with a single frame. TempCompass builds conflicting videos that share static content but differ in one temporal aspect: it plays videos in reverse for direction and attribute change, and concatenates clips for speed and event order. EgoSchema introduced the temporal certificate, the length of video a human must watch to verify an answer. Its three-minute clips have certificates far longer than earlier datasets. Video-MME filtered out questions that Gemini 1.5 Pro could answer from the text alone; that model scored below 15 percent in the text-only setting.

The results on these benchmarks are sobering. In TempCompass's multiple-choice action questions, an image-only model, SPHINX-v2, scored 89.9. The best video model scored 88.5. On direction, every model scored between about 24 and 37, against a random baseline of 27.8 and a human score of 96.7. Vinoground found that GPT-4o scored about 50 percent on short counterfactual pairs where humans scored about 90, and open models were mostly near chance. TemporalBench reported GPT-4o at 38.5 percent on fine-grained temporal questions and found that multiple-choice options themselves leaked a cue, so it proposed a multiple binary accuracy instead.

Insight

On TempCompass, an image model with no notion of time matched the best video models on action recognition, while every model sat near chance on direction, speed and event order. A video benchmark score is therefore a blend of image skill and temporal skill. To measure the temporal part, run three controls on any video benchmark you care about: text only, one frame, and shuffled frames. The gap between the full score and the best control is the part that needs video.

Training against the shortcut

If a model can get reward without using time, it will. Video-R1 found exactly this when it applied GRPO to video reasoning: models took shortcuts through a single frame. Its fix, T-GRPO, generates answers twice per question, once from ordered frames and once from shuffled frames. The model earns the positive reward only when its accuracy on ordered frames beats its accuracy on shuffled frames. The reward itself now requires temporal reasoning. The general design of such rewards is the subject of Rewarding What the Model Sees.

Nuance

Longer is not harder in the temporal sense. Apollo found that the share of Video-MME that needs the video shrinks for the long subset, and EgoSchema's authors showed that a clip's length is only weakly correlated with its temporal certificate. A one-hour video with one question about one moment is a retrieval problem. So report long-video results together with a short, temporally adversarial benchmark such as TempCompass or Vinoground.

Streaming, real time and sound

Everything so far assumed a finished video file. A live camera is different. The end does not exist yet. Questions arrive at any time. Sometimes the model should speak before anyone asks.

VideoLLM-online introduced a training objective for this, called Streaming EOS prediction. At each new frame, the model learns whether to respond or stay silent. Silent frames add little to the context. The authors report streaming dialogue at over 10 frames per second on an A100 for a five-minute clip. Flash-VStream keeps a memory that is updated as frames arrive, so it can answer asynchronous questions with low latency and bounded GPU memory. Dispider separates perception, decision and reaction into different modules. A lightweight module keeps watching the stream and decides when to interact, while a separate module writes the answer, so watching does not stop while the model talks. StreamingBench tests this setting with 900 videos and 4,500 questions asked at different points in the stream. At release, Gemini 1.5 Pro and GPT-4o were both well below human level.

Sound is the other half of most videos. Video-LLaMA added an audio branch built on ImageBind with its own Q-Former. VideoLLaMA 2 integrated an audio branch through joint training. Qwen2.5-Omni interleaves audio and video in time order and introduces TMRoPE, a time-aligned multimodal RoPE, so that a sound and the frame it accompanies share a temporal position. The value of audio shows up even in a vision benchmark. On Video-MME, adding subtitles raised Gemini 1.5 Pro's accuracy by 6.2 points and adding audio by 4.3 points, with larger gains for longer videos.

Nuance

Video-MME scores are often quoted as two numbers, without and with subtitles. The difference can be six points or more. For long videos, much of the story is in the speech. A model with a strong language backbone and subtitles can look like a strong video model. When you compare long-video models, compare the without-subtitles numbers, and treat audio and subtitles as a separate capability.

What you can now reason about

Return to the five questions about the ball and the cup. You can now say what each one demands of a model. The colour of the cup needs one good frame. The direction of the roll needs two frames close together inside the roll, and enough per-frame detail to see the ball move. The order of events needs frames from both events and a sequence position for each. The duration of the hold needs real time, either written as text or built into positions. The moment of the drop needs a frame inside a one-second window, which means dense sampling where it matters.

You can also read a video model's design as a budget. Given a frame rate, a tokens-per-frame setting and a context window, you can compute how much video fits. Given a compression method, you can say what it gives up: detail, exact timing or old memories. Given a benchmark score, you can ask how much of it a single frame would have earned.

Two threads continue in the next posts. The single-frame shortcut is a reward-hacking problem as much as an evaluation problem, and T-GRPO's shuffled-frame comparison is one instance of a broader idea: design the reward so that it can only be earned by looking. That is the subject of Rewarding What the Model Sees. The search route for long video, where a model decides which moments to look at, is a form of active perception that Thinking With Images develops for images.
