The machine you actually have

Every distributed training system in existence is an answer to a very small question: what do you do when the thing you want to compute does not fit inside the thing you have? Before we answer it, we should be precise about what we have. Not "a GPU" in the abstract, but a specific device with a specific budget, because every parallelism strategy in this article is a trade you make against that budget.

Take one NVIDIA H100 SXM. It gives you 80 GB of HBM3 at roughly 3.35 TB/s, and about 990 TFLOP/s of dense bf16 matrix throughput with FP32 accumulation. Those two numbers — a capacity and a rate — are the entire story. Capacity decides whether your step is even representable. Rate decides how long it takes. Distributed training is the art of spending network bandwidth to relax one or both.

So: where does the 80 GB actually go? People usually answer "the model", which is wrong by roughly an order of magnitude. Let us count properly, because the counting is the argument.

The memory ledger of a single step

Let Ψ be the number of parameters. Modern training runs in mixed precision: the tensors that flow through matmuls are bf16, but the optimizer keeps a higher-precision copy of the weights so that tiny updates do not vanish. With Adam, the ledger is:

parameters (bf16) 2Ψ bytes gradients (bf16) 2Ψ bytes optimizer: master weights (fp32) 4Ψ momentum m (fp32) 4Ψ variance v (fp32) 4Ψ = 12Ψ bytes ────────────────────────────────────────── persistent state 16Ψ bytes The 16Ψ figure is the one worth memorising. It is why a 7-billion-parameter model needs ~112 GB of persistent state before a single activation is stored, and therefore why a 7B model does not fit on an 80 GB card under plain Adam — even though the weights themselves are only 14 GB.

Why fp32 master weights? Because bf16 has 8 bits of mantissa. If a weight is around 1.0 and the update is around 1e-4, then adding them in bf16 rounds the update away entirely: the weight is unchanged and the run silently stops learning in its smallest, most numerous coordinates. The fp32 copy exists so the accumulation survives. This is not precision paranoia; it is the difference between a run that converges and one that plateaus.

Then there are activations — every intermediate tensor the backward pass will need. Unlike the 16Ψ term, activation memory scales with batch size and sequence length, not just with the model. For a transformer with L layers, hidden size h, batch b, sequence s, storing everything needed for backward is roughly

A ≈ L · b · s · h · (c₁ + c₂ · s/h) The first term collects the per-token tensors: layernorm inputs, QKV projections, MLP intermediates, residuals. The second term is the attention score matrix, which is b·s·s per head — quadratic in sequence length. With FlashAttention the score matrix is never materialised, so c₂ collapses to nearly zero and the quadratic term stops dominating. That single change is why 128k-token contexts became trainable at all.

Figure 01 makes the ledger concrete. Drag the sliders and watch the bar overflow the 80 GB line. There is no distributed training in this figure — it is just arithmetic — but it is the arithmetic that everything else exists to fix.

Often said"A 7B model needs 14 GB, so it fits comfortably on any modern GPU."

ActuallyThat is the inference number. Training needs the gradient, three optimizer tensors and every activation the backward pass will read. Persistent state alone is 8× the bf16 weights. Inference memory and training memory are different quantities and it is worth never conflating them.

The second budget: time

Memory decides feasibility; FLOPs decide patience. The standard estimate for transformer training cost is C ≈ 6 · Ψ · D FLOPs, where D is the number of training tokens. The 6 comes from counting matmuls: 2 FLOPs per multiply-accumulate, once for the forward pass, twice for the backward pass (one matmul for the input gradient, one for the weight gradient). Three passes × 2 = 6.

T = 6 · Ψ · D / (N · P · u) N devices, P peak FLOP/s each, u the fraction you actually achieve (model FLOPs utilisation, typically 0.35–0.55 on well-tuned large runs). For Ψ = 7e9 and D = 2e12 tokens on one H100 at u = 0.45: T ≈ 6·7e9·2e12 / (990e12 · 0.45) ≈ 1.9e8 seconds ≈ 6 years.

Six years is the whole argument for data parallelism. And 112 GB > 80 GB is the whole argument for model parallelism. Every technique below is a way of dividing one of these two numbers by N without paying too much at the wire.

Hold on to this

Memory is a constraint (binary: fits or does not). Time is a cost (continuous: always improvable). Parallelism strategies that attack memory buy you the possibility of a run. Strategies that attack time buy you its completion. Most real configurations need both, and the interesting engineering is in the interaction.

Seven primitives and one ring

Before splitting anything, we need to be able to talk about moving data between processes. The vocabulary here is older than deep learning by decades — it comes from MPI, standardised in 1994 — and every line of NCCL, every torch.distributed call, and every parallelism strategy below is expressed in it.

The setup: N processes, called ranks, numbered 0…N−1. Each owns some local buffer. A collective is an operation every rank calls together, which rearranges or combines those buffers. The rules are strict: all ranks must call the same collectives in the same order, or you deadlock. That constraint is why distributed training code looks so rigid.

The atlas

  • Point-to-point (send / recv). One rank hands a buffer to one other rank. The only truly asymmetric primitive; everything else is symmetric.
  • Broadcast. One root's buffer is copied to every rank. Used to synchronise initial weights so all replicas start identical.
  • Scatter. The root's buffer is cut into N pieces and piece i goes to rank i. Used to hand out shards.
  • Gather. The inverse: every rank's buffer is collected at one root. Used for logging and checkpointing.
  • AllGather. Gather, but every rank ends up with the concatenation. This is how FSDP reassembles a full weight tensor from shards.
  • Reduce. Combine all buffers elementwise with an associative operator (sum, max, …), result at one root.
  • ReduceScatter. Combine elementwise, then give rank i only chunk i of the result. This is how FSDP produces sharded gradients.
  • AllReduce. Combine elementwise, result everywhere. The workhorse of data parallelism. Crucially: AllReduce = ReduceScatter + AllGather, and that identity is the key to its cost.
  • All-to-All. A full transpose: rank i sends chunk j to rank j, for every pair. The most expensive shape, and the one MoE routing needs.
  • Barrier. Nobody proceeds until everybody arrives. Carries no data.

Figure 03 animates every one of them on the same set of ranks so you can see the shapes rather than memorise the names. Switch primitives and step through — the movement is the definition.

Often said"You need a barrier before a collective to make sure everyone is ready."

ActuallyCollectives on a communicator are already ordered and self-synchronising — a rank cannot complete an AllReduce until every peer has contributed. Barriers add latency without adding correctness. They are useful for timing measurements and for fencing off non-NCCL work, and almost nowhere else in a training loop.

Why AllReduce costs what it costs

The naïve implementation of AllReduce is: everyone sends their buffer to rank 0, rank 0 sums, rank 0 broadcasts. That moves (N−1)·S bytes into a single link in each direction, so the time grows linearly with N and the root's NIC is the bottleneck. Nobody does this.

The ring algorithm does better by never giving any link more than its share. Arrange the ranks in a logical circle. Split each rank's buffer into N chunks. Then:

phase 1 — reduce-scatter: N−1 steps step k: rank i sends chunk (i−k) to rank i+1 rank i receives chunk (i−k−1) and adds it in after N−1 steps: rank i holds the fully reduced chunk i phase 2 — all-gather: N−1 steps step k: rank i forwards the finished chunk it received after N−1 steps: every rank holds every finished chunk bytes leaving each rank = 2(N−1) · S/N Every step moves exactly S/N bytes per link, and there are 2(N−1) steps. As N grows the factor 2(N−1)/N approaches 2, so per-rank traffic saturates at twice the buffer size. This is the single most important fact about data-parallel training: the volume each GPU must move does not grow with cluster size.

What does grow is the number of steps, and each step pays a fixed latency α. The standard α–β cost model gives:

T_ring = 2(N−1)·α + 2(N−1)/N · S/B T_tree = 2·log₂(N)·α + 2·log₂(N)/… · S/B (latency-optimal) α is per-message latency, B is per-link bandwidth. Ring is bandwidth-optimal but its latency term is linear in N. Tree/recursive-halving algorithms are latency-optimal but move more bytes. NCCL picks between them by message size: small buffers go tree, large buffers go ring. This is why fusing many small gradient tensors into large buckets matters so much — it moves you out of the α-dominated regime.

Often said"AllReduce gets more expensive as you add nodes."

ActuallyPer-rank bytes converge to 2S and stop growing. What grows is the latency term and the probability that one straggler holds up everyone. On a 1024-GPU ring the bandwidth cost is essentially the same as on 8 GPUs; the pain is 2046 sequential hops of α and the fact that the slowest rank sets the pace for all of them.

Often said"AllReduce is deterministic, so distributed training is reproducible."

ActuallyFloating-point addition is not associative. A ring reduction sums in a fixed order for a fixed N, so it is reproducible for that configuration — but change the world size, change the algorithm NCCL picks, or let it pick a different ring, and you get a bit-different gradient. Runs are reproducible per-configuration, not across configurations.

Cut the graph

Before the named strategies, one frame that makes all of them the same idea.

A single-GPU training step is already a dataflow graph: tensors flow through operations, and each operation waits for its inputs. Distributed training does not invent a new algorithm. It cuts that graph, puts different pieces on different devices, and inserts communication wherever a dependency crosses the cut. Nothing else happens. Every technique in this article is a choice of where to cut.

That reframing is worth the effort because it turns a list of acronyms into four questions you can ask of any configuration:

  1. What is replicated? Parameters, optimizer state, activations, the batch, the experts — or nothing at all.
  2. What is partitioned? The batch, the parameters, a layer's inner dimensions, whole layers, sequence positions, or experts.
  3. What bytes must move? Gradients, weights, activation tensors, KV blocks, or routed tokens. The size of the message follows directly from this.
  4. When must ranks agree? Once at the end of backward, around every layer, at each pipeline boundary, or at every routing decision. The frequency follows from this, and frequency is usually what hurts.

Answer those four and you have derived the strategy's cost model without looking anything up. Figure 06 runs the same training step through six different cuts and answers all four questions for each one.

Two things become obvious once you see it this way.

First, communication is not a design choice — it is a consequence. Nobody decided that tensor parallelism should have an AllReduce. The AllReduce exists because a row-parallel layer produces partial sums that no single device can finish. If you cut there, you pay there. The engineering freedom is in where to cut, not in whether to communicate.

Second, the cuts are orthogonal. Cutting the batch does not stop you also cutting the depth. That is why real configurations multiply five strategies together, and why the device mesh in §10 has five dimensions rather than one.

T_message ≈ α + n / β The whole optimisation game, in one line. α is the fixed per-message latency, n the bytes, β the bandwidth. Cuts that produce many small messages are α-bound and want fusing; cuts that produce few large ones are β-bound and want fast links. This is the same α–β model from §2, now doing real work: it tells you which cuts a given fabric can afford.

The sentence to carry through the rest of the article

Distributed training is not "more GPUs". It is deciding where every tensor lives, when it moves, who waits for it, and whether the useful compute on either side of the cut is large enough to hide the movement.

Why distributed training exists

There are exactly two reasons, and it is worth keeping them separate because they call for different medicine.

Reason one: it does not fit. The 16Ψ ledger plus activations exceeds device memory. No amount of waiting helps. You must place different parts of the state on different devices, which means the devices must talk during every forward and backward pass. This is model parallelism in its various forms, and it is intrinsically chatty.

Reason two: it takes too long. The state fits, but 6ΨD FLOPs at one device's rate is longer than your patience or your cluster reservation. You want to process more tokens per second, so you run copies of the same computation on different data and combine the results. This is data parallelism, and it is intrinsically cheap — one synchronisation per step, on a buffer whose size does not depend on batch size.

Throughput scaling has a ceiling, and it is not the network

Speed-up is bounded by the sequential part of the work, which is Amdahl's law with a twist: in distributed training the "sequential" part grows as you add workers, because the collective's latency term does. Let t_c be per-device compute time for a fixed local batch and t_n be the exposed (non-overlapped) communication time. Then

throughput(N) = N · b_local / (t_c + t_n(N)) efficiency(N) = t_c / (t_c + t_n(N)) Weak scaling holds the local batch fixed, so t_c is constant and efficiency degrades only through t_n. Strong scaling holds the global batch fixed, so t_c shrinks as 1/N while t_n does not — which is why strong scaling collapses far earlier. Almost all large-model training is weak scaling, and almost all benchmark charts that look linear are weak-scaling charts.

But there is a second ceiling that has nothing to do with hardware. Weak scaling grows the global batch size, and past a model-dependent critical batch size the extra examples stop reducing gradient noise meaningfully. Beyond it, you spend twice the FLOPs to reach the same loss. Cluster efficiency can be 95% while training efficiency quietly halves.

Often said"We got 94% scaling efficiency at 512 GPUs, so the system is nearly perfect."

ActuallyScaling efficiency is measured against your own single-node number. If that baseline achieves 30% MFU, then 94% of it is 28% MFU, and half your cluster is idle in a way the scaling chart cannot see. Report MFU alongside scaling efficiency or the number means very little.

Data parallelism

The simplest thing that works, and still the backbone of nearly every real configuration. Four moves:

  1. Replicate. Every rank holds a complete copy of the model, identical at step zero (a Broadcast from rank 0 guarantees it).
  2. Split the batch. Global batch B becomes B/N per rank. Each rank runs a normal forward and backward on its own slice.
  3. AllReduce the gradients. Each rank has a gradient computed from different data. Averaging them gives exactly the gradient of the global batch — because gradients of a sum are the sum of gradients.
  4. Step. Every rank applies the same averaged gradient with the same optimizer state, so replicas stay bit-identical without any further communication.

Point 3 deserves its derivation, because it is what makes the whole thing exact rather than approximate:

L(θ) = (1/B) Σ_{j=1..B} ℓ(x_j; θ) = (1/N) Σ_{i=1..N} [ (1/(B/N)) Σ_{j ∈ shard i} ℓ(x_j; θ) ] = (1/N) Σ_i L_i(θ) ∇L = (1/N) Σ_i ∇L_i ← this is exactly an AllReduce with op=avg DDP is not an approximation of large-batch training. It is large-batch training, computed in pieces. The only sources of difference from a single-device run of the same global batch are floating-point summation order and any batch-coupled operation — which is why BatchNorm needs SyncBatchNorm and LayerNorm does not.

What the global batch is actually made of

Three separate knobs multiply into the number every paper reports, and conflating them causes a surprising amount of confusion:

global batch = microbatch_per_device × DP_degree × accumulation_steps The first is bounded by device memory. The second is bounded by how many GPUs you have. The third is bounded only by patience — accumulation buys effective batch size in time rather than in hardware, and with no_sync() it even skips the intermediate reductions, so it genuinely lowers communication per token. Under pipeline parallelism a fourth factor appears, because the microbatch count that keeps the bubble small also multiplies the batch.

Below that arithmetic sits a correctness question that is easy to get wrong and almost impossible to see in a loss curve: which examples each rank gets. A distributed sampler has to partition the dataset so that the union across ranks is the epoch and the intersection is empty. Get the seed handling wrong and two ranks draw the same shard — you are then training on half your data at twice the weight, and the loss curve looks completely normal. The same coordination problem applies to dropout streams, to sequence packing, and to resuming mid-epoch after a crash.

The overlap trick

A naïve implementation waits for the whole backward pass, then AllReduces one giant buffer. That serialises compute and communication, and the network sits idle for the entire backward pass before becoming the only thing running.

The key observation is that backpropagation produces gradients in reverse layer order, and the gradient for the last layer is final the moment it is computed. So you can start reducing it while the earlier layers are still computing. PyTorch DDP does this with autograd hooks: parameters are assigned to buckets (25 MB by default, in reverse registration order), and when every parameter in a bucket has its gradient, that bucket's AllReduce launches asynchronously on a separate CUDA stream.

Bucketing solves two problems at once. It overlaps communication with compute, and it fuses many tiny tensors into few large messages — which, per the α–β model, is the difference between latency-bound and bandwidth-bound. Figure 09 shows both effects on the same timeline.

Often said"DDP synchronises gradients at the end of the backward pass."

ActuallyIt synchronises throughout the backward pass. The last bucket to fire is the one holding the first layer's parameters, and only that final reduction is exposed. If you profile DDP and see one large AllReduce at the end, something has broken your bucketing — usually unused parameters, a conditional branch that skips part of the graph, or gradient checkpointing interacting badly with hook ordering.

Often said"Gradient accumulation is a free substitute for more GPUs."

ActuallyIt buys the same effective batch size, and with no_sync() it even skips the intermediate AllReduces — so it genuinely reduces communication per token. What it does not buy is time: accumulating K microbatches takes K times as long. It is data parallelism in the time dimension, and it does nothing whatsoever about the 16Ψ memory ledger.

Overlap is not free

The timeline above draws communication on its own lane, as if issuing a collective on a separate CUDA stream made it cost nothing. It does not. NCCL collectives are kernels: they occupy streaming multiprocessors, they contend for memory bandwidth, and they compete with your matmuls for the same copy engines. A reduction running "in parallel" with a GEMM makes that GEMM slower.

So the honest model has three resources, not two. A collective is only genuinely hidden when the network can absorb the bytes in the time available and the SMs it borrows are ones the compute was not going to use anyway. On a well-fed GEMM that is rarely true, which is why measured overlap efficiency is usually somewhere between 60% and 90% rather than the 100% every timeline diagram implies.

The same reasoning explains a failure mode that catches people at scale: two process groups that are each efficient in isolation can destroy each other when they share a NIC. A tensor-parallel AllReduce and a data-parallel gradient reduction issued at the same instant do not each get full bandwidth; they split it, and the one on the critical path pays. Multi-dimensional parallelism is a traffic-engineering problem as much as an algorithmic one.

Often said"Communication is fully overlapped, so it is free."

ActuallyOverlap moves the cost from the timeline into the throughput. The step gets no longer, but the compute inside it runs slower because the collective is holding SMs and memory bandwidth. If you compare MFU with and without the collective rather than comparing step times, the tax shows up immediately. Budget 5–15% for it on a busy step.

Where DDP stops

DDP's communication volume per step is 2 · 2Ψ · (N−1)/N ≈ 4Ψ bytes per rank for bf16 gradients — fixed, independent of batch size. Its compute per step is proportional to the local batch. So the ratio of communication to compute is

comm/compute ∝ Ψ / (b_local · s · Ψ) = 1 / (b_local · s) More tokens per device means better overlap. This is the reason "just increase the per-device batch size" is the first and most effective fix for a communication-bound DDP run — and the reason it stops being available exactly when memory gets tight. The two constraints are coupled, which is the recurring theme of this article.

But DDP's real wall is memory, not bandwidth. Replicating 16Ψ bytes on every rank means a 7B model is out of reach on 80 GB cards no matter how many you have. Adding GPUs does not help at all: 1024 replicas of an unfittable model are still unfittable. That is the observation ZeRO is built on.

ZeRO and FSDP

Here is the observation. In DDP, every rank stores an identical copy of the optimizer state, the gradients and the parameters. Identical. That is N−1 copies of redundancy, held for no reason other than convenience — each rank only needs the full parameter tensor at the instant it is computing with it.

ZeRO (Zero Redundancy Optimizer) removes the redundancy in three stages, each shedding one term of the ledger.

per-device bytes 7B on 8 GPUs DDP / ZeRO-0 2Ψ + 2Ψ + 12Ψ = 16Ψ 112.0 GB ZeRO-1 2Ψ + 2Ψ + 12Ψ/N = 4Ψ + 12Ψ/N 38.5 GB ZeRO-2 2Ψ + (2Ψ + 12Ψ)/N = 2Ψ + 14Ψ/N 26.3 GB ZeRO-3 (2Ψ + 2Ψ + 12Ψ)/N = 16Ψ/N 14.0 GB Stage 1 shards optimizer states — safe, because they are only touched during the update. Stage 2 additionally shards gradients — each rank keeps only the slice it will use for its slice of the update, which is exactly what ReduceScatter produces. Stage 3 shards the parameters themselves, which means a rank must fetch a layer's full weights before it can use them.

Stage 3 is what PyTorch calls FSDP, and it is worth being precise about its step, because the entire cost model follows from it. FSDP groups parameters into units (typically one transformer block each). For each unit, in order:

  1. Forward: AllGather the unit's parameter shards into a full tensor → run the layer → free the full tensor immediately.
  2. Backward: AllGather the same shards again (they were freed) → compute the gradient → ReduceScatter the gradient so each rank keeps only its slice → free.
  3. Step: each rank updates its own shard using its own optimizer slice. No communication at all.

Count the traffic. Two AllGathers of Ψ parameters and one ReduceScatter of Ψ gradients gives 3Ψ units of movement, against DDP's AllReduce at 2Ψ. FSDP is therefore 1.5× the communication volume of DDP — the exact price of the memory it saves. ZeRO-1 and ZeRO-2, notably, are free: ReduceScatter + AllGather is precisely what an AllReduce already does internally, so their volume is identical to DDP's.

Often said"FSDP shards everything, so memory scales as 1/N."

ActuallyFSDP shards persistent state. Activations are not sharded — they belong to the local microbatch and stay wherever they were produced. On long-context or large-microbatch runs, activation memory dominates and FSDP does nothing for it. That is precisely the gap context parallelism and activation checkpointing exist to fill, and it is why "I enabled FSDP and still OOM'd" is such a common report.

Often said"ZeRO-3 has the same communication as ZeRO-2 because ReduceScatter + AllGather = AllReduce."

ActuallyThat identity is true for the gradient path, which is why ZeRO-1 and ZeRO-2 are volume-neutral. Stage 3 adds a second parameter AllGather in the backward pass that neither of the others needs, because the weights were freed after the forward. That extra Ψ is the 1.5× factor. You can trade it back by keeping the forward AllGather resident (reshard_after_forward=False), spending memory to buy bandwidth.

Prefetch: making 1.5× feel like 1×

Volume is not latency. FSDP's AllGather for layer i+1 does not depend on layer i's output — only on the parameter shards, which are already resident. So it can be issued while layer i is still computing. With correct prefetching, the AllGather is fully hidden behind compute and the 1.5× volume costs you nothing in wall-clock. Without it, every layer stalls waiting for its own weights and FSDP looks catastrophically slow.

This is the single largest performance lever in FSDP, and it fails in predictable ways: too-small FSDP units (per-parameter wrapping) give messages too small to saturate the link; too-large units (whole-model wrapping) defeat sharding entirely; and a backward prefetch policy of BACKWARD_POST instead of BACKWARD_PRE issues the gather too late to hide.

Hybrid sharding: sharding is not one dimension

FSDP's cost depends on the bandwidth of the group you shard across. Inside a node, 8 GPUs are connected by NVLink/NVSwitch at ~450 GB/s per direction. Between nodes, even a good fabric like AWS EFA gives ~50 GB/s per node — roughly a 9× gap. Sharding across all 512 GPUs of a cluster puts the AllGather on the slow fabric, which is exactly where you cannot hide it.

HSDP (hybrid sharded data parallel) arranges the world as a 2-D mesh: shard within a node, replicate across nodes. Now the AllGather and ReduceScatter — the frequent, per-layer, latency-critical operations — ride NVLink, while the cross-node traffic is a single gradient AllReduce per step, which is easy to overlap. You keep 1/8 of the memory saving instead of 1/512, but a 7B model needs only 8-way sharding to fit; the other 504 GPUs are better spent on throughput.

Often said"Shard as widely as possible; more sharding is more memory saved."

ActuallyMemory saving is 1/N with sharply diminishing returns — going from 8-way to 64-way sharding takes you from 12.5% to 1.6% of the ledger, a saving of 11 percentage points, while moving every per-layer collective onto a wire that is roughly 9× slower. Shard to fit, then replicate. The corollary: if the model fits at shard degree 8, sharding at 512 is a pure loss.

Often said"Sharding by parameter count gives every rank an equal share."

ActuallyIt gives equal parameter counts, which is not equal memory or equal work. Embedding and output projection tensors are enormous and often live in units of their own; tied embeddings create sharing constraints; and a unit's activation memory has nothing to do with its parameter count. Peak memory is set by the largest unit's AllGather buffer plus its activations, not by the average.

Tensor parallelism

FSDP shards storage but not computation: every rank still runs the whole layer on its own data, it just borrows the weights first. Tensor parallelism does the opposite. It splits a single matrix multiplication across devices so that each one performs a genuinely smaller matmul, and it never needs the full weight tensor anywhere.

The construction rests on two ways of cutting a linear layer, and on the fact that they compose so cleanly you can chain them with a single collective between blocks.

Column parallel

Y = X A X: [b·s, h] A: [h, 4h] split A by columns: A = [A₁ | A₂] Y = X [A₁ | A₂] = [X A₁ | X A₂] = [Y₁ | Y₂] Rank 1 computes Y₁ = X A₁, rank 2 computes Y₂ = X A₂. Both need the full X — which they already have, because X is the block's replicated input. No communication in the forward pass at all. The output is sharded along its feature dimension.

Row parallel

Z = Y B Y: [b·s, 4h] B: [4h, h] split B by rows and Y by columns: Z = [Y₁ | Y₂] · [B₁ ; B₂] = Y₁B₁ + Y₂B₂ Each rank computes a partial sum of the full-shaped output, and the pieces must be added: one AllReduce. Crucially, row-parallel consumes a column-sharded input — exactly what the previous layer produced. So an MLP built as column-parallel → GELU → row-parallel needs no communication in the middle, only one AllReduce at the end.

The GELU in the middle is the subtle part, and it is why the ordering is not arbitrary. GELU is elementwise, so GELU([Y₁|Y₂]) = [GELU(Y₁)|GELU(Y₂)] — it commutes with the column split. If you had used row-parallel first you would need to AllReduce before the nonlinearity, doubling the collectives. Megatron's layout is chosen precisely so that the nonlinearity sits where the tensor is already correctly sharded.

Attention shards even more naturally: each head is independent, so you assign heads to ranks. QKV projections are column-parallel (producing per-rank heads), the attention itself is local to those heads, and the output projection is row-parallel — one AllReduce, same as the MLP.

per transformer layer, per step: forward: AllReduce after attention + AllReduce after MLP = 2 backward: AllReduce for each block's input gradient = 2 total: 4 AllReduces × 2(T−1)/T · (b·s·h·2 bytes) Megatron writes these as conjugate operators f and f̄: f is identity forward / AllReduce backward, f̄ is AllReduce forward / identity backward. The message size is the activation b·s·h, not the parameter count — so unlike DDP, tensor-parallel traffic grows with batch and sequence length. For a 70B-class model at b·s = 8192 and h = 8192, that is ~134 MB per collective, four times per layer, eighty layers deep, every step.

That last derivation explains the cardinal rule of tensor parallelism: it must stay inside a node. Four AllReduces per layer, each on a hundred-megabyte activation, with nothing to overlap them against — because unlike DDP gradients, these collectives sit directly in the critical path. The next layer literally cannot begin. On NVLink at 450 GB/s this is tolerable. On a 50 GB/s inter-node fabric it is a 9× longer stall, repeated hundreds of times per step.

Often said"Tensor parallelism divides activation memory by T."

ActuallyIt divides the memory of the tensors inside the parallel regions. LayerNorm inputs, dropout masks and the residual stream sit between them and are fully replicated on every TP rank. Megatron's sequence parallelism fixes this by splitting those regions along the sequence dimension and replacing each AllReduce with a ReduceScatter–AllGather pair — same total bytes, meaningfully less memory. If you use TP without SP you are paying for TP's communication while keeping much of TP's memory.

Often said"Set TP = 8 to use the whole node, always."

ActuallyTP shrinks each rank's matmuls. At TP=8 a hidden size of 8192 becomes per-rank GEMMs of width 1024, which start to under-fill the tensor cores and lose efficiency to launch overhead and reduced arithmetic intensity. TP=8 is right for very large hidden sizes; for 7B-class models TP=2 or 4 often beats it, and TP=1 with FSDP frequently beats both. Measure the GEMM, not the theory.

Pipeline parallelism

The third way to cut. Instead of splitting the batch (DP) or splitting each matrix (TP), split the layer stack: stage 0 holds layers 1–20, stage 1 holds 21–40, and so on. Activations flow forward along the chain and gradients flow backward along it, using nothing but point-to-point send/recv of one activation tensor per boundary.

That is the enormous advantage: pipeline communication is tiny and infrequent. One tensor of shape [b_micro, s, h] crosses each boundary per microbatch, versus tensor parallelism's four full AllReduces per layer. Pipeline stages are the only form of model parallelism that is genuinely comfortable across a slow network.

The equally enormous disadvantage is the bubble. Run one batch through a 4-stage pipeline and stage 3 sits idle until stage 0, 1 and 2 have finished; then stages 0–2 sit idle while the backward pass drains. The fix is microbatching: split the batch into m microbatches and keep them in flight simultaneously.

GPipe schedule: all m forwards, then all m backwards busy time = m · (t_f + t_b) bubble time = (P−1) · (t_f + t_b) bubble ratio = (P−1) / (m + P − 1) ≈ (P−1)/m for m ≫ P With P=8 stages and m=8 microbatches you waste 47% of the cluster. With m=64 you waste 10%. The rule of thumb m ≥ 4P comes straight from this expression — and it is the reason pipeline parallelism wants large global batches, which is another way of saying it competes with data parallelism for the same batch budget.

1F1B: the same bubble, a third of the memory

GPipe's schedule holds all m microbatches' activations alive on stage 0 before any backward begins. That is m× the activation memory, which usually breaks before the bubble does.

1F1B (one-forward-one-backward) reorders the same operations: after a warm-up of P−i−1 forwards, stage i alternates strictly between one forward and one backward. The bubble is identical — the same (P−1) idle slots at each end — but the number of live activations per stage drops from m to at most P. Same throughput, memory independent of microbatch count. There is almost no reason to run GPipe scheduling today.

Interleaved 1F1B goes further: give each device v non-contiguous chunks of layers instead of one contiguous block (device 0 gets layers 1–5 and 21–25). The bubble becomes (P−1)/(v·m), at the cost of v× more point-to-point messages. Zero-bubble schedules take the last step, splitting the backward into its input-gradient and weight-gradient halves — the weight gradient has no downstream dependency, so it can be deferred into the bubble.

Often said"1F1B is faster than GPipe."

ActuallyThey have exactly the same bubble ratio and exactly the same throughput at the same m. 1F1B wins because its activation memory is bounded by P instead of m, which lets you raise m — and raising m is what shrinks the bubble. It is an indirect win, through the memory budget, not a direct one.

Often said"Split the layers evenly across stages."

ActuallyBalance by time, not by layer count. The first stage carries the token embedding and the last carries the output projection and loss — for a large vocabulary the LM head plus its softmax can cost more than several transformer blocks, and it materialises a [b·s, V] logit tensor that dwarfs everything else. Standard practice is to give the first and last stages fewer transformer layers, and to compute the loss in chunks so the full logit tensor never exists. A pipeline runs at the speed of its slowest stage; one heavy stage makes every other stage's bubble worse.

Often said"More microbatches is always better, because the bubble shrinks."

ActuallySmaller microbatches mean smaller GEMMs, and below a certain size the matmuls stop saturating the GPU. You trade a bubble you can see in the schedule for an efficiency loss you can only see in MFU. There is an optimum and it is usually a good deal lower than "as many as possible".

Sequence and context parallelism

Everything so far shards parameters or batches. Long-context training breaks both, because the offending quantity is neither: it is the activation memory of a single sequence, and it grows with s.

Consider one sequence of 128k tokens. The per-layer activations scale as b·s·h, which at s=131072 and h=4096 is already ~1 GB per layer per sample in bf16 before attention. You cannot reduce the batch below one. FSDP does not shard activations. TP replicates the residual stream. Pipeline parallelism divides by depth but not by sequence. The only remaining axis is the sequence itself.

Context parallelism splits the token dimension across C devices: rank i owns tokens [i·s/C, (i+1)·s/C). Every operation that is token-local — layernorm, MLP, elementwise — becomes trivially parallel with no communication. Attention is the entire difficulty, because token i attends to every token before it, including the ones living on other devices.

Often said"Sequence parallelism and context parallelism are two names for the same thing."

ActuallyThey are related and routinely confused, but they solve different problems. Megatron's sequence parallelism is an add-on to tensor parallelism: it shards the sequence dimension of the operations between the tensor-parallel regions — layernorm, dropout, the residual stream — which TP leaves replicated, and it changes no collective count, only their shape. Context parallelism is a parallelism axis in its own right: it shards the sequence across a separate process group and needs a genuine distributed attention algorithm, because local queries must reach remote keys. SP rides on TP's degree; CP has a degree of its own. A run can have both.

Two ways to make attention work

Ring attention. Keep queries local. Rotate the keys and values around a ring: at step k, each rank has the KV block belonging to rank (i−k) mod C, computes the partial attention of its local queries against that block, and accumulates using the same online-softmax rescaling FlashAttention already uses. After C steps every query has seen every key. Communication is one KV block per step, and it can be prefetched during the previous block's compute — so with enough tokens per rank, it hides completely.

online softmax accumulation, block by block: m_new = max(m_old, rowmax(S_k)) O_new = O_old · exp(m_old − m_new) + exp(S_k − m_new) · V_k ℓ_new = ℓ_old · exp(m_old − m_new) + rowsum(exp(S_k − m_new)) This is exactly why ring attention is possible: softmax over a partitioned axis can be computed incrementally with a running max and a running denominator, so no rank ever needs the full score row. Causal masking makes it uneven — rank 0's queries need only one KV block, rank C−1's need all C — so implementations either load-balance by giving each rank two non-contiguous token chunks (one from each end of the sequence) or accept the imbalance.

Ulysses. A different decomposition. Before attention, do an All-to-All that converts a sequence-sharded, head-complete layout into a head-sharded, sequence-complete one. Now each rank owns a few complete heads over the whole sequence and runs entirely standard attention with no modification. Afterwards, a second All-to-All converts back. Four all-to-alls per layer, each moving b·s·h/C per rank.

The trade is clean. Ulysses moves less data and reuses the unmodified attention kernel, but C cannot exceed the number of key/value heads, and All-to-All is the pattern most punished by an oversubscribed fabric. Ring attention has no head limit and uses only nearest-neighbour point-to-point, but its communication scales with sequence length and its causal load balance needs care. In practice: Ulysses inside a node, ring across nodes, and often both at once.

Often said"Context parallelism is just tensor parallelism along a different axis."

ActuallyThey shard different things and interact differently with the model. TP shards weights and requires the full activation; CP shards activations and requires the full weights. TP's degree is limited by hidden size and head count and its collectives are AllReduces on the critical path; CP's degree is limited by sequence length and its collectives can often be overlapped with attention compute. They compose — a run can have TP=8 and CP=4 simultaneously — which would be impossible if they were the same thing.

Often said"Attention is O(s²), so long context is hopeless."

ActuallyAttention compute is O(s²) and always was. What FlashAttention removed was the O(s²) memory, by never materialising the score matrix. Context parallelism then divides the remaining quadratic compute across C devices, giving O(s²/C) per rank. Long context is expensive, not impossible, and the expense is now honest FLOPs rather than an allocator failure.

Expert parallelism and MoE

Every strategy so far assumes every token goes through every parameter. Mixture-of-experts breaks that assumption: replace the MLP in each block with E parallel MLPs and a small router that sends each token to the top-k of them. Total parameters multiply by roughly E; FLOPs per token multiply by k, which is typically 1 or 2. You get a much larger model for nearly the same compute.

The cost is a new communication pattern. Experts are large — you place different experts on different devices — but tokens arrive wherever the data-parallel split put them. A token on device 3 that routes to an expert on device 11 must physically travel there, be processed, and travel back. Across the whole batch, every device sends some tokens to every other device: an All-to-All. Two of them, in fact, dispatch and combine, per MoE layer, in both forward and backward.

router: g = softmax(x · W_r) W_r: [h, E] pick top-k experts per token y = Σ_{e ∈ topk} g_e · Expert_e(x) capacity per expert = capacity_factor · k · T / E T tokens, E experts, k experts per token. If routing were perfectly uniform each expert would get exactly k·T/E tokens. It is not uniform, so implementations allocate a capacity_factor (typically 1.0–1.5) of headroom and drop tokens that overflow — they skip the expert and pass through the residual. The capacity factor is a direct trade between wasted compute on under-filled experts and information lost to dropped tokens.

Routing is learned, and left alone it collapses: a few experts get slightly better early, receive more tokens, improve faster, and absorb everything. So MoE training carries an auxiliary load-balancing loss that penalises the correlation between routing probability and token fraction, pushing the distribution back toward uniform. It is a genuine multi-objective problem — too little balancing loss and experts collapse, too much and the router stops specialising.

Two refinements matter in practice. First, when a single expert is itself large, expert parallelism composes with tensor parallelism inside the expert: EP decides which rank group a token is routed to, and expert-TP splits that expert's MLP across the ranks of the group. Second, dropping tokens is increasingly out of favour. Dropless implementations keep every token by using grouped or ragged matmul kernels that tolerate uneven expert loads, which removes the information loss but hands the imbalance straight to the critical path — the layer still finishes when the busiest expert does. You are choosing which failure you prefer, not eliminating it.

Often said"MoE gives you 8× the parameters for the same cost."

ActuallyThe same matmul FLOPs, not the same cost. You add two all-to-alls per layer per direction, you add memory for E copies of the MLP, you add a load-balancing loss and its tuning, and you get a step time set by the slowest expert group — so any routing imbalance is a direct throughput tax. MoE is a very good trade at large scale and a poor one at small scale, and the crossover is a systems question as much as a modelling one.

Often said"All-to-All moves the same bytes as AllReduce, so it costs about the same."

ActuallyVolume is similar; the pattern is not. Ring AllReduce uses only nearest-neighbour links and can be scheduled to keep every link busy with no contention. All-to-All is all-pairs: on a fat-tree with any oversubscription it drives traffic through the upper tiers simultaneously from every node, and achieved bandwidth can fall far below the nominal per-link number. All-to-All is the pattern that most rewards keeping expert groups inside a node.

Combining parallelisms

Real runs use several of these at once. The mental model that makes this tractable is a device mesh: view your N GPUs not as a list but as a multi-dimensional grid, where each axis is one parallelism strategy and each GPU's coordinate along an axis says which shard of that strategy it holds.

N = DP × TP × PP × CP × EP 512 = 8 × 8 × 8 × 1 × 1 for example rank → coordinate, with TP fastest-varying: tp = r % TP cp = (r / TP) % CP dp = (r / (TP·CP)) % DP pp = r / (TP·CP·DP) The ordering is the whole design. Ranks that are adjacent in the linear ordering are physically adjacent — same node, same NVSwitch. So the axis you place fastest-varying gets the fastest links. TP goes innermost because its collectives are the largest and the least hideable. PP goes outermost because its traffic is a single small tensor per microbatch boundary and it tolerates latency well.

Written out as a preference order, from the wire outward:

  1. TP — innermost, never crosses a node boundary. Four AllReduces per layer on the critical path.
  2. CP — next, often intra-node for Ulysses, sometimes spanning nodes for ring attention where the P2P pattern is friendlier.
  3. EP — where present, wants intra-node all-to-all, and often shares devices with the DP axis.
  4. DP / FSDP — tolerant, because its one collective per step overlaps with the whole backward pass. With HSDP this axis itself splits into an intra-node shard axis and an inter-node replicate axis.
  5. PP — outermost, happily crosses racks. Cheapest communication of all.

The dimensions also interact multiplicatively on the batch. Global batch = b_micro × m × DP, where m is the pipeline microbatch count. Raising PP forces you to raise m to keep the bubble small, which raises the global batch, which may push you past the critical batch size. That coupling — a systems decision changing an optimisation outcome — is the part that never appears in throughput charts.

Often said"3-D parallelism means DP × TP × PP, and the order does not matter."

ActuallyThe order determines which ranks share a node, and that determines which collective rides NVLink versus the fabric. The same 512 GPUs with the same degrees can differ by well over 2× in step time depending purely on rank assignment. This is why frameworks expose the mesh ordering explicitly, and why CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES shuffles and bad SLURM rank-to-node mappings are a recurring source of mysterious slowdowns.

Often said"Use every parallelism dimension — more axes, better scaling."

ActuallyEach dimension you enable adds a failure mode, a constraint on divisibility, and usually some replicated state. The best configurations are the simplest ones that fit. A 7B model on 64 H100s should almost certainly be plain FSDP or HSDP with nothing else turned on; adding TP and PP will make it slower, harder to debug, and no more capable.

  3. Does it fit sharded across one node? If yes: FSDP with shard degree 8, replicate across nodes (HSDP). Stop.
  4. Still too big? Add tensor parallelism up to the node boundary, then pipeline parallelism across nodes. TP first because it is cheap inside a node; PP second because it is cheap outside one.
  5. Activations, not parameters, are the problem? Activation checkpointing first — it is a pure ~30% compute cost for a large memory win, and needs no new communication. Then context parallelism if sequences are genuinely long.
  6. MoE? Expert parallelism, sized so an expert group fits inside a node.
  7. Now optimise. Raise per-device batch until memory is nearly full, because comm/compute ratio improves with it.

What actually goes wrong

The theory above describes a cluster where every device is identical and never fails. Real ones are neither, and at scale the difference dominates.

Stragglers

A synchronous collective completes when its last participant arrives. If one GPU is 5% slow — thermal throttling, a noisy neighbour on the NIC, an ECC error being corrected, a slightly different clock — then the entire cluster is 5% slow, not 5%/N slow. The expected step time is the expectation of a maximum over N samples, and maxima of N samples grow with N even when the mean does not.

T_step = max_i (t_i) over N ranks If each t_i is drawn from a distribution with mean μ and a modest tail, E[max] grows roughly like μ + σ·√(2 ln N). At N=8 the penalty is small. At N=4096 it is substantial even with a tight distribution. This is why large runs invest so heavily in per-rank timing telemetry: finding the one bad card is often worth more than any algorithmic optimisation.

The ways a run hangs rather than crashes

A crash gives you a stack trace. A hang gives you a job that burns cluster hours doing nothing until a watchdog kills it, and the failure modes that produce hangs are all variations on one rule: every rank in a process group must call compatible collectives in the same order. That is not a style guideline, it is the contract. A ring AllReduce cannot complete if one participant never arrives, and NCCL has no way to tell "still computing" from "never coming".

Three ways to break it, all common:

  • Divergent control flow. A conditional that depends on data — skipping a loss term when a batch has no valid targets, an early continue on a malformed sample, logging or evaluating on rank 0 only — makes one rank call a different collective, or none. Every other rank blocks forever.
  • Shape or dtype mismatch. The ranks agree on which collective but not on the buffer. Variable sequence lengths and per-rank padding are the usual culprits, and the symptom is often corruption or a wedge rather than a clean error.
  • The wrong process group. With five parallelism axes there are five distinct groups, and calling a correct collective on the wrong one is a category of bug that only exists in distributed code. It sometimes even completes — producing an answer that is silently wrong rather than a hang.

The defences are unglamorous and worth building early: make control flow structurally identical across ranks (compute the branch condition, then AllReduce it so everyone takes the same path), assert shapes before collectives during bring-up, set a NCCL timeout short enough that a hang fails loudly, and name your process groups so a misuse is visible at the call site.

Often said"The job is hung, so the network must be broken."

ActuallyNetwork faults do cause hangs, but application-level rank divergence causes far more of them. Before blaming the fabric, dump every rank's last collective and compare: if rank 5 is inside a Broadcast while everyone else is inside an AllReduce, that is a control-flow bug, not a NIC. Per-rank collective logging costs almost nothing and answers this question in seconds instead of hours.

Checkpoints have a geometry

A checkpoint is not a file, it is a distributed data structure. Under FSDP each rank owns a slice of every optimizer tensor; under TP the slices are cut along a different axis; under PP the layers themselves live in different processes. Saving that state means choosing between gathering everything into full tensors — slow, memory-hungry, but trivially loadable at any world size — or writing it sharded, which is fast but encodes your current mesh into the files.

The consequence is that resuming on a different number of nodes is a resharding operation, not a load. If you have written sharded checkpoints without enough metadata to redistribute them, you have quietly made your topology permanent. This is discovered at the worst possible moment: after a node failure, when the replacement capacity has a different shape and the run cannot restart. Store the mesh description alongside the tensors and test a reshard before you need one.

Failure is the expected case

If a single node has a mean time between failures of one year, then 512 nodes have an expected failure every 17 hours. A month-long run will be interrupted dozens of times, so checkpointing is not a safety feature but part of the throughput calculation: checkpoint too rarely and you lose hours of work per failure; too often and you spend a meaningful fraction of the run writing tens of terabytes. The optimum interval is roughly √(2 · T_checkpoint · MTBF) — the classic Young–Daly result, which is worth actually computing rather than guessing.

Sharded state complicates this. Under FSDP each rank owns a slice of the optimizer state, so a checkpoint is either gathered to full tensors (slow, but resharding to a different world size is trivial) or written sharded (fast, but tied to the topology unless you store enough metadata to redistribute). Getting this wrong is discovered at the worst possible moment: when you try to resume on a different number of nodes.

Often said"We checkpoint every 1000 steps, which feels safe."

Actually"Feels safe" is not a unit. Compute the interval from your measured checkpoint duration and your observed failure rate. Clusters differ by an order of magnitude in both, and the same interval that is wasteful on a stable cluster is reckless on a flaky one. Also measure the restart cost — process launch, NCCL bootstrap and dataloader warm-up can exceed the checkpoint write itself.

Often said"Activation checkpointing is a free way to save memory."

ActuallyIt costs an extra forward pass over the checkpointed regions, roughly +30% compute if applied everywhere. Selective checkpointing is far better: recompute the cheap, memory-heavy operations (layernorms, activations, dropout) and keep the expensive ones (the matmuls and attention) resident. On a vision-language model the right split is often asymmetric — full checkpointing on the vision tower, which is activation-heavy and FLOP-light, and selective checkpointing on the language model.

Often said"The loss curve looks the same, so the distributed setup is correct."

ActuallyMany bugs are invisible in the loss for hundreds of steps: a wrong data shard giving some ranks duplicate samples, a gradient that is summed instead of averaged (which silently scales your learning rate by N), an unsharded parameter drifting out of sync, a dropped token in an MoE layer that should not have been dropped. Test with the boring things — assert parameter equality across DP ranks after the step, check that the gradient norm matches a single-device run on the same data, and verify that the number of unique samples per epoch is what you think it is.

The whole article in five lines

Memory is a wall and time is a slope. Data parallelism attacks the slope with one cheap collective whose per-rank cost does not grow with the cluster. Sharding attacks the wall by deleting redundancy, at 1.5× the traffic. Tensor, pipeline and context parallelism cut the computation itself along its three natural axes — width, depth and sequence — and each puts its traffic on a different part of the network. Choose the simplest combination that fits, put the chattiest axis on the fastest wire, and then measure, because every number above is a first-order model of a machine that has opinions of its own.
